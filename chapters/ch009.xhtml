Chapter 6: The Inquisitor’s Circle

Reyhan bint Salim had executed forty-three alchemists in ten years, and she remembered every one of them.

Not their faces — faces blurred, merged, became a composite of fear and defiance and the particular expression that people wore when they understood that the next few minutes would be their last. She remembered their circles. The transmutation arrays that she had confiscated before the executions — drawn on paper, carved in wood, tattooed on skin that she had ordered removed and preserved in the Muhtasib’s archive. Forty-three circles. Forty-three variations on the same fundamental geometry. Forty-three attempts to reshape reality by people who believed that the laws of the universe were suggestions rather than commandments.

She kept the archive in a locked room in the Muhtasib’s headquarters in Dar Suwar. She visited it at night, when the other inquisitors were sleeping, when the corridors were empty and the only light was the oil lamp she carried and the only sound was the scratch of her pen as she copied the circles into a journal that no one knew existed.

She was studying them. Had been studying them for ten years. Not to practice alchemy — she was an inquisitor, a hunter, a destroyer of the heretical art. But to understand it. Because you could not hunt what you did not understand, and Reyhan understood alchemy better than most of the alchemists she had killed.

She understood the geometry. The way the lines channeled energy. The way the symbols directed the flow. The way the circles — the containment rings, the directional arrows, the transformation arrays — worked together like the components of a machine, each part performing a specific function, each function contributing to the whole. She understood the mathematics. The equations that governed the Balance. The calculations that determined the cost of a transmutation. The formulas that predicted the rebound when the exchange was unequal.

She understood the beauty. This was the part she could not admit to anyone — not to her officers, not to her superiors, not to the Caliph whose decree she enforced with the mechanical precision of a woman who had learned to separate her duty from her conscience. The circles were beautiful. The geometry was beautiful. The precise, elegant way that the lines and symbols combined to describe a process that was both scientific and artistic — the reshaping of reality through mathematics and will — was beautiful. And beauty, Reyhan had learned, was dangerous. Beauty was the thing that made you hesitate. Beauty was the thing that made you doubt. Beauty was the crack in the wall of certainty through which the light of understanding entered, and understanding was the enemy of obedience.

The circle on her forearm was her secret. Her shame. Her obsession.

She remembered the day she had acquired it. The memory was a wound that would not scar — a cut that healed on the surface and bled beneath, the blood pooling in the spaces between her ribs where no one could see it.

Farid had been brought to the execution square at dawn. The square in Tal Hadid — a small city, a provincial posting, the kind of place where the Muhtasib’s authority was absolute because there was no one important enough to challenge it. The square was paved with sandstone. The buildings around it were mud-brick, two stories, the windows shuttered because the residents knew what happened in the square at dawn and did not want to see it and could not stop themselves from listening.

He had been a healer. That was the part that Reyhan could not reconcile — not then, not now, not in the ten years since. Farid ibn Yusuf. Twenty-eight years old. A healer who had used transmutation circles to mend broken bones, close wounds, cure fevers. He had operated from a small clinic in the lower quarter — the poor quarter, the quarter where the physicians did not go because the patients could not pay. Farid had not charged. Farid had healed because healing was what his circles did and doing what your circles did was, to Farid, as natural and as necessary as breathing.

The Muhtasib had arrested him on a Tuesday. The charge was heresy — the practice of unsanctioned alchemy, the activation of transmutation circles without the Caliph’s license, the reshaping of God’s creation without God’s permission. The charges were technically correct. Farid had no license. Farid had never applied for a license because the licensing process required a bribe that exceeded his annual income and Farid had spent his annual income on reagents and bandages and the small, necessary supplies of a healer who treated the poor for free.

They had tortured him first. This was procedure. The Muhtasib’s protocol required confession before execution — the formal acknowledgment of heresy, the verbal renunciation of the alchemical art, the public declaration that the accused understood the nature of his crime and accepted the justice of his punishment. The protocol did not specify the methods of obtaining the confession. The protocol left the methods to the discretion of the commanding officer.

Reyhan had been the commanding officer.

She had not performed the torture herself. She had ordered it. She had stood in the stone room beneath the Muhtasib’s headquarters and watched as her officers stripped Farid of his clothes and his dignity and the quiet, gentle composure that had been his defining characteristic. They had hung him from the ceiling by his wrists — the ancient method, the method that the Muhtasib had inherited from empires older than the Caliphate, the method that dislocated the shoulders slowly and allowed the prisoner to feel each joint separating, each tendon stretching, each ligament tearing with the precise, unhurried cruelty of a process designed not to kill but to unmake.

Farid had not screamed. That was the thing Reyhan remembered most clearly — more clearly than the blood, more clearly than the sound of his shoulders dislocating, more clearly than the wet, organic noise of a human body being systematically damaged. He had not screamed. He had wept. Silently. The tears running down his face in two steady streams, the weeping of a man who was not crying from pain but from grief — the grief of a healer whose hands had spent their entire life mending and who was now hanging from those hands while they were destroyed.

“Confess,” Reyhan had said. The word was procedure. The word was protocol. “Confess to the practice of unsanctioned alchemy. Renounce the art. Accept the judgment.”

Farid had looked at her. Through the tears. Through the pain. Through the systematic destruction of his body. He had looked at her with eyes that were not angry and not afraid and not defiant but sad. The sadness of a man who understood something about the woman torturing him that the woman did not understand about herself.

“I healed people,” he had said. His voice was steady. The steadiness was extraordinary — the voice of a man whose shoulders were dislocated and whose body was hanging from the ceiling and who was still, somehow, speaking with the calm precision of a person making a clinical observation. “I healed people who had no one else to heal them. Children with fevers. Women with broken bones. Old men with wounds that would not close. I drew circles on their skin and the circles closed the wounds and the fevers broke and the bones mended. That is my crime. That is my heresy. I made people whole.”

“You practiced alchemy without sanction.”

“I practiced mercy without sanction. Is mercy a crime? Does mercy require a license? Does the Caliph own the right to decide who is healed and who is left to suffer?”

Reyhan had not answered. The question was not in the protocol. The protocol did not account for prisoners who asked questions that the interrogator could not answer without dismantling the entire structure of authority that gave the interrogation its legitimacy.

They had brought his patients. This was the part that Reyhan could not think about without the taste of bile rising in her throat — the part that woke her at night, that lived behind her eyes when she closed them, that had driven her to copy Farid’s healing circle onto her own forearm in an act of penance that was also an act of heresy and that she could not stop performing because the performing was the only thing that kept the memory from consuming her entirely.

They had brought his patients to watch. The protocol required witnesses. The protocol specified that the witnesses should include the beneficiaries of the heretical practice — the people who had been healed, the people who had received the fruits of the unsanctioned alchemy, the people whose continued health was itself evidence of the crime. The protocol required them to watch the execution so that they would understand the cost of accepting heretical healing and would not seek it again.

The patients had come. Not willingly — escorted, compelled, dragged from their homes in the lower quarter by Muhtasib officers who did not explain and did not apologize. A woman whose broken arm Farid had mended. A child whose fever Farid had broken. An old man whose wound Farid had closed. They stood in the execution square at dawn and they watched the man who had healed them hanging from the scaffold with his shoulders dislocated and his body broken and his eyes still weeping those silent, steady tears.

The woman had screamed. The child had hidden behind the old man’s legs. The old man had stood very still and very straight and had looked at Reyhan with an expression that she would carry for the rest of her life — the expression of a person who understood exactly what was happening and exactly why it was happening and who had decided, in that moment, that the person responsible was not evil but lost. The old man had looked at Reyhan the way a father looked at a child who had done something terrible and did not yet understand how terrible it was.

Farid had refused to confess. Through the torture. Through the hanging. Through the systematic destruction of his hands — the healer’s hands, the hands that had drawn circles of mercy on the skin of the poor — he had refused. Not from stubbornness. Not from pride. From principle. The principle that healing was not a crime. The principle that mercy did not require permission. The principle that a man who had spent his life mending the broken should not, in his final hours, declare that the mending was wrong.

They had executed him at noon. The method was the sword — quick, clean, the Muhtasib’s concession to efficiency over spectacle. Reyhan had given the order. The blade had fallen. Farid’s head had separated from his body with the sound that Reyhan would hear every night for the rest of her life — not the sound of metal through flesh but the sound of silence. The sudden, absolute silence of a voice that had been speaking mercy into the world and had been stopped.

That night, alone in her quarters, Reyhan had wept. The ugly, broken weeping of a woman who had just killed the best person she had ever met and who understood, with the devastating clarity of hindsight, that the best person she had ever met had been right and she had been wrong and the wrongness was not a mistake but a system — a system she had served and enforced and believed in and that had used her hands to destroy a man whose only crime was kindness.

She had copied his circle that night. From the archive. The healing array that Farid had drawn on the skin of the poor — the geometry of mercy, the mathematics of mending. She had copied it onto her own forearm with ink and a needle and the steady, precise hand of a woman who had been trained to draw circles of destruction and was now, in the dark, in secret, in the privacy of her own shame, drawing a circle of healing. The tattoo was her confession. Her penance. Her heresy. The proof that the woman who had killed Farid understood what Farid had been and mourned what Farid had meant and carried, in the geometry on her skin, the memory of a mercy she had destroyed.

Ten years. The circle had been on her arm for ten years. She had never activated it. She did not know if she could. She did not know if she deserved to.

So the circle stayed dormant. A tattoo on her forearm, hidden beneath her sleeve, burning against her skin with the phantom heat of a transmutation that had never been performed and a question that had never been answered.

The report arrived at dawn.

A rider from the southern garrison, half-dead from dehydration, his horse lathered and trembling. He carried a sealed message — the Muhtasib’s cipher, the code that only Reyhan and her senior officers could read. She broke the seal in her office, alone, the morning light falling through the narrow window and illuminating the cramped script.

Commander. Alchemical activity detected in the ruins of Iram. Multiple practitioners. Transmutation energy signatures consistent with advanced arrays — military grade or higher. At least three individuals. Possibly four. One signature is anomalous — no circle pattern detected. Recommend immediate investigation. The ruins have been dormant for eleven years. Whatever has awakened them is significant.

Reyhan read the message twice. Then she burned it — standard procedure, the cipher was too valuable to risk capture. She watched the paper curl and blacken in the flame of her lamp, and she thought about Iram.

She had been there once. Five years ago. A routine patrol — the Muhtasib maintained a perimeter around the glass city, not to protect it but to prevent alchemists from accessing it. The ruins were the most dangerous site in the Cradle — a repository of advanced alchemical knowledge preserved in glass, readable by anyone with the training to interpret transmutation circles. The Order of the Mizan’s entire body of research, frozen in crystal, waiting for someone to come and study it.

She had walked through the glass streets. She had seen the frozen figures. She had heard the singing — the sustained note, the ten thousand voices, the sound that was not quite sound and not quite silence. And she had felt something that she had never felt before and had spent five years trying to forget.

She had felt the circle on her forearm respond. The tattoo had warmed. The ink had tingled. The healing array, dormant for five years, had reacted to the ambient alchemical energy of the ruins the way a compass needle reacted to a magnetic field — orienting, aligning, wanting to activate.

She had left Iram at a run. She had not returned.

Now someone was there. Multiple practitioners. Advanced arrays. And one anomalous signature — a transmutation without a circle, which was impossible, which meant either the report was wrong or the world was stranger than the Muhtasib’s doctrine allowed.

Reyhan stood. Buckled her sword — a straight blade, unadorned, the weapon of a soldier, not a ceremonial officer. She pulled on her riding coat — black, the color of the Muhtasib, the color of authority and judgment and the particular shade of darkness that people associated with the end of things.

She called for her lieutenant. A young man named Hasan — efficient, loyal, unimaginative. The ideal subordinate for a commander who needed obedience more than initiative.

Hasan had been with her for three years. He had been assigned to her unit fresh from the military academy — a graduate with high marks in tactics and low marks in independent thought. He was twenty-four. He believed in the Muhtasib’s mission with the fervor of a man who had never been forced to question his beliefs. He was, in every way that mattered, the person Reyhan had been ten years ago — before Farid, before the circle, before the slow, corrosive erosion of certainty that had turned the Muhtasib’s most effective commander into its most dangerous liability.

She envied him. She envied the simplicity of his conviction. She envied the clean, uncluttered architecture of a mind that had never been forced to accommodate contradiction — the contradiction of a woman who hunted alchemists and studied alchemy, who destroyed circles and memorized them, who enforced a law she believed was wrong and carried a tattoo that proved it.

“Assemble a squad,” she said. “Twelve riders. Full combat kit. We ride for Iram.”

“The ruins, Commander? That’s a week’s ride.”

“Five days if we push the horses. Four if we change mounts at the southern garrison.”

“May I ask what we’re pursuing?”

“Alchemists. In the ruins. At least three, possibly four.”

Hasan’s face tightened. He was young enough to still believe in the Muhtasib’s mission with the uncomplicated fervor of a man who had never been forced to question his convictions. “Heretics in the glass city. They’re after the Order’s knowledge.”

“They’re after something. Our job is to find out what and stop them.” She paused. “There’s something else. The village we passed through yesterday — Tal Zahra. Three children missing in the last month. The villagers blame Djinn.”

“Djinn don’t take children, Commander.”

“No. They don’t.” She had investigated the homes herself, while her soldiers watered the horses. In the bedroom of the last child taken — a girl, seven years old, an orphan living with her grandmother — Reyhan had found marks on the wall behind the sleeping mat. Not transmutation circles. She knew transmutation circles the way she knew her own handwriting. These were different. Organic. Curved like wounds, like the marks disease left on skin. Drawn in something dark that had soaked into the plaster and could not be scraped away. The marks had made her stomach turn in a way that forty-three executions never had. Alchemy was clean. Geometric. Whatever had been drawn on that wall was neither.

She had copied the marks into her journal. She did not know what they were. But she knew they were not alchemy, and she knew they were not natural, and she knew that three children were missing and the marks were on the walls of their homes and the two facts were connected in a way she could not yet articulate.

“And Hasan. This stays between us. No reports to the Caliph’s court until we know what we’re dealing with. The last thing we need is political interference in a field operation.”

“Understood, Commander.”

He left. Reyhan stood alone in her office. She rolled up her sleeve and looked at the circle on her forearm — Farid’s circle, the healer’s array, the geometry of compassion tattooed on the skin of a woman who had killed the man who designed it.

The circle was warm. Not from the morning sun. Not from the friction of her sleeve. Warm the way it had been warm in Iram — the warmth of alchemical resonance, the warmth of a dormant array responding to something that it could feel and she could not.

Something was happening. Something that the circle recognized and she didn’t. Something that was pulling at the tattoo on her forearm as a current pulled at a swimmer — gently, insistently, in a direction that she could not see and was afraid to follow.

She pulled down her sleeve. Covered the circle. Walked out of her office and into the morning light, where twelve riders were assembling in the courtyard, their horses stamping, their weapons gleaming, their faces set with the grim determination of men who hunted heretics for a living and had never once considered the possibility that the heretics might be right.

She considered it every day. And she did nothing. And the nothing was the heaviest thing she carried.

In Iram, the four of them prepared to leave.

Dawn was an hour away. The glass city was dark — or as dark as a city made of crystal could be, which was not very dark at all. The starlight passed through the transparent walls and floors and ceilings, refracting and multiplying, filling the streets with a pale luminescence that was not moonlight and not starlight but something between — the light of a universe filtered through the lens of the greatest transmutation in history.

Khalil packed his satchel. Journal. Pen. Ink. The dried food that Tariq had produced from a cache hidden in the laboratory — provisions that had been stored in sealed containers before the catastrophe and had survived three centuries in the glass city’s stable, energy-rich environment. The food was old but edible. Alchemy preserved things. That was, in a sense, the entire point.

Zahra packed her notebooks. Dozens of them — three months of research, observations, measurements, the accumulated data of a scholar who had been studying the ruins with the methodical intensity of a woman who believed that understanding was the highest form of alchemy. She packed instruments too — a compass that measured alchemical energy fields, a set of calibrated lenses for examining transmutation circles at magnification, a small scale for measuring the mass of transmuted materials.

Tariq packed nothing. He was his own archive. Three hundred years of knowledge, stored in a mind that was slowly being encased in glass. He carried the formula in his memory — the complete transmutation circle for the Philosopher’s Stone, every line, every symbol, every curve of the alien geometry that the Djinn had given to the Order of the Mizan. He was a walking library. A breathing manuscript. The most valuable and most fragile repository of alchemical knowledge in the world.

Idris packed nothing because he had nothing to pack. He sat on the glass floor of the laboratory, cross-legged, his dark eyes half-closed, his lips moving silently. Talking to the Whisperer. The conversation was inaudible — whatever the primordial Djinn fragment said to its vessel, it said in frequencies that human ears could not detect. But Khalil could see the effects. Idris’s hands moved as he listened — small gestures, unconscious, the fingers tracing shapes in the air that were not quite transmutation circles and not quite random. Patterns. The geometry of a language that was older than human speech, older than human thought, older than the concept of language itself.

“What does it say?” Khalil asked.

Idris opened his eyes. The dark irises were darker than usual — deeper, as if the Whisperer’s presence had added a layer of depth to the boy’s gaze, a dimension that normal eyes did not possess.

“It says the Kahf Thul are dangerous. It says the Order left guardians — transmutation circles that activate when someone enters, designed to test whether the visitor is worthy of the knowledge hidden inside. It says the guardians are old and the circles are degraded and the tests may not work the way they were designed to work, which makes them more dangerous, not less.”

“Helpful.”

“It also says—” Idris paused. The dark eyes flickered — a moment of internal communication, the boy listening to a voice that no one else could hear. “It says the woman is coming. Nasreen. She’s not in Iram — she’s ahead of us. She’s already at the Caves. She’s been following the trail for months. We’re not racing her to the first site. We’re racing her to the second.”

Tariq’s glass body chimed — a sound of alarm, the crystal resonating with the tension in his muscles. “She’s ahead of us. How far ahead?”

“The Whisperer doesn’t measure distance the way we do. It measures — proximity. Closeness to the formula. Nasreen is closer to the second piece than we are to the first.”

“Then we need to move faster,” Khalil said.

“We need to move smarter,” Zahra corrected. “Speed without understanding is just a faster way to fail. We need to know what we’re looking for. The formula — the Stone’s formula — was scattered across multiple sites. Each site contains a piece. But a piece of what? A piece of the circle? A piece of the theory? A piece of the process?”

“All three,” Tariq said. “The Order’s failsafe was comprehensive. Each site contains a section of the transmutation circle, a portion of the theoretical framework, and a component of the process — the specific sequence of steps required to assemble and activate the complete array. You need all three from every site to reconstruct the formula. The circle without the theory is a drawing. The theory without the process is an abstraction. The process without the circle is a recipe with no ingredients.”

“How many sites?” Khalil asked.

“Seven. The Kahf Thul. The Salt Flats of Sahra Bayda. The Underwater Temple of Dilmun. The Mountain Forge of the Jabal Khem. The Library Beneath the Nahr Sirr. The Desert Observatory of Mada’in Salih. And the final site — the one that contains the last piece, the keystone that completes the formula.”

“Where’s the final site?”

“Here. Iram. The glass city itself. The Order hid the last piece in the place where the first attempt was made — in the transmutation circle in the plaza, encoded in the geometry that Khalil has been copying. The circle is not just a record of the catastrophe. It’s a key. The final piece of the formula, hidden in plain sight, readable only by someone who has assembled the other six pieces and understands how they fit together.”

“So we need to visit six sites across the Cradle, collect six pieces of a scattered formula, avoid a three-hundred-year-old immortal and her shape-shifting Djinn ally, and then return here to assemble the complete array. While being hunted by the Muhtasib.”

“Yes.”

“And we have a head start of — how much?”

“Negative. Nasreen is ahead of us.”

Khalil looked at the ceiling. The glass was transparent — he could see through the layers of the palace above them, through the crystal floors and walls and domes, all the way to the stars. The stars looked back, indifferent, the way stars always looked at human problems — with the vast, cold patience of objects that had been burning for billions of years and would continue burning long after the problems were resolved or the humans were extinct, whichever came first.

“Dawn,” he said. “We leave at dawn. The Kahf Thul. We move fast, we move smart, and we don’t stop until we have the first piece.”

“And if Nasreen is already there?” Zahra asked.

“Then we deal with Nasreen.”

“You say that like it’s simple. She’s three hundred years old. She’s survived the catastrophe that killed ten thousand people. She has a Si’lat ally. She can’t die.”

“She can’t die,” Khalil agreed. “But she can be delayed. She can be outmaneuvered. She can be outsmarted. She’s had three centuries to develop her plan, but she’s been working alone — alone with a Djinn, which is a particular kind of alone that I suspect is worse than solitude. We have something she doesn’t.”

“What?”

Khalil looked at his companions. The scholar with ink-stained hands who understood alchemy better than anyone alive. The glass man who carried the formula in his memory and the weight of three centuries of guilt on his crystal shoulders. The boy who was alchemy made flesh, who carried a primordial Djinn in his bones and could reshape matter with a touch.

“Each other,” he said.

It was not a profound statement. It was not a strategic insight. It was the simple, obvious, frequently underestimated truth that four people working together could accomplish things that four people working separately could not. The Balance applied to cooperation as well as transmutation — the whole was greater than the sum of its parts, and the difference between the whole and the sum was the thing that alchemists called synergy and normal people called trust.

They did not trust each other. Not yet. Khalil trusted no one — five years of searching had burned the trust out of him, leaving behind a residue of caution that was harder than fired clay and less flexible. Zahra trusted evidence, not people. Tariq trusted the formula and the guilt and the slow, grinding certainty that he was dying and needed to accomplish something before the glass finished its work. Idris trusted the Whisperer, which was not the same as trusting the people the Whisperer had told him to find.

But they were here. Together. In the ruins of a city that had been destroyed by people who worked alone — who pursued the Stone in isolation, who kept secrets from each other, who believed that the formula was too dangerous to share and too valuable to abandon.

The Order of the Mizan had failed because they had treated the Stone as a secret. Khalil and his companions would succeed — if they succeeded at all — because they would treat it as a conversation. A shared problem. A collaborative transmutation, with each of them contributing a piece that the others lacked.

Zahra was already thinking about the logistics. The scholar’s mind — the mind that processed the world through the lens of evidence and calculation — was mapping the route, estimating the distances, calculating the time required to reach each site and the energy required to perform each test. She had produced a map from her satchel — a detailed chart of the Cradle, annotated with the locations of known alchemical sites, the routes between them, the terrain features that would affect travel time.

“The Kahf Thul are four days northeast,” she said, spreading the map on the glass floor. “Through the desert and into the Jabal Khem foothills. The terrain is rough — mountain passes, narrow valleys, the kind of landscape that slows travel and limits options. If Nasreen is ahead of us, the Caves are the best place to close the gap. The mountain terrain favors people on foot over people who are — whatever Nasreen is. The passes are narrow. The valleys are defensible. And the Caves themselves are deep — the Order built their research station in a network of natural caverns that extends for kilometers into the mountain. If Nasreen is already inside, she’ll be dealing with the guardian circles. The guardians will slow her down. They slow everyone down.”

“You’ve studied the Caves?”

“I’ve studied every known alchemical site in the Cradle. The Kahf Thul are mentioned in seventeen separate texts in the Dar Ilm archive. The descriptions are consistent — a network of caverns, protected by transmutation circles, containing a secondary laboratory where the Order conducted research that was too dangerous for Iram. The texts describe the guardian circles as ‘tests of worthiness’ — challenges designed to evaluate whether a visitor deserves access to the knowledge hidden inside.”

“Tests of worthiness,” Khalil repeated. “What kind of tests?”

“The texts are vague. Deliberately vague. The Order didn’t want anyone to prepare for the tests — preparation would defeat the purpose. The tests are supposed to evaluate the practitioner’s genuine qualities, not their ability to rehearse answers. But the texts do mention one thing consistently: the tests have a cost. Each test demands a payment. Something of value. Something personal. Something that the practitioner cannot replace.”

“The Balance,” Tariq said from his glass chair. “Even the tests obey the law. The knowledge hidden in the sites is valuable. The access to that knowledge must be purchased. And the currency is not gold or labor or the conventional mediums of exchange. The currency is — the practitioner themselves. Their memories. Their abilities. Their attachments. The personal, irreplaceable things that make them who they are.”

“We’ll be paying with pieces of ourselves,” Khalil said.

“We’ll be paying with pieces of ourselves,” Tariq confirmed. “Seven sites. Seven tests. Seven costs. By the time we’ve collected all seven pieces of the formula, we will be — less. Less than we were when we started. The journey will take things from us. Important things. Things we cannot get back.”

“And if we refuse to pay?”

“Then we don’t get the pieces. And Nasreen does. And the world ends.”

The choice was not a choice. The choice was a calculation — the mathematical, Balance calculation of a man who understood that the cost of paying was high and the cost of not paying was higher. The cost of the journey was pieces of themselves. The cost of not journeying was everything else.

Or they would fail. And the failure would be spectacular. And the cost would be everything.

But that was the nature of alchemy. The cost was always everything. The question was never whether you would pay. The question was what you would receive in exchange.

Dawn broke over the glass city. The crystal walls caught the first light and shattered it into a thousand colors — the expanded spectrum, the frequencies that existed only in transmuted glass, the beauty that was the residue of catastrophe.

Four figures walked out of the ruins. Into the desert. Toward the mountains.

The glass city sang behind them — the sustained note, the ten thousand voices, the message that Khalil could almost understand and couldn’t quite translate.

He would learn, later, that the message was a warning.

He would learn too late.
