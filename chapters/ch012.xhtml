Chapter 9: The Sisters

Nasreen’s camp was not a camp. It was a laboratory.

Hidden in a box canyon south of the Jabal Khem foothills, the walls of dark basalt rising on three sides like the walls of a furnace, the fourth side sealed by a transmutation barrier that shimmered in the heat like a curtain of liquid glass. Inside the barrier, the canyon floor had been transmuted — the rough stone smoothed, leveled, inscribed with transmutation circles of such complexity that the geometry covered every surface like scripture on the walls of a temple. The circles were layered — old work beneath new work beneath newer work, three centuries of refinement carved into the stone, each layer a revision, each revision an improvement, each improvement a step closer to the formula that Nasreen had been pursuing since the glass city sang its first note.

Samira al-Fahd had been living in the canyon for five years.

She was twenty-two. Tall. Angular. The kind of beauty that cut rather than warmed — sharp cheekbones, sharp eyes, sharp mind, the geometry of her face as precise and as unforgiving as the transmutation circles she had spent five years learning to draw. Her hair was black, pulled back, severe. Her hands were scarred — not from violence but from practice, the burns and calluses of an alchemist who drew circles with acid on stone and occasionally miscalculated the splash radius.

She had come to Nasreen at seventeen. A prodigy — the word was insufficient but no better word existed. She had been performing transmutations since childhood, teaching herself from fragments of forbidden texts that circulated in the underground markets of Aleppo, drawing circles on the walls of her family’s cellar with charcoal and activating them with a precision that exceeded anything the texts described. She had transmuted copper to silver at fourteen. Iron to steel at fifteen. At sixteen, she had attempted to transmute glass to diamond and the rebound had taken two fingers from her left hand — the ring finger and the small finger, consumed by the failed reaction, the flesh converted to carbon dust that scattered on the cellar floor like black snow.

The missing fingers had not stopped her. The missing fingers had focused her. The cost of the failed transmutation had taught her what no text could teach — that alchemy was not a game, that the law was absolute, that every circle was a contract and every contract had a price and the price was always paid in the currency you could least afford to lose.

She had sought Nasreen the way a river sought the sea — with the patient, inexorable, gravity-driven certainty of something that had no choice but to flow toward the thing that would complete it. She had heard the rumors. The immortal alchemist. The woman who had survived the Iram catastrophe. The woman who knew the formula for the Philosopher’s Stone. Samira had walked for three weeks through the mountains, following coded markers that Nasreen had left for those with the knowledge to read them, and she had arrived at the box canyon with two missing fingers and a mind that burned with the consuming, all-excluding fire of a person who had found their purpose and would not be deterred.

Nasreen had tested her. Three days of tests — circles drawn under pressure, transmutations performed with inadequate materials, theoretical problems solved in real time while the canyon walls shook with the energy of Nasreen’s own practice. Samira had passed every test. Not perfectly — perfection was not what Nasreen sought. Nasreen sought hunger. The relentless, unsatisfiable hunger of a mind that would not stop learning because stopping was not an option and the learning was not a choice but a compulsion.

Samira had the hunger. Samira had the talent. Samira had the missing fingers to prove that she understood the cost.

Nasreen had accepted her.

The first night, Nasreen had sat across from her in the canyon’s deepest alcove, the gold eyes studying Samira the way a jeweler studied an uncut stone—assessing the flaws, calculating the yield.

“You remind me of someone,” Nasreen said.

“Who?”

“A girl who came to me eleven years ago. Brilliant. Hungry. Missing something she couldn’t name.” The gold eyes dimmed. “Her name was Amira.”

“What happened to her?”

Nasreen’s silence lasted three full breaths. When she spoke, the layered voice was stripped to a single harmonic—thin, human, grieving. “She trusted me. That was what happened to her.”

Samira waited for more. More did not come. The gold eyes closed, and the immortal’s bone-and-parchment hands folded in her lap, and the conversation was over in the way that conversations with Nasreen were always over—not concluded but sealed, the subject locked behind a door that would not open again until Nasreen decided it would.

“Will I end up like her?” Samira asked the closed eyes.

“That depends,” Nasreen said without opening them, “on whether you are smart enough to know when to stop.”

“Was she?”

“No. She was brave. Bravery and intelligence are not the same thing. Bravery will walk you into a fire. Intelligence will tell you the fire is hot. The question is which one you listen to when they disagree.”

Samira had listened to intelligence. For five years, she had listened to intelligence. The listening had made her the most talented alchemist of her generation. The listening had also made her the kind of person who could gather children for slaughter and feel nothing, because feeling was brave and feeling was dangerous and intelligence said: do not feel. Feeling will walk you into the fire.

Five years. Five years of study under the most dangerous alchemist in the world. Five years of learning the forbidden circles, the human transmutation theory, the terrifying, essential knowledge that the academies had suppressed and the Muhtasib had burned and Nasreen had preserved in the stone walls of a box canyon in the mountains.

Samira had learned. Samira had excelled. Samira had become, in Nasreen’s assessment, the most talented alchemist of her generation — a mind that combined theoretical brilliance with practical skill, a practitioner who could draw circles with the speed of a soldier and the precision of a scholar and the intuition of a natural transmuter who understood the art not as a system of rules but as a language, a conversation with the universe conducted in the grammar of geometry.

Samira had also learned something that Nasreen had not intended to teach. She had learned the formula. Not the complete formula — Nasreen guarded the pieces she had collected with the paranoid security of a woman who had been betrayed before and would not be betrayed again. But the theory. The principle. The understanding that the Stone required the blood of children.

Samira believed it. The way Nasreen believed it. With the absolute, unquestioning, foundation-deep certainty of a person who had been taught by the greatest authority in the world and had no reason to doubt the teaching.

The belief was the foundation of everything Samira was. The belief justified the five years of isolation. The belief justified the missing fingers. The belief justified the cold, methodical preparation that Samira had been conducting for the past two years — the gathering. The quiet, patient, invisible work of identifying orphans and street children in the cities of the Cradle, the ones nobody counted, the ones whose absence would not be noticed, the ones whose futures could be consumed without consequence because the world had already decided that their futures did not matter.

Samira had gathered seventeen children. They were housed in a cave system adjacent to the box canyon — fed, clothed, cared for with the clinical, emotionless efficiency of a woman who understood that the children were not people but materials. Reagents. The raw substance that the formula required. Samira did not hate the children. Samira did not love the children. Samira regarded the children the way she regarded any alchemical ingredient — with the professional assessment of a practitioner evaluating the quality of her materials.

The assessment was the horror. The assessment was the thing that made Samira dangerous — not the cruelty, because Samira was not cruel, but the absence. The cultivated, deliberately maintained absence of the emotional response that would have made the gathering impossible. Samira had transmuted her own compassion the way she transmuted copper to silver — methodically, precisely, converting the soft metal of human feeling into the hard currency of purpose.

The cost of that transmutation was invisible. The cost was Samira herself — the woman she might have been, the warmth she might have felt, the connections she might have made. The cost was paid in the currency of humanity, and the payment was so gradual, so incremental, so perfectly calibrated to the tolerance of a mind that was too brilliant to notice its own erosion, that Samira did not know she had paid it.

She did not know she was empty.

She did not know until Daliya arrived.

Daliya al-Fahd was nineteen. She was everything Samira was not.

Where Samira was angular, Daliya was soft. Where Samira was precise, Daliya was chaotic. Where Samira’s beauty cut, Daliya’s beauty warmed — round face, wide eyes, a smile that appeared without permission and lingered without apology, the irrepressible, fundamentally unkillable warmth of a person who had been born sunny and had remained sunny despite every effort of the world to cloud her.

She had followed Samira. Not immediately — three years after Samira’s departure, three years of waiting in Aleppo, three years of watching the door and expecting her sister to walk through it and gradually, painfully, accepting that the door would remain closed. Daliya had been fourteen when Samira left. She had been seventeen when she decided to follow. She had been seventeen and three weeks when she arrived at the box canyon, sunburned and dehydrated and grinning with the radiant, absolutely inappropriate joy of a person who had accomplished something difficult and was too pleased with herself to notice that the accomplishment had nearly killed her.

“Samira,” she had said. Standing at the transmutation barrier, her pack on her back, her hair wild, her smile wide. “I found you.”

Samira had stared at her sister through the shimmering barrier with an expression that Daliya could not read and that Samira could not name. The expression contained, compressed within its sharp geometry, everything that Samira had spent five years transmuting out of herself — the love, the warmth, the connection, the human, untransmutable bond between sisters that no amount of alchemical training could eliminate because the bond was not alchemical. The bond was biological. The bond was written in the shared blood and the shared childhood and the shared memory of a house in Aleppo where two girls had drawn circles on the cellar walls and dreamed of changing the world.

“Go home,” Samira had said.

“No.”

“This is not a place for you. This is dangerous. The work here is—”

“I’m not going home. I walked three weeks to get here. I’m staying.”

Daliya had stayed. Nasreen had permitted it — not out of kindness but out of the same calculating pragmatism that governed all of Nasreen’s decisions. Another pair of hands. Another mind. Another potential tool. Nasreen collected people the way she collected formula pieces — with the patient, methodical, long-term assessment of a woman who understood that every resource had a use and every use had a time.

Daliya had no talent for alchemy. This was the truth that Samira had known since childhood and that Daliya had spent her entire life refusing to accept. Daliya could not draw circles with precision. Daliya could not calculate energy pathways. Daliya could not perform the demanding, unforgiving work of transmutation that required a mind like Samira’s — a mind that was equal parts mathematics and intuition, equal parts discipline and instinct.

But Daliya had something else. Daliya had the thing that Samira had transmuted out of herself. Daliya had warmth. The human, untransmutable capacity for connection that made the children in the cave system trust her, that made the guards relax around her, that made even Nasreen — the rotting queen, the three-hundred-year-old monster — soften in her presence, the gold eyes losing their analytical intensity and becoming, for moments, almost human.

Daliya cared for the children. She fed them. She told them stories. She braided the girls’ hair and taught the boys games and sang songs that she remembered from Aleppo, from the house with the cellar, from the childhood that she and Samira had shared before alchemy consumed one of them and the other followed.

One evening, the smallest boy—a child of four named Rami who had arrived with bruises on his arms and a silence that was louder than screaming—climbed into Daliya’s lap and pressed his face against her shoulder.

“Are you our mother now?” he asked.

Daliya’s throat closed. She held him tighter. “I’m not your mother, habib. But I’m here. I’m not going anywhere.”

“Promise?”

“Promise.”

An older girl—Yasmin, twelve, sharp-eyed, the self-appointed guardian of the younger children—watched from across the cave. “Don’t promise things you can’t keep,” she said. Not cruel. Tired. The tiredness of a child who had been promised things before and had learned that promises were the currency adults spent most freely and honored least often.

“I can keep this one,” Daliya said.

“That’s what the last person said. Before they left us at the market in Basra.” Yasmin’s voice was flat. “Before the woman with the gold eyes found us.”

Daliya looked at Yasmin. Looked at the sharp eyes that were too old for twelve. Looked at the arms crossed over the thin chest, the posture of a child who had built walls because the world had taught her that walls were necessary.

“I’m not leaving,” Daliya said. Quietly. With the weight of a woman who meant it. “Not tomorrow. Not next week. Not when it gets hard. I’m staying.”

Yasmin stared at her for a long time. Then the sharp eyes softened—not much, not dramatically, but enough. The arms uncrossed. The wall lowered one brick.

“The little ones need new stories,” Yasmin said. “They’ve heard the prince one four times.”

Daliya laughed. The laugh was real—the warm, unguarded sound of a woman who had been given permission to stay by a twelve-year-old gatekeeper and found the permission more meaningful than any adult’s approval.

“I’ll think of new ones,” she said.

“Make them about girls,” Yasmin said. “The boys get enough stories.”

She did not know why the children were there. Samira had told her they were orphans that Nasreen had rescued. Daliya had believed it because believing was easier than questioning and because the alternative — that her sister was gathering children for slaughter — was a thought so monstrous that Daliya’s sunny mind could not hold it the way a hand could not hold fire.

Two years. Two years of Daliya caring for the children and Samira preparing them and neither sister speaking the truth that lay between them like a transmutation circle drawn on the floor of their relationship — the geometry of a secret that, when activated, would destroy everything it contained.

The Muhtasib column passed the canyon on a Tuesday.

Daliya saw them from the ridge — twelve riders in black coats, moving south along the foothills, the dust of their passage rising in a plume that was visible for leagues. She watched them with the idle curiosity of a person who had been living in isolation for two years and found any evidence of the outside world fascinating.

She saw Hasan.

Not clearly — the distance was too great for features, too great for the details that would later become the architecture of her obsession. She saw a figure. A young man on a horse, riding at the rear of the column, his posture straight, his movements easy, the unconscious grace of a person who was comfortable in his body and comfortable on his horse and comfortable in the world in a way that Daliya, who had never been comfortable anywhere except in her sister’s presence, found immediately and irrationally compelling.

She watched until the column disappeared. Then she went back to the children and told them a story about a prince on a horse who rode through the desert looking for something he could not name, and the children listened, and the story was not about Hasan because Daliya did not know Hasan’s name, but the story was about the feeling that seeing him had produced — the sudden, inexplicable warmth of a heart recognizing something in a stranger that it had been waiting to recognize.

Samira saw her sister watching the column. Samira saw the expression on Daliya’s face — the softness, the warmth, the quality of attention that a woman directed at a man who had caught her interest. Samira saw it and felt something that she had not felt in five years.

Jealousy.

Not of Hasan — Samira had never seen the young lieutenant, had no interest in soldiers, had no capacity for the kind of romantic attachment that Daliya wore on her face like a lantern in a dark room. The jealousy was deeper. Older. The jealousy was of Daliya’s capacity. The capacity to feel. The capacity to want. The capacity to look at a stranger on a horse and feel warmth, to look at children in a cave and feel compassion, to look at the world and feel anything other than the cold, precise, methodical assessment that Samira had cultivated as her primary mode of existence.

Samira had transmuted her own warmth into purpose. The transmutation had been successful. The transmutation had been necessary. The transmutation had made her the most talented alchemist of her generation and the most effective gatherer of children that Nasreen had ever employed.

But the transmutation had a cost. And the cost was standing on a ridge, watching a column of soldiers disappear into the desert, her face soft with a feeling that Samira could no longer produce.

The jealousy was not rational. The jealousy was not productive.

She said nothing. She turned away. She went back to the circles on the canyon walls and drew geometry with acid on stone and did not think about the expression on her sister’s face and did not think about the warmth she could no longer feel and did not think about the children in the cave and did not think about the cost.

The not-thinking was its own kind of transmutation. The not-thinking was the circle that Samira drew around herself every day — the containment geometry that held the emptiness inside and prevented it from leaking out and contaminating the purpose that was the only thing she had left.

The circle held.

For now.

Before dawn, Daliya stirred. Samira was still awake—had been awake all night, the sharp eyes fixed on the stone ceiling, the mind running calculations that had nothing to do with alchemy.

“You’re not sleeping,” Daliya whispered.

“I don’t sleep much.”

“You used to. In Aleppo. You used to sleep like the dead. Mother would have to pour water on your face.”

The memory hit Samira like a rebound—sudden, precise, devastating. Their mother’s laugh. The cold water. Samira sputtering awake, furious, Daliya giggling from the doorway. A lifetime ago. A different person ago.

“Do you miss it?” Daliya asked. “Home?”

“There is no home. There’s the work.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

Samira was quiet for a long time. In the darkness, with the children breathing around them, with the transmutation barrier humming at the canyon’s mouth, the quiet was almost comfortable. Almost like the cellar in Aleppo where two girls had drawn circles on the walls and dreamed.

“Yes,” Samira said. The word was so small it barely existed. “I miss it.”

Daliya reached across the dark and found her sister’s hand. The scarred hand. The hand with the missing fingers. She held it the way she had held it when they were children—without judgment, without condition, without the need to understand what the hand had done or what the hand would do. She held it because holding was what sisters did.

Samira did not pull away. Samira held on. In the dark, in the canyon, in the space between the woman she had been and the weapon she had become, Samira held her sister’s hand and felt something that she had not felt in five years.

Not warmth. Not love. Not the full, rich, complicated emotion that Daliya produced as naturally as breathing.

An echo. A residue. The alchemical trace of a feeling that had been transmuted but not destroyed—because nothing was ever truly destroyed, because the law said that matter could not be created or destroyed, only transformed, and the warmth that Samira had transmuted into purpose still existed somewhere in the architecture of her soul, faint and distant and unreachable but present.

She held Daliya’s hand until dawn. She did not sleep. But the not-sleeping was different from the usual not-sleeping. The usual not-sleeping was calculation. This not-sleeping was something else.

This not-sleeping was the closest thing to peace that Samira al-Fahd had felt in five years.

That night, in Nasreen’s camp, Samira could not sleep.

The canyon was quiet. The transmutation barrier hummed at the entrance — the low, constant vibration of active alchemy, the sound that had been Samira’s lullaby for five years. The other practitioners slept in their alcoves — the carved niches in the canyon wall that served as quarters, each one containing a bedroll, a lamp, and the personal effects of a person who had given up their previous life to serve Nasreen’s vision.

Samira lay on her bedroll and stared at the stone ceiling and thought about Amira.

Amira had been Nasreen’s previous apprentice. Before Samira. Before the canyon. Before the five years of training and refinement and the slow, patient transformation of a prodigy into a weapon. Amira had been brilliant — more brilliant than Samira, if Samira was honest, and Samira was always honest because dishonesty required a kind of emotional flexibility that she did not possess. Amira had understood the formula at a level that exceeded Samira’s comprehension. Amira had drawn circles that made Samira’s best work look like a child’s scribbling.

Amira was dead.

Nasreen had killed her. Not with violence — with alchemy. The experiment had been Nasreen’s design — a test of the formula’s fourth piece, the transformation stage, the step that required the practitioner to undergo a fundamental change. Amira had volunteered. Amira had always volunteered. The eagerness of a young woman who wanted to prove herself to the immortal who had taken her in and trained her and given her purpose.

The transformation had failed. The rebound had consumed Amira from the inside — the alchemical energy turning inward, the transmutation reversing, the change that was supposed to make her more making her less instead. Samira had watched. She had stood in the canyon and watched Amira dissolve — not into glass, not into crystal, but into nothing. The flesh becoming transparent, then translucent, then absent. The body disappearing like when you erased it — the meaning gone, the space empty, the page blank where a person had been.

Nasreen had wept. This was the thing that Samira could not reconcile — the weeping. The immortal, the three-hundred-year-old woman with gold eyes and a rotting body and a plan to save the world, had wept for Amira. The tears had been real. The grief had been real. The devastation of a woman who had lost someone she cared about and who understood, with the terrible clarity of three centuries of experience, that the loss was her fault.

“I loved her,” Nasreen had said. Afterward. In the dark. The gold eyes dim with grief, the rotting body hunched in the chair that she occupied when the pain was too great to stand. “I loved her the way I loved the Order. The way I loved Iram. The way I love the formula. With everything. And the everything was not enough. The everything is never enough. The cost is always more than the everything.”

Samira had not known what to say. She had stood in the dark and watched the immortal weep and had felt something shift inside her — not sympathy, not compassion, but understanding. The understanding that Nasreen was not a monster. Nasreen was a woman who had been alive for three hundred years and who had lost everyone she had ever loved and who continued, despite the losses, to pursue a goal that she believed would save the world. The losses were not incidental. The losses were the cost. And Nasreen paid the cost because the alternative — stopping, giving up, letting the barrier fall without the Stone to manage the transition — was worse.

But the understanding had a shadow. The shadow was this: if Nasreen had loved Amira and had still used Amira in an experiment that killed her, then Nasreen’s love was not a protection. Nasreen’s love was a selection criterion. Nasreen loved the people she was willing to sacrifice. The love and the sacrifice were not contradictions. They were the same thing.

Samira was Nasreen’s new apprentice. Samira was the replacement. Samira was the next person that Nasreen would love and use and lose.

She turned on her bedroll. Daliya was sleeping beside her — the younger sister, the warm one, the impulsive one who had followed Samira to Nasreen’s camp because following Samira was what Daliya did and had always done and would always do until the following led her somewhere that Samira could not protect her.

Daliya was nineteen. Beautiful in the way that Samira was not — soft where Samira was sharp, warm where Samira was cold, open where Samira was closed. Men looked at Daliya. Men had always looked at Daliya. The looking was a constant — the background radiation of Daliya’s existence, the attention that followed her like sunlight followed a flower. Samira had spent her life standing beside a sister who attracted light and had learned to live in the shadow and had told herself that the shadow was preferable because the shadow was where the work happened and the work was what mattered.

But the shadow had a cost. The cost was this: Samira had never been looked at the way men looked at Daliya. Samira had never been wanted for anything except her mind. Samira’s value was her brilliance, her precision, her ability to draw circles that exceeded anything her contemporaries could achieve. And brilliance, Samira had learned, was a cold thing to be valued for. Brilliance did not keep you warm at night. Brilliance did not look at you across a room and smile. Brilliance did not reach for your hand in the dark.

She watched Daliya sleep. The younger sister’s face was peaceful — the face of a person who had never questioned whether she was loved, who had never wondered whether her value was conditional, who had never lain awake in the dark calculating the probability that the person who claimed to care about her would, when the cost came due, choose the mission over the person.

Samira envied her. The envy was small. Manageable. The kind of envy that a person could carry without it poisoning them — the wistful, resigned envy of a woman who understood that some things were not distributed equally and that the distribution was not a judgment but a fact.

But envy, like alchemy, had a rebound. And the rebound, like all rebounds, would come due at the worst possible moment.

In the deepest alcove of the canyon, a man named Tariq al-Wasiti — not the glass man, a different Tariq, a common name in the Cradle — was copying Nasreen’s notes.

He had been Nasreen’s servant for three years. A quiet man. Unremarkable. The kind of person who existed in the background of other people’s stories — the servant who brought the tea, the assistant who cleaned the equipment, the functionary who maintained the camp’s supplies and was so thoroughly invisible that no one thought to watch him.

Tariq al-Wasiti was a Bloodsworn agent.

Rashid al-Fahim had placed him in Nasreen’s camp eighteen months ago. The placement had been delicate — the work of a spymaster who understood that the best agents were the ones who were never suspected because they were never noticed. Tariq al-Wasiti was not noticed. Tariq al-Wasiti was furniture. Tariq al-Wasiti was the man who refilled the oil lamps and swept the canyon floor and who, every third night, copied Nasreen’s latest research into a journal that he kept hidden in the lining of his bedroll.

The journal would reach Rashid through the Bloodsworn’s courier network. The information would be analyzed. The sabotage would be adjusted. The degraded reagent would be calibrated to account for Nasreen’s latest refinements, ensuring that the Bloodsworn’s resurrection ritual remained precisely miscalculated — close enough to attempt, far enough from success to guarantee catastrophic failure.

Tariq al-Wasiti did not know this. Tariq al-Wasiti believed he was serving the Bloodsworn’s mission — the resurrection of Vaelith Khael, the sealing of the barrier, the salvation of the world. He copied Nasreen’s notes with the diligence of a true believer and the invisibility of a man who had spent his entire life being overlooked and had turned the overlooking into a weapon.

But Tariq al-Wasiti was not merely invisible. Tariq al-Wasiti was charming.

The charm was not the obvious kind — not the loud, performative warmth of a man who wanted to be liked. The charm was quiet. Subtle.

He brought her tea. Every evening. Without being asked. The tea was prepared like it — sweet, with mint, the leaves steeped longer than most people preferred because Daliya liked the bitterness beneath the sweetness, the complexity that most palates rejected. He had noticed this. He had remembered. He had adjusted. The adjustment was the weapon. The adjustment was the seduction — not of the body but of the attention, the slow, patient, methodical process of making a person feel seen.

Daliya was lonely. The loneliness was the vulnerability. Two years in a canyon with seventeen children and a sister who had transmuted her warmth into purpose — two years of being the only warm person in a cold place, the only person who told stories and braided hair and remembered names. Two years of giving warmth and receiving nothing in return except the children’s need, which was not warmth but demand, the constant, exhausting, never-satisfied demand of seventeen small lives that required feeding and comforting and protecting and the protecting was endless and the comforting was endless and the feeding was endless and the loneliness was the space between the demands where Daliya existed as a person rather than a function.

Tariq al-Wasiti filled the space. Not with words — with presence. He sat beside her at the canyon’s edge. He listened to her stories — the stories she told the children, the stories she told herself, the stories that were not about princes on horses but about a woman who wanted to be seen by someone who was not a child and not a sister and not a three-hundred-year-old immortal but a person. A man. A warm, quiet, attentive man who brought her tea and remembered how she liked it and sat beside her at dusk and said nothing because the nothing was what she needed.

“You’re kind,” Daliya told him one evening. The words were simple. The words were the most dangerous words a lonely woman could say to a man who was using kindness as a weapon. “You’re the only kind person here. Besides the children. But the children are kind because they don’t know how to be anything else. You’re kind because you choose to be. That’s different.”

Tariq al-Wasiti smiled. The smile was warm. The smile was practiced — not in the mirror, not with the conscious, deliberate rehearsal of a man perfecting a performance, but in the field. In the years of service to the Bloodsworn. In the dozens of camps and households and institutions where he had been placed and where he had used the same quiet charm to extract information from people who did not know they were being extracted from. The smile was a tool. The smile was a key. The smile opened doors that locks could not.

“You deserve kindness,” he said. The words were simple. The words were the second most dangerous words a charming man could say to a lonely woman. “You give so much to the children. To your sister. To everyone. Someone should give something to you.”

The giving began. Small things. A blanket when the nights were cold. A carved wooden bird that he said he had found in the canyon but had actually made — the carving precise, detailed, the work of a man who had spent hours shaping wood into a gift that would seem casual and was anything but. A flower — a desert bloom, tough and small and beautiful, placed on the rock beside her sleeping mat where she would find it in the morning.

And the questions. Always the questions. Woven into the kindness like poison woven into honey — invisible, tasteless, detectable only by someone who knew to look for it.

“How many children does Nasreen expect?” asked between sips of tea.

“When does your sister meet with Nasreen?” asked while placing the blanket around her shoulders.

“Has Nasreen mentioned the formula? The pieces she’s collected?” asked while sitting beside her at the canyon’s edge, the dusk light making his face warm and trustworthy and the question seeming natural, conversational, the curiosity of a servant who was interested in his employer’s work.

Daliya answered. She answered because the questions were reasonable and the questioner was kind and the kindness had earned the right to curiosity. She answered because she was lonely and the answering was a form of connection and the connection was what she needed. She answered because she did not know — could not know, had no framework for knowing — that the kind man with the tea and the blanket and the carved bird was a spy, and the kindness was a strategy, and the strategy was the oldest and most effective intelligence-gathering technique in the history of espionage: make the target feel valued, and the target will give you everything.

She told him about the children. About the gathering. About Samira’s schedule and Nasreen’s visits and the formula pieces that Samira discussed in the evenings when she thought Daliya was asleep. She told him things that Samira would have killed her for sharing — not because the things were secret but because the sharing was trust, and trust given to the wrong person was the most dangerous transmutation in the world.

Tariq al-Wasiti wrote it all down. Every third night. In the journal hidden in his bedroll. The information flowed from Daliya’s loneliness through Tariq’s charm through the Bloodsworn’s courier network to Rashid al-Fahim’s basement chamber in Dar Ilm. The seduction was complete. Not of the body — Tariq al-Wasiti never touched Daliya, never kissed her, never crossed the physical boundary that would have made the seduction visible and therefore resistible. The seduction was of the soul. The slow, patient, devastating corruption of a lonely woman’s trust, performed with tea and blankets and carved birds and the quiet, lethal kindness of a man who understood that the most effective weapon in the world was not a blade or a circle but attention.

Daliya would never know. She would never learn that the kind man in the canyon was a Bloodsworn agent. She would never learn that her loneliness had been weaponized. She would never learn that the carved bird on her sleeping mat was not a gift but a receipt — the physical evidence of a transaction in which she had given everything and received nothing and the nothing had felt like kindness.

The corruption was complete. The seduction was invisible. And the information — the schedule, the formula pieces, the children’s locations — flowed south through the Bloodsworn’s network like water through a crack in a dam, silent and steady and unstoppable.

Nasreen did not suspect him. Nasreen, who had survived three centuries of betrayal and conspiracy and the particular paranoia of a woman who had outlived everyone she had ever trusted, did not suspect the quiet servant who refilled her oil lamps. Because Nasreen’s paranoia was calibrated for threats that looked like threats — for enemies who carried weapons, for rivals who drew circles, for the obvious, visible, dramatic forms of betrayal that three centuries of experience had taught her to recognize.

She did not recognize the quiet kind. The kind that came from the people you did not see. The kind that lived in the background, in the margins, in the spaces between the important people and the important events. The kind that was, in its own way, the most devastating betrayal of all — because it was performed by someone who had been trusted not because they had earned trust but because they had been too insignificant to distrust.

Tariq al-Wasiti finished copying. Closed the journal. Hid it in his bedroll. Lay down. Closed his eyes.

In the morning, he would refill the oil lamps. He would sweep the canyon floor. He would be invisible.

And the information would flow. From the canyon to the courier to Rashid to the Bloodsworn. The quiet betrayal. The invisible knife. The servant’s revenge on a world that had never bothered to see him.

On the road south, the column passed a group of pilgrims.

Thirty people. Men, women, children. Dressed in white — the color of purity, the color of mourning, the color of a people who believed they were walking toward something sacred and were willing to suffer for the walking. They carried no weapons. They carried no provisions beyond what they could carry on their backs. They carried, instead, a text — a scroll, copied and recopied, the words worn smooth by generations of handling, the ink faded but the message preserved with the fanatical, unquestioning devotion of people who believed that the words were not words but truth.

The Scroll of the Glass Oracle.

Omar watched them from his mule. He had seen pilgrims before — the Cradle was full of them, the landscape dotted with shrines and holy sites and the persistent, unkillable human need to walk toward something that promised meaning. These pilgrims were different. These pilgrims had the look of people who were not walking toward meaning but fleeing from its absence — the desperate, hollow-eyed intensity of people who had lost everything and had found, in the scroll and the walking and the white robes and the promise of the Glass Oracle, a replacement for the everything that was gone.

Their leader was a man named Jibril. Fifty years old. Gaunt. The gauntness of a man who fasted not for health but for penance — the self-inflicted deprivation of a person who believed that suffering was the price of salvation and was determined to pay in advance. His eyes were bright — too bright, the brightness of fever or faith or the dangerous, indistinguishable combination of the two.

“The Glass Oracle speaks,” Jibril said. He said it to anyone who would listen. He said it to the soldiers who rode past. He said it to the desert. He said it to the sky. “The Glass Oracle speaks. In the city of crystal, the Oracle waits. When the barrier falls, the Oracle will rise. The glass will become flesh. The dead will speak. The truth will be revealed.”

The scroll said more. The scroll described a prophecy — the fall of the barrier between worlds, the awakening of the glass city, the emergence of an oracle from the crystal who would speak the truth that the world needed to hear. The prophecy was detailed. The prophecy was specific. The prophecy was, in every particular that could be verified, wrong.

The prophecy had been written by Nasreen. Three centuries ago. A text designed to draw believers to Iram — to create a steady stream of pilgrims who would bring their children to the glass city, who would camp in the contaminated zone, who would be available when the time came for the gathering. The prophecy was a net. The believers were the fish. And the fish had been swimming toward the net for three hundred years, generation after generation, the scroll copied and recopied, the faith transmitted from parent to child, the walking continuing long after the original purpose of the text had been forgotten by everyone except the woman who had written it.

Jibril’s daughter was seven years old. Her name was Noor. She walked beside her father, her small hand in his large one, her white robe trailing in the dust, her dark eyes wide with the uncomplicated wonder of a child who believed that her father knew where they were going and that the going was good.

Noor had been born on the road. Her mother had died in childbirth — the specific, common, devastating death that claimed women in the desert where physicians were scarce and the distance between settlements was measured in days. Jibril had raised her alone. Jibril had raised her on the scroll and the walking and the promise of the Glass Oracle, and Noor believed in the oracle the way she believed in her father — completely, unconditionally, with the absolute trust of a child who had never been given a reason to doubt.

The pilgrims walked south. The column rode south. Their paths converged and diverged and converged again, the two groups moving through the same landscape toward the same destination for different reasons — the soldiers hunting alchemists, the pilgrims seeking an oracle, the merchant supplying both, the desert holding all of them in its vast, indifferent, sun-hammered embrace.

Omar watched the pilgrims. He watched Jibril’s bright eyes and Noor’s small hand and the white robes trailing in the dust. He watched and he thought about faith and trust and the devastating similarity between a man who trusted his wife despite the evidence and a man who trusted a prophecy despite the evidence and the universal, fundamentally human need to believe in something — anything — that made the walking bearable.

The desert stretched. The sun burned. The column and the pilgrims moved south, toward Iram, toward the glass city, toward the place where all the threads of fate were converging with the patient, inexorable, mathematically precise inevitability of a transmutation circle activating — each line connecting to the next, each symbol building on the previous, the geometry of destiny assembling itself from the choices of people who did not know they were being drawn and could not have stopped the drawing if they had.

The thread connected them all — Jibril’s walking to Noor’s presence to Nasreen’s gathering to Khalil’s search. The Djinn watched from the spaces between and did not intervene. They never did. The humans were doing it to themselves.

Free will was the trap.

The Djinn smiled. In the spaces between. In the frequencies between sound and silence. The ancient, patient, amoral smile of beings who had been watching humans choose their own destruction since the beginning of time and had never grown tired of the spectacle.

The walking continued. The thread tightened. The fates converged.

And the desert, vast and indifferent and older than any of them, held its breath and waited for the moment when the convergence would be complete and the cost would come due and the exchange — the vast, terrible, mathematically precise exchange that had been building for three hundred years — would finally be balanced.
