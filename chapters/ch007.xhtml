Chapter 4: The Thief Who Didn’t Need Circles

The boy came out of nowhere.

Or so it seemed. In truth, a merchant’s cart had overturned at the intersection of the glass market’s eastern entrance twenty minutes earlier — a wheel cracking on a seam in the crystal pavement, spilling amphorae of olive oil across the street, blocking the only other route out of the district. The merchant would later swear the wheel had been sound that morning. It had been. But a thread had been pulled — gently, precisely, by fingers that were not in the market and never would be — and the wheel had found a flaw in the glass that had not been there yesterday, and the cart had tipped, and a boy who would otherwise have left the district by the eastern gate was funneled west, toward a man with a clay arm.

Coincidence. Except it wasn’t.

One moment Khalil was walking through the glass market district — the frozen stalls with their crystallized wares, the transparent merchants locked in eternal commerce — and the next moment his satchel was lighter by the weight of his journal, and a figure was sprinting down the glass street with the fluid, boneless speed of someone who had been running from things since before he could walk.

“Hey—”

The figure didn’t stop. Didn’t look back. Bare feet slapping against the glass surface with a sound like rapid applause, weaving between the frozen figures with an intimacy that suggested he knew the layout of the dead city the way other people knew the layout of their own homes. Left past the spice merchant. Right through the gap between the two running boys. Under the outstretched arm of the woman reaching for her child — a gap so narrow that Khalil, following at a dead sprint, had to turn sideways to fit through.

The thief was small. Young. A boy — fifteen, sixteen at most, with the wiry build of someone who ate when he could and ran when he couldn’t. Dark skin, darker than Khalil’s, the deep brown of the river cities to the south. Hair that hadn’t been cut in months, maybe years, a wild tangle that bounced as he ran. Clothes that were more patch than fabric — a tunic that had been repaired so many times the original material was a matter of archaeological speculation.

He was fast. Khalil was faster — military training, five years of hard travel, the desperate fitness of a man who could not afford to be slow. He closed the distance in thirty strides, reaching with his clay hand, the ceramic fingers extending toward the boy’s collar—

The boy touched a wall.

Not leaned against. Not braced himself on. Touched. His palm pressed flat against the glass surface of a building as he rounded a corner, and the glass changed. Under his hand, in the space of a heartbeat, the transparent crystal became opaque — a wall of milky white stone that hadn’t existed a second ago, blocking the alley, cutting off Khalil’s pursuit as cleanly as a door slamming shut.

Khalil hit the wall. His left hand struck the transmuted surface and the impact rang through the glass street like a bell. The wall was solid. Real. Stone where glass had been, matter fundamentally restructured at the molecular level.

A transmutation. Performed without a circle. Without preparation. Without the geometric array that every alchemist since Sahir Kem Hayyan had required to channel the energy of transformation. The boy had touched the wall and willed it to change, and the wall had obeyed.

That was impossible.

Khalil stood in the blocked alley, the transmuted hand pressed against the transmuted stone, his mind racing through the theoretical framework that he had spent fourteen years learning and five years applying. Transmutation required circles. This was not a suggestion. This was not a guideline. This was the fundamental mechanism by which alchemical energy was channeled from the practitioner’s intent into the physical structure of matter. Without a circle, the energy had no pathway. Without a pathway, the energy dispersed. Without focus, the transmutation rebounded — instantly, violently, fatally.

Every alchemist knew this. Every alchemist had been taught this. Every alchemist who had ever tried to transmute without a circle was dead.

The boy was not dead. The boy was on the other side of this wall, probably already three streets away, carrying Khalil’s journal — his life’s work, his obsession, every transmutation circle he had ever encountered or designed, including the partial copy of the Iram array that he had spent six hours transcribing in the plaza.

Khalil drew a circle on the wall. Fast — his right hand moving with the speed that Zahra had identified as military training, the pen tracing the geometric array for material dissolution in under four seconds. He pressed his palm to the center. The circle activated — blue light, the standard luminescence of a properly formed array, the energy flowing through the geometric channels and into the transmuted stone. The wall dissolved. Not dramatically — no explosion, no shower of fragments. The stone simply became sand, falling away in a cascade of fine particles that pattered against the glass street like rain.

The alley was empty. The boy was gone.

Khalil retrieved his satchel from the ground where the boy had dropped it — dropped it, not kept it, which was strange. A thief who stole a journal and then abandoned it three streets later was either a very bad thief or a thief who had taken what he actually wanted and discarded the container. Khalil opened the satchel. Checked the contents. The journal was there. The pen was there. The ink was there. The dried food and the water skin and the small pouch of coins were there.

Nothing was missing.

But something was different. He opened the journal. Flipped through the pages — the familiar arrays, the careful line work, the geometric precision of fourteen years of practice. Everything was in order. Everything was where it should be. Except—

The last page. The page on which he had been copying the Iram array. The page that had been half-finished when the boy appeared. The page was complete. The outer ring — the containment hexagons that Khalil had been transcribing — was finished. Not in Khalil’s handwriting. Not in ink. The lines were transmuted into the paper itself, the cellulose fibers restructured to form dark channels that replicated the geometry of the array with a precision that exceeded anything a human hand could produce.

The boy had completed his copy. Without a pen. Without ink. By touching the paper and willing the fibers to rearrange themselves into the pattern of the transmutation circle.

Khalil closed the journal. Stood in the empty alley. Listened to the glass city sing its endless note.

Then he went to find Zahra.

“Impossible,” Zahra said.

“I saw it.”

“Then you saw something that contradicts every theoretical model of alchemical energy transfer developed in the last five hundred years. Circles are not optional. They’re structural. They’re the mechanism by which human intent is translated into physical transformation. Without the geometric framework, the energy has no—”

“I know the theory. I also know what I saw. He touched the wall. The wall changed. No circle. No array. No preparation. He touched it and it obeyed.”

They were in the laboratory. Tariq was there, sitting in his glass chair, his transparent fingers steepled in front of his face. He had not spoken since Khalil began his account. He had not moved. He had listened with the absolute stillness of a man who was hearing something he had expected to hear and had been dreading.

“The boy,” Tariq said. “Describe him again.”

“Young. Fifteen, sixteen. Dark skin — river city complexion. Small. Fast. Barefoot. Clothes held together by optimism and thread.”

“And the transmutation. The wall. Describe exactly what happened.”

“He was running. He touched the wall as he rounded the corner — palm flat, full contact. The glass under his hand became stone. Opaque. Solid. The transmutation was instantaneous — no delay, no visible energy discharge, no luminescence. One moment glass, the next moment stone.”

“No light,” Tariq said. “You’re certain. No blue glow. No visible activation.”

“None.”

Tariq closed his eyes. The glass in his eyelids was thin enough to be translucent — Khalil could see the brown irises moving beneath the crystal skin, tracking thoughts that were three hundred years in the making.

“His name is Idris,” Tariq said. “And he is not performing alchemy.”

The silence that followed was the silence of a statement that sounded like a contradiction and wasn’t.

“Explain,” Zahra said. Her fingers were drumming — fast, the rhythm of a mind that had encountered a paradox and was attempting to resolve it through sheer cognitive force.

“Alchemy is a process,” Tariq said. “A practitioner channels intent through a geometric framework — the circle — which translates that intent into physical transformation. The circle is the language. The alchemist is the speaker. The transmutation is the sentence. Without the language, the speaker cannot form sentences. This is fundamental. This is true.”

“Then how—”

“Because Idris is not the speaker. Idris is the language.”

Tariq opened his eyes. The brown irises fixed on Khalil with an intensity that the glass face could not fully express — the human emotion trapped inside the crystal shell, pressing against the transparent surface like a hand pressing against a window.

“Sixteen years ago,” Tariq said, “Nasreen performed an experiment. She had been refining the Stone’s formula for nearly three centuries. She had tried dozens of variations — different circles, different catalysts, different configurations of energy and matter. All failed. All produced rebounds. All confirmed what the Iram catastrophe had demonstrated: the Stone required the blood of many children as its catalyst, and the rebound of the creation process was catastrophic.”

“So she changed her approach. Instead of creating the Stone as an external object — a substance, a thing, a product of transmutation — she attempted to create a person who embodied the Stone’s properties. A human being whose body was itself a transmutation circle. A living array. A person who could channel alchemical energy without the geometric framework because the framework was built into their flesh, their bones, their blood. Written into them at the cellular level. Encoded in their DNA, if you’ll forgive the anachronism.”

“She experimented on a person,” Khalil said. His voice was flat. The flatness was not calm — it was the surface tension of anger held in check by the understanding that anger was expensive and he could not afford to spend it.

“She experimented on a pregnant woman. A woman from the river cities — Madinat Sayl, specifically. A woman who was carrying a child. Nasreen performed a transmutation on the unborn child — a subtle, precise, extraordinarily complex transmutation that rewrote the developing body’s structure, embedding transmutation arrays in the cellular architecture. The circles are not drawn on Idris’s skin. They are not tattooed or carved or painted. They are grown. They are part of him. They are him.”

“The mother,” Zahra said. “What happened to the mother?”

“She survived the transmutation. She died in childbirth. The somatic rebound — there is always a rebound — manifested as a hemorrhage that no physician could stop and no alchemist could heal, because the bleeding was not physical. It was alchemical. The exchange for creating a living transmutation circle was the life of the circle’s vessel. The mother for the child. A life for a life.”

“The Balance,” Khalil said.

“The Balance,” Tariq confirmed. “The law is absolute. Even Nasreen, with three centuries of research and a Djinn ally and the most sophisticated understanding of alchemy in human history, could not bypass it. She could redirect it. She could choose where the cost was paid. But she could not eliminate the cost.”

“And the boy — Idris — he doesn’t know any of this.”

“He knows he can transmute without circles. He knows he hears a voice — a presence that guides his hands when he reshapes matter. He calls it the Whisperer. He thinks it’s a Djinn. He’s half right.”

“What’s the other half?”

Tariq’s glass fingers tapped against each other — a crystalline clicking, like beads on an abacus. “The Whisperer is a Djinn. But not a normal Djinn. Not a Marid or an Ifrit or a Jann or a Si’lat. The Whisperer is something older. Something that predates the categories. A fragment of the primordial residue — the original rebound of the original transmutation, the moment when existence was created from nothing and the cost of that creation produced the Djinn as its byproduct.”

“A fragment of the first Djinn,” Zahra said. “Embedded in a human child. By Nasreen.”

“Nasreen didn’t embed the Whisperer. Nasreen created the vessel. The living circle. The human array. The Whisperer found the vessel on its own — drawn to it the way all Djinn are drawn to transmutation, but more intensely, more specifically, because Idris is not just performing transmutation. He is transmutation. He is the process made flesh. And the Whisperer — the fragment of the primordial Djinn — recognized in Idris something it had been searching for since the beginning of existence: a body. A form. A way to exist in the material world not as a shadow or a voice or a presence in the spaces between, but as a being. Physical. Real. Alive.”

“The Whisperer wants to become human,” Khalil said.

“The Whisperer wants to become real. Human is the closest available option.”

The laboratory hummed. The glass walls vibrated with the singing of the trapped souls. And somewhere in the ruins of Iram, a sixteen-year-old boy with transmutation circles grown into his bones was running through the glass streets, guided by a voice that was older than the universe and hungrier than anything that had ever existed.

“We need to find him,” Khalil said.

“Yes,” Tariq said. “Before Nasreen does. Because Nasreen created Idris for a purpose. She didn’t experiment on his mother out of curiosity. She did it because the Stone’s formula requires a catalyst — the blood of many children, their unlived potential as fuel. And a living transmutation circle — a human being who is alchemy — would be the most powerful catalyst imaginable. Idris is not just a boy who can transmute without circles. He is the final ingredient in Nasreen’s formula. He is the key to the Philosopher’s Stone.”

“He’s sixteen years old,” Zahra said.

“He’s sixteen years old,” Tariq agreed. “And he is the most valuable and the most endangered person in the world.”

They found him at sunset.

He was sitting on the highest point of the glass palace — the apex of the central dome, the violet dome, the seventh stage of alchemical transformation. He was sitting cross-legged on the curved glass surface, which should have been impossible — the dome was steep, the glass was smooth, there was nothing to grip and no reason for a human body to remain stationary on a surface angled at forty-five degrees.

But the glass under him had been transmuted. A small patch — just large enough for a seated boy — had been changed from smooth crystal to rough stone, providing grip. A casual transmutation. An afterthought. The kind of thing a normal alchemist would need a circle and thirty seconds of concentration to achieve, performed by Idris with a touch and a thought while he was busy doing something else.

He was reading Khalil’s journal.

“That’s mine,” Khalil called up from the base of the dome.

“I gave it back,” the boy called down. His voice was lighter than Khalil expected — not the rough growl of a street survivor but something musical, something that carried in the glass city’s acoustics like a note added to the eternal singing. “I just borrowed it for a bit. Wanted to see your circles.” He was eating something — a strip of dried meat, chewing with the unselfconscious enthusiasm of a teenager who was always hungry. A crumb fell from his lip and he caught it mid-air, reflexes sharp as a cat’s. “Also, your handwriting is terrible. How does anyone read those notes?”

“You completed my copy of the Iram array.”

“It was half-finished. That bothered me. Things should be finished or not started. Half is worse than nothing.”

He pulled a strip of dried meat from somewhere inside his clothes — Khalil did not want to know where a street thief stored his provisions — and offered it downward. “Hungry? You look hungry. You all look hungry. Do scholars not eat? Is that a rule?”

Khalil looked at Zahra. Zahra looked at Tariq. Tariq looked at the boy on the dome with the expression of a man watching a lit fuse burn toward a powder keg — fascinated, terrified, unable to look away.

“Come down,” Khalil said. “We need to talk.”

“People who say ‘we need to talk’ never have anything good to say. In my experience, that phrase is followed by either a beating or a lecture, and I’m not interested in either.”

“No beating. No lecture. I want to understand how you did what you did. The wall. The journal. The transmutation without circles.”

Silence from the dome. The boy — Idris — was still. The journal was open in his lap. The sunset painted the glass city in colors that had no names — the expanded spectrum of alchemically transmuted crystal, refracting the dying light into frequencies that existed only in the boundary between the visible and the invisible.

“The voice told me to come here,” Idris said. His tone had changed — the lightness gone, replaced by something careful, something guarded, the voice of a person who had learned that honesty was dangerous and was choosing to be honest anyway. “The Whisperer. It’s been talking to me since I was small. Since before I could talk back. It said: go to the glass city. It said: find the man with the his left arm. It said: show him what you can do.”

“The Whisperer told you to steal my journal?”

“The Whisperer told me to get your attention. Stealing seemed efficient.”

Despite everything — the impossibility, the danger, the weight of Tariq’s revelations about Nasreen and the Stone and the boy’s terrible origin — Khalil felt the corner of his mouth twitch. The boy was sharp. The boy was funny.

“Come down,” Khalil said again. “Please.”

A pause. Then the boy’s expression changed. The lightness vanished — replaced by something else, something harder, something that was not Idris at all. The dark eyes went flat. The posture shifted — the loose, easy stance of a street survivor becoming rigid, formal, the body language of a person who was not a sixteen-year-old boy but something vastly older wearing a sixteen-year-old boy’s skin.

“He should not come down,” the boy said. But the voice was wrong. Deeper. Layered. The voice of something speaking through a human throat the way wind spoke through a reed — using the instrument but not belonging to it. “He should stay here. The dome is safe. The streets are not. There are energy signatures in the eastern quarter that suggest—”

The boy blinked. Shook his head. The rigidity dissolved. The flat eyes became dark and warm and confused. Idris was back — the real Idris, the street survivor, the boy who stole journals and sat on domes and was funny.

“Sorry,” Idris said. He spoke with shaky. “It does that sometimes. Takes over. I lose — time. Minutes. Sometimes longer. I’ll be walking somewhere and then I’m somewhere else and I don’t remember how I got there. The Whisperer says it’s for my protection. Says it takes control when there’s danger I can’t see. But—” He stopped. Swallowed. “But sometimes I think it takes control because it wants to. Because it wants to be the one walking. The one seeing. The one being. And I’m just — the body it’s wearing.”

The confession hung in the air like smoke. The terrifying, intimate confession of a boy who shared his body with something ancient and was not always sure which of them was in charge.

“Come down,” Khalil said again. “Please.”

A pause. Then the boy moved — not climbing down but sliding, the glass surface under him transmuting as he went, roughening ahead of him and smoothing behind him, creating a controlled descent that was half slide and half controlled fall. He landed on the glass street with the easy grace of someone who had been falling and catching himself since childhood.

Up close, he was younger than Khalil had estimated. Fifteen, maybe. The wildness of his hair and the sharpness of his features had added years from a distance. His eyes were dark — nearly black, the irises so deep they seemed to absorb light rather than reflect it. There was something in those eyes that Khalil recognized. Not the desperation — though that was there too, the baseline desperation of a child who had grown up alone in a world that did not care whether he lived or died. Something else. Something deeper.

Khalil recognized the look. He had seen it in the mirror. The look of a person who had been betrayed by the world so many times that trust itself had become a foreign language.

“Who hurt you?” Khalil asked. Not gently — directly. The way you asked a soldier about a wound.

Idris was quiet for a long time. The glass city sang around them.

“There was a boy,” Idris said. “In the river cities. Yaqub. We ran together for two years. Shared food. Shared hiding places. Shared the cold when winter came and the doorways were full and the only warmth was another body pressed against yours in the dark.” He paused. “I thought he was my friend. First friend. Only friend. I would have died for him. I told him that once, and he laughed, and the laugh was warm, and I thought that was what friendship was. The warmth. The laugh. The knowing that someone would be there when you woke up.”

“What happened?”

“He sold me.” The words were flat. The tone of a person describing something that had happened to someone else, because the only way to say it was to pretend it had. “A slaver came through the river district. Yaqub told me we were going to rob the slaver’s camp — said there was gold, said we’d be rich, said we’d buy a boat and sail south and never be cold again. I believed him. I believed him because he was my friend and friends don’t lie and I was thirteen and I didn’t know yet that the world was the kind of place where a boy could sell his only friend for twelve silver coins.”

Khalil said nothing. Some stories needed silence the way wounds needed air.

“The slaver’s name was Murad. Big man. Hands like shovels. He had a camp outside the city — tents, cages, the smell of unwashed bodies and fear. Yaqub led me right to the gate. Said ‘wait here, I’ll check the guard rotation.’ I waited. Like a fool. Like a trusting, stupid fool who thought the warmth was real.” Idris’s voice cracked. “Murad came out instead. With chains. Yaqub was behind him, counting coins. Twelve silver. That was my price. Two years of friendship. Twelve silver.”

“Idris—”

“They stripped me.” The words came faster now, the wall crumbling. “In front of the other slaves. Thirty people in cages, watching. Murad said it was procedure — check for disease, check for marks, check for value. But it wasn’t procedure. It was breaking. You strip a person naked in front of strangers and you take something from them that clothes can’t give back. You take the self. The part that says ‘I am a person and I have boundaries and you cannot cross them.’ They crossed them. They crossed all of them.”

He was shaking. The living array beneath his skin flickered — the transmutation circles in his bones responding to the emotion, the alchemy in his body resonating with the memory. The glass beneath him hummed.

“Murad put me in a cage with a man named Salih. Old man. Thin. He’d been a teacher once — you could tell by his hands, the ink stains, the way he held things carefully like everything was a book. Salih gave me his blanket. His only blanket. Said ‘the cold is worse for the young.’ He died three days later. The cold took him. The cold he gave to me by giving me his blanket.”

“How did you escape?”

“The transmutation.” Idris looked at his hands. “I didn’t know what I was doing. Didn’t know what I was. I just touched the cage bars and I wanted them to open and they opened. The iron became dust. The lock became nothing. I walked out. The other slaves were screaming — not at me, at the miracle. A boy who could dissolve iron with his hands. Murad came running. I touched his chains and they became sand. He looked at me the way people look at things they don’t understand and can’t control. The way people look at fire.”

“Did you free the others?”

“I tried. I opened every cage. Dissolved every chain. Thirty people, free, running into the night. But Murad had guards. The guards had swords. I could dissolve metal but I couldn’t dissolve a blade that was already in someone’s back.” His tone was very quiet. “Salih’s wife was in the women’s cage. I freed her. She ran. A guard caught her at the perimeter. I heard the sound. I still hear the sound.”

The glass city was quiet. Even the singing seemed to have dimmed.

“And Yaqub?” Khalil asked.

“I found him. Three months later. In a market in Basra. He was spending the twelve silver. Buying dates. Dates. He sold me for dates and a new pair of shoes.” Idris’s dark eyes were wet. The moisture caught the glass city’s light and held it — two points of refracted luminescence in a face that was trying very hard to be stone. “I stood across the market and I watched him eat the dates and I thought — I could kill him. I could touch him and turn his bones to powder. I could dissolve him the way I dissolved the chains. And I wanted to. The wanting was so strong it tasted like metal in my mouth.”

“But you didn’t.”

“I didn’t. Because Salih gave me his blanket. Because an old man who had nothing gave me the only thing he had, and the giving killed him, and the killing was the most generous thing anyone had ever done for me. And I thought — if I kill Yaqub, then Salih died for nothing. Then the blanket meant nothing. Then the world is just cages and chains and twelve silver coins and there’s no point in any of it.” He wiped his eyes with the back of his hand. The gesture was rough. Angry. The anger of a boy who did not want to cry and was crying anyway. “So I walked away. And I’ve been walking ever since. Three years. Alone. Because alone is safe. Alone means no one can sell you. Alone means no one can give you their blanket and die.”

Khalil was quiet for a long time. The glass dome was warm beneath them. The city sang.

“Alone is not safe,” Khalil said. “Alone is just a different kind of cage. The bars are invisible but they’re there. I know. I’ve been in that cage for five years.”

“You have a sister.”

“I have a sister I destroyed. That’s not the same as having a sister. That’s having a reminder.” He looked at his transmuted arm. “We’re both carrying cages, Idris. The question is whether we keep carrying them alone or whether we—” He stopped. Shook his head. “I’m not going to promise you anything. Promises are what Yaqub made. I’m going to do something instead. I’m going to be here tomorrow. And the day after. And the day after that. And if that’s not enough, it’s not enough. But it’s what I have.”

Idris looked at him. The dark eyes — wet, angry, afraid — searched Khalil’s face for the lie. For the twelve silver coins. For the betrayal that experience had taught him was always coming.

He didn’t find it.

The look of a person who carried something inside them that they did not understand and could not control and were afraid of.

Khalil knew that look. He saw it in the mirror every morning, in the eyes of a man who had seen the Mizan and the Weighing and had been changed by the seeing in ways he could not articulate.

“I’m Khalil,” he said. “This is Zahra. And the man who looks like he’s made of glass is Tariq, because he is made of glass.”

“I know who you are,” Idris said. “The Whisperer told me. The soldier whose arm was transmuted. The scholar who won’t draw circles. The glass man who’s been dying for three hundred years.” He looked at each of them in turn — quick, assessing glances, the evaluations of a street survivor who had learned to read people the way Zahra read transmutation arrays. “The Whisperer says you’re looking for the Stone. It says I should help you. It says—” He stopped. His expression changed — a flicker of something that might have been pain, might have been fear, might have been the sensation of a voice inside your head saying something you didn’t want to hear. “It says I’m part of the formula.”

“Do you know what that means?” Zahra asked. Her voice was gentle — gentler than Khalil had heard it, the sharpness softened by something that might have been compassion or might have been the particular tenderness that brilliant people felt toward things they could not explain.

“It means I’m not normal. I know that. I’ve always known that. Normal people can’t do what I do. Normal people need circles. Normal people don’t hear voices.” He held up his hand — palm out, fingers spread. “Watch.”

He touched the glass street. And the glass moved. Not transmuted — not changed from one substance to another. Moved. The crystal surface rippled like water, concentric rings spreading out from his palm, the solid glass behaving like liquid for a radius of three meters before settling back into solidity. The ripple passed through the frozen figures nearby — the merchant, the customer — and for one instant, one fraction of one heartbeat, the glass people moved. The merchant’s hand completed its offering gesture. The customer’s hand closed around the invisible merchandise. The transaction, frozen for centuries, was completed.

Then the glass was still again. The figures were frozen. The moment was over.

But Khalil had seen it. And Zahra had seen it. And Tariq, standing behind them, his glass body catching the last light of the sunset, had seen it too.

“That wasn’t transmutation,” Zahra whispered. “That was animation. He didn’t change the glass. He gave it motion. He gave it—”

“Life,” Tariq said. The voice was very quiet. “He gave it life. For one heartbeat. The way the Stone gave itself existence for one heartbeat during the catastrophe.” He looked at Idris with the brown eyes that were the last human part of him, and in those eyes was something that Khalil had not seen before in the glass man’s face.

Hope. And terror. In equal measure.

“You’re not just a living circle,” Tariq said. “You’re a living Stone. Or the potential for one. The Whisperer — the primordial fragment — it’s not just guiding you. It’s growing you. Developing you. Preparing you for—”

“For what?” Idris asked. His eyes were wide. The street-survivor bravado was gone, stripped away by the weight of what he had just done and the weight of what Tariq was saying and the weight of being sixteen years old and learning that you were not a person but a formula.

“For the transmutation,” Tariq said. “The final one. The one that Nasreen has been preparing for three hundred years. The one that will either create the Philosopher’s Stone or destroy everything.”

the ruins sang. The sunset faded. Four people stood in the ruins of a civilization that had been destroyed by the pursuit of the impossible, and the youngest of them — the boy with no circles and no training and no understanding of what he was — looked at his hands as if seeing them for the first time.

“I don’t want to be a formula,” Idris said.

“No one does,” Khalil said. He held out the transmuted hand — the transmuted arm, the reminder, the price of a failed exchange. “But we don’t always get to choose what we are. We only get to choose what we do about it.”

Idris looked at the the ceramic hand. At the ceramic fingers that moved stiffly but moved. At the arm that was a wound and a monument to failure.

He took it.

His grip was warm. Human. The hand of a boy, not a formula. And when his skin touched the clay, the transmuted surface hummed — a vibration that traveled up through the inert arm and into the boundary where flesh met clay and from there into his bones, his blood, his mind. For one instant, Khalil felt what Idris felt — the constant presence of the Whisperer, the low murmur of a voice that was older than time, the sensation of carrying something vast inside a body that was too small to contain it.

Then the contact broke. Idris let go. The humming stopped.

“Dawn,” Khalil said. “We leave at dawn. The Kahf Thul. The first piece of the formula.”

“I don’t have anything to pack,” Idris said. Then, quieter: “I’ve never had anything to pack.”

He said it without self-pity — just a fact, the way Zahra stated axioms. But something in the way he looked at Khalil’s satchel, at Zahra’s notebooks, at Tariq’s scroll — the possessions of people who had lives worth carrying — made Khalil’s chest tighten.

“Then you’ll be ready early.”

The boy almost smiled. Almost. The expression flickered across his face like the ripple he had sent through the glass — there and gone, a moment of humanity in a situation that was rapidly exceeding the boundaries of what humanity could contain.

Four of them now. A soldier, a scholar, a glass man, and a boy who was alchemy made flesh. Walking into the desert. Following a trail left by dead alchemists. Racing a three-hundred-year-old immortal to the most dangerous substance in the world.

Iram hummed them through the night. And in the spaces between the notes — in the frequencies that existed between sound and silence, between the human world and the Djinn world, between what was and what could be — the Whisperer watched through Idris’s dark eyes and calculated the cost of what was coming.

The cost, as always, would be everything.

The question was who would pay it.
