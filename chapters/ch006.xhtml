Chapter 3: The Glass Man

They found Tariq in the deepest part of the palace, in a chamber that had been a laboratory before the transmutation turned it into a crystal tomb.

The chamber was underground — beneath the palace’s central dome, accessible through a staircase that spiraled down through glass floors, each step transparent, each landing offering a view into the rooms below like looking through layers of ice into a frozen lake. The deeper they descended, the warmer the glass became. The residual energy of the transmutation was concentrated here, in the place where the reaction had originated, where the circle’s center had been, where Nasreen had stood and offered her soul and the universe had responded with a catastrophe.

The laboratory was large. Circular. The walls were lined with glass shelves that held glass instruments — alembics, retorts, crucibles, the tools of the alchemist’s trade, all transmuted, all preserved, all useless. Transmutation circles covered every surface — carved into the walls, the floor, the ceiling, overlapping and intersecting in a palimpsest of alchemical research that represented decades of work by the Order’s finest practitioners. Zahra’s breath caught when she saw them. Her fingers twitched. Her eyes moved across the arrays with the rapid, hungry scanning of a scholar who had just walked into the largest library in the world and been told she could read anything she wanted.

“Don’t touch the walls,” said a voice from the far end of the chamber. “The circles are still active. Residual charge. Touch the wrong line and you’ll lose a finger. I lost three before I learned to be careful.”

The voice was dry. Cracked. The voice of a man who had not spoken to another human being in a long time and whose vocal cords had atrophied from disuse, producing sounds that were more rasp than speech, more friction than language.

Tariq the Burned sat in a chair made of glass — a chair that had been a wooden stool before the transmutation and was now a transparent throne, beautiful and uncomfortable. He was old. Sixty, perhaps. Perhaps older — age was difficult to determine on a body that had been so thoroughly damaged that the normal markers of time were obscured by the markers of catastrophe.

His skin was the first thing Khalil noticed. It was not skin. Not entirely. The surface was human — pores, wrinkles, the fine hair of a man’s forearms — but beneath the surface, visible through the translucent outer layer, the flesh had been transmuted. Partially. Incompletely. The muscle tissue had been converted to glass — not the clear, flawless glass of the city but a cloudy, milky substance that caught the light and refracted it through his body, making him glow faintly from within, as if he had swallowed a lamp and the lamp was slowly consuming him.

His hands were the worst. The fingers were almost entirely glass — transparent from the second knuckle to the tip, the bones visible inside like specimens in a jar. He held them carefully, the way you’d hold something fragile, which they were. Three fingers on his left hand were missing — broken off, Khalil realized. Glass fingers. Shattered by contact with a surface that was harder than the transmuted flesh.

His eyes were human. Brown. Warm. The eyes of a man who had been kind once, before the glass started growing, before the catastrophe took his body and left his mind intact inside a shell that was slowly, inexorably, becoming a statue.

“Khalil ibn Amir,” Tariq said. He did not stand. Standing was difficult — the glass had reached his knees, and the joints moved stiffly, grinding crystal against crystal with a sound like sand in a mechanism. “I sent the message. I’m glad you came. I was beginning to think no one would.”

“You sent the coded letter?”

“I sent twelve. Over three years. To every alchemist whose work I could access through the underground networks. You’re the first to come. The others were either too afraid, too skeptical, or too dead. The Muhtasib have been efficient lately.”

Zahra stepped forward. Her eyes were on Tariq’s hands — on the glass fingers, the transparent flesh, the visible bones. She was reading him the way she read transmutation circles — tracing the lines, identifying the patterns, inferring the process from the product.

“You were at Iram,” she said. “During the catastrophe. You were inside the circle.”

“I was at the edge. The outer ring. I was part of the containment team — twelve alchemists responsible for maintaining the hexagonal boundary that was supposed to keep the energy inside the circle. We failed. Obviously.” He raised his glass hands. The light passed through them, casting prismatic shadows on the floor. “The somatic rebound hit the containment team first. We were the barrier. When the barrier broke, we absorbed the initial wave. The others — the ones closer to the center — were transmuted instantly. Completely. Glass in a heartbeat. We had a few seconds longer. Long enough for the partial transmutation to begin but not complete. Long enough for the glass to start growing but not finish.”

“You’ve been partially transmuted for three hundred years,” Zahra said. The restless fingers had stopped. Her voice was quiet — the quiet of a scholar confronting a phenomenon that exceeded her theoretical framework. “That’s not possible. A partial transmutation should resolve — either completing or reversing — within hours. Days at most. The energy state is unstable. The system should seek equilibrium.”

“It does seek equilibrium. Constantly. The glass grows. Every day, a little more of me becomes crystal. The process is slow — glacially slow, compared to the instantaneous transmutation that took the city. But it’s relentless. It doesn’t stop. It doesn’t reverse. It just — advances. Millimeter by millimeter. Year by year.” He flexed his glass fingers. The joints ground. A fine powder of crystal dust fell from the knuckles like snow. “I have a Jann. A pact. The Djinn slows the process — feeds its own energy into the boundary between flesh and glass, maintaining the instability, preventing the equilibrium that would complete the transmutation and turn me into another statue in the collection.”

“A Jann pact,” Khalil said. “For three hundred years.”

“The Jann is patient. That’s their nature. And the payment is modest — sensory experiences. The feeling of sunlight on skin. The taste of water. The sound of wind. Small things. Human things. Things that a being made of earth and stone cannot generate on its own.” He paused. “I’m running out of sensations to trade. centuries of experiences is a large inventory, but it’s finite. The Jann has consumed most of my memories of taste. I can’t remember what bread tastes like. Or wine. Or the dates that grew in the courtyard of my family’s house in Iram — before the house became glass and the dates became crystal and the courtyard became a monument to hubris.”

He said this with the practiced calm of a man who had rehearsed the loss so many times that the rehearsal had become the reality. But Khalil heard the fracture beneath the calm — the sound of a person who had been losing pieces of himself for three centuries and had learned to describe the losses with precision because precision was the only defense against the enormity of what was being lost.

“The Jann’s name is Bahir,” Tariq continued. “It means ‘dazzling.’ An ironic name for a being made of earth and stone — the least dazzling of the Djinn types. The Jann are the lowest order. The simplest. The closest to the material world. They don’t have the Marid’s power or the Ifrit’s fire or the Si’lat’s ability to change shape. They have patience. They have endurance. They have the geological stubbornness of beings that are made of the same substance as mountains and share the mountains’ indifference to time.”

“Bahir approached me three days after the catastrophe. I was lying in the ruins — half glass, half flesh, unable to move, unable to die, unable to do anything except feel the transmutation advancing through my body millimeter by millimeter. The other survivors — the other members of the containment team who had been partially transmuted — were dying. The instability was resolving. Their bodies were completing the transmutation, the glass consuming the remaining flesh, the process reaching the equilibrium that physics demanded. One by one, they became statues. One by one, the partial became complete. I watched eleven colleagues die. Eleven friends. Eleven alchemists who had stood at the edge of the circle with me and had absorbed the rebound with me and had been partially transmuted with me. They died, and I did not, and the reason I did not was Bahir.”

“The Jann offered the pact?”

“The Jann offered the pact. Not out of compassion — Jann do not experience compassion the way humans do. Out of curiosity. Bahir had never encountered a partial transmutation that lasted more than hours. The instability fascinated it. The boundary between flesh and glass — the dynamic, constantly shifting interface where organic matter met silicon dioxide — was, to a being made of earth and stone, the most interesting phenomenon it had ever observed. Bahir wanted to study the boundary. Bahir wanted to understand why my body was resisting the equilibrium that had claimed the others. And the price of that study — the payment for the Jann’s intervention, the energy required to maintain the instability — was sensation. My sensation. My memories of taste and touch and smell, fed to a being that had existed for millennia without ever tasting bread or feeling sunlight or smelling the specific, complex, irreplaceable scent of dates ripening in a courtyard in a city that no longer existed.”

The chamber was quiet. The glass walls hummed — the ambient vibration of the ruins, the singing of ten thousand trapped souls, filtered through layers of crystal and stone.

Tariq paused. Tilted his head — the glass in his neck grinding, the crystal producing a sound like wind chimes in a slow breeze. He was listening. Not to them. To the city. To the singing. To the sustained, ten-thousand-voice frequency that filled the ruins like water filled a basin.

“Do you hear that?” he said. “The singing. I’ve been listening to it for three hundred years. Three hundred years of the same note. The same frequency. The same ten thousand voices compressed into a single sound. And I still — every morning, when the dawn light hits the glass and the singing shifts, just slightly, just enough to notice — I still find it beautiful. centuries, and the beauty has not diminished. Three hundred years, and I still sit in this chair at dawn and listen and feel — grateful. For the hearing. For the capacity to hear. For the irreplaceable, temporary experience of being a consciousness that can perceive sound.”

He flexed his glass fingers. The crystal dust fell. He watched it fall with the attention of a man who had learned to savor every sensation because every sensation might be the last one the Jann had not yet consumed.

“I cannot taste bread,” he said. “I cannot smell dates. I cannot feel the warmth of another person’s hand. The Jann has taken these things. But I can hear. I can see. I can feel the vibration of the glass beneath me and the warmth of the residual energy in the walls. And these things — these remaining things — are not consolation. They are not the scraps of a life that has been consumed. They are enough. They are the irreducible minimum of experience that makes existence worth continuing. And I savor them. Every one. Every day. Every dawn.”

He looked at Khalil with the brown eyes — still warm, still human.

“You said the Order succeeded,” Khalil said. “In your letter. You said the Stone was created.”

“For one heartbeat. One fraction of one second. The Stone existed. I felt it — even at the edge of the circle, even through the containment array, I felt the moment when the Balance was suspended. The moment when the law that governs every transmutation, every reaction, every exchange of matter and energy in the universe — stopped. For one heartbeat, the universe held its breath. And in that breath, the Stone was real.”

He paused. Something moved behind his eyes — a shadow, a memory he was choosing not to share. In that fraction of a second, he had seen something else. Something beneath the city. A vast shape, buried, radiating, alive in a way that had nothing to do with biology. A white light, impossibly deep, impossibly old, responding to the synthetic Stone the way a sleeping giant responds to a hand on its shoulder. He had seen it. He had understood what it meant. And he had spent three hundred years deciding not to tell anyone.

“Then the rebound.”

“Then the rebound. The cost came due. All at once. Three centuries of accumulated debt — every shortcut, every violation, every time the Order pushed past the boundaries of what was permitted and told themselves the cost would be manageable — all of it, compounded, collected in a single instant. The city paid. The alchemists paid. thousands of innocent people paid.”

“And Nasreen?”

Tariq’s expression changed. The warmth in his brown eyes dimmed — not extinguished but shadowed, the way a lamp dims when something passes between it and the viewer. “Nasreen paid the most. And the least. She lost her mortality. She gained — what? Existence? Persistence? The ability to continue rotting for three centuries while the world moved on and forgot her name?” He shook his head. The motion produced a faint chiming sound — glass against glass, the crystal in his neck grinding against the crystal in his skull. “She was my student. Before the catastrophe. I taught her. I saw the talent in her — the instinctive understanding, the ability to feel the flow of alchemical energy the way a musician feels the flow of melody. I nurtured it. I encouraged it. I told her she was special.”

“She was special.”

“She was dangerous. And I was too proud to see the difference. I saw a gifted student. I should have seen a woman who would volunteer her soul for an experiment because she believed — truly, absolutely, with the unshakeable certainty of a person who has never been wrong about anything important — that the experiment would work. That the Stone would be created. That the Balance would be bypassed. That the cost would be acceptable.”

He was quiet for a moment. The brown eyes — the last fully human part of him — looked at something that was not in the room. Something centuries distant. Something that existed only in the memory of a man who had been losing memories for three centuries and had chosen, with the deliberate cruelty of a person who understood the economics of loss, to preserve the memories that hurt the most.

“I remember the day she came to the Order. She was seventeen. A girl from the southern provinces — a merchant’s daughter, educated beyond her station, brilliant beyond her education. She had taught herself alchemy from a manuscript she found in her father’s warehouse — a cargo that had been confiscated from a trader and never claimed. A basic text. Elementary theory. The kind of primer that the Order gave to first-year students. She had read it in a week. She had understood it in a month. She had surpassed it in a year.”

“She arrived at Iram with a letter of introduction from a provincial alchemist who had tested her and been frightened by the results. The letter said: This girl understands things I cannot teach her. She needs teachers who are better than I am. She needs the Order. I read the letter. I tested her myself. I asked her to draw a transmutation circle from memory — a standard test, the kind we gave to all applicants. She drew a circle I had never seen. Not from the textbooks. Not from the curriculum. A circle she had designed herself, at seventeen, that solved a problem in energy distribution that the Order’s senior researchers had been working on for a decade.”

“I should have been cautious. I should have recognized the danger of a mind that moved faster than its wisdom, that could solve problems before it understood the implications of the solutions, that could design circles before it understood the cost of activating them. I should have slowed her down. I should have taught her patience before I taught her power.”

“Instead, I taught her everything. I gave her access to the restricted archives. I introduced her to the senior researchers. I brought her into the inner circle — the group that was developing the formula for the Stone. I made her my protégé. My successor. The person who would carry the Order’s work forward after I was gone.”

“And she carried it forward. Into the catastrophe.”

“The cost was a city.”

“The cost was a city. And Nasreen’s response — her response, when she walked out of the glass and saw what the rebound had done — was not horror. Not grief. Not the crushing, annihilating guilt that any sane person would feel upon realizing that their sacrifice had killed ten thousand people. Her response was: The formula was almost right. I need to adjust the variables. I need to try again.”

The silence that followed held the weight of results that were technically fascinating and morally catastrophic.

“She’s been trying again,” Khalil said. “For three hundred years.”

“She’s been refining the formula. Adjusting the variables. Searching for the configuration of circle and catalyst and soul that will produce the Stone without the rebound. She believes it’s possible. She believes that the Iram catastrophe was not a fundamental failure but a calibration error. A miscalculation. A rounding error in the most complex equation ever attempted.”

“Is she right?”

Tariq looked at him. The brown eyes — human, warm, the last fully human part of a body that was slowly becoming glass — held Khalil’s gaze with the steady, unflinching directness of a man who had spent three centuries thinking about this question and had arrived at an answer he did not want to give.

“I don’t know,” he said. “And that’s what terrifies me. Because if she’s wrong, she’ll destroy another city. Maybe more than a city. The formula she’s developing now is more powerful than the one we used at Iram. The circle is larger. The energy requirements are greater. The potential rebound is — incalculable.”

“And if she’s right?”

“If she’s right, then the Balance is a lie. The fundamental law of alchemy — the principle that governs every transmutation, every reaction, every exchange of matter and energy in the universe — is not a law at all. It’s a suggestion. A guideline. A rule that can be broken by anyone with sufficient knowledge and sufficient will.” He paused. “And if the law can be broken, then everything we know about alchemy is wrong. Everything we know about the universe is wrong. The cost of every transmutation ever performed — every arm lost, every memory traded, every life consumed by a rebound — was unnecessary. Optional. A price that was paid not because it had to be paid but because no one knew it didn’t have to be.”

“That would change everything.”

“That would destroy everything. Because the law is not just a principle of alchemy. It’s a principle of existence. The Balance is the reason the universe is stable. It’s the reason matter doesn’t spontaneously transmute. It’s the reason the Djinn stay on their side of the barrier and humans stay on theirs. It’s the reason the world works. Remove the law, and you remove the foundation. And a universe without a foundation is a universe that collapses.”

Tariq reached into the folds of his robe — a garment that was half cloth and half glass, the fabric transmuting along with his body, the weave becoming crystal thread by thread. He produced a scroll. Old. The paper was yellowed, the edges crumbling, the ink faded but legible. He unrolled it on the glass table between them.

A transmutation circle. But not like any circle Khalil had seen. The geometry was — wrong was not the right word. Alien. The lines curved in ways that Euclidean geometry could not describe. The symbols were not the standard elemental notation but something older, something that predated the Order, something that looked less like human writing and more like the patterns that formed naturally in crystal growth, in river deltas, in the branching of lightning.

“This is the formula,” Tariq said. “The original. The circle that was carved in the plaza of Iram. The circle that created the Stone for one heartbeat and destroyed a city in the next. I’ve been studying it for three hundred years. I’ve memorized every line. I’ve analyzed every symbol. I’ve calculated every energy pathway and every potential rebound and every possible outcome of every possible variation.”

“And?”

“And I still don’t understand it. Not fully. The outer rings — containment, direction — those are comprehensible. Advanced, but comprehensible. The inner ring — the transformation array — is beyond me. The geometry is not human. The mathematics are not human. The circle was not designed by the Order of the Mizan. It was given to them.”

“Given by whom?”

Tariq looked at the glass walls. At the humming crystal. At the ruins of a city that had been destroyed by a formula that no human mind had created.

“By the Djinn,” he said. “The transformation ring — the organic geometry, the non-Euclidean curves — it’s Djinn mathematics. Djinn geometry. The language that beings made of smokeless fire use to describe the structure of reality from their side of the barrier. The Order didn’t develop the formula for the Stone. They received it. From a Djinn. A Marid, if the records are accurate. A Marid that approached the Order’s founder three centuries before the catastrophe and offered a trade: the formula for the Stone, in exchange for—”

He stopped. The glass in his throat ground against itself. He coughed — a dry, crystalline sound, like gravel in a glass jar.

“In exchange for what?” Khalil asked.

“In exchange for the attempt. The Marid didn’t want the Stone. It wanted the transmutation. The act of attempting to create the Stone — the massive, reality-bending, barrier-tearing energy release — was what the Marid wanted. Because the energy of the transmutation would tear the barrier between worlds. And a torn barrier would allow the Djinn to cross over. To enter the material world. To experience — directly, physically, in bodies of matter instead of bodies of fire — the sensations and experiences that they had been collecting secondhand through pacts for millennia.”

“The catastrophe wasn’t a failure,” Zahra said. Her voice was barely a whisper. Her fingers had stopped moving entirely — frozen, the way they froze when her mind encountered something that exceeded her capacity to process in real time. “It was the point. The Djinn gave the Order the formula knowing it would fail. Knowing the rebound would tear the barrier. The Stone was bait. The transmutation was the trap. And the Order walked into it.”

“The Order walked into it,” Tariq confirmed. “And ten thousand people paid the price. And the barrier tore. And Suhail — the Marid who gave us the formula — came through. And was trapped. Because the barrier sealed behind it, and the Djinn that had planned to cross over in triumph was instead imprisoned in the ruins of the city it had helped destroy.”

“Poetic justice,” Khalil said.

“There is no justice in alchemy,” Tariq said. “There is only exchange. And the exchange, in this case, was: one Djinn’s freedom for thousands of human lives. The Marid calculated the cost. The Marid accepted the cost. The Marid was wrong about the outcome but not about the price.”

Tariq was quiet for a moment. The glass in his hands caught the light — prismatic, shifting, the colors of a man who was slowly becoming a window.

“But the barrier,” he said. “The barrier is older than the Order. Older than alchemy. Older than any human institution. Do you know what built it?”

Zahra’s fingers stopped drumming. “The Qaedri.”

Tariq looked at her with something that might have been surprise, if surprise were possible in a face that was half crystal. “You’ve read the fragments.”

“Enough to know the name. Not enough to know the history.”

“The history is this.” Tariq leaned back in his glass chair. The crystal groaned. “Before the barrier, the Djinn walked freely in the material world. Not as guests. Not as partners. As predators. The Marid ruled dominions of human cattle — feeding on sensation, on experience, on the raw perceptual data of beings who could taste and touch and smell. The Ifrit burned at will. The Si’lat wore human faces and lived human lives and discarded the lives when the novelty faded. The world belonged to the smokeless fire, and humanity existed to feed it.”

“Then the Qaedri rose. Women — always women, for reasons the texts do not explain — who could see the Nasij. The Pattern. The weave of probability that underlies every event, every choice, every falling leaf and rising tide. They could pull the threads. Rearrange what was likely to happen until the Djinn found themselves outmaneuvered by the geometry of chance itself. A Marid that had ruled for millennia would find its dominion collapsing — not through force but through a cascade of improbable events that were, individually, unremarkable and collectively, devastating. The right drought at the right time. The right rebellion in the right province. The right disease in the right court. The Qaedri didn’t fight the Djinn. They made the universe fight the Djinn.”

“The war lasted centuries. The Djinn adapted. The Qaedri adapted faster. And then one of them — Vaelith Khael, the Dualborn — saw something no Qaedri had seen before. She could perceive both the Pattern and the Void — the space between threads, the absence that defined the weave. The dual perception drove her half-mad. But it also showed her the architecture of reality itself — the seams, the joints, the places where the world could be torn apart and stitched back together in a different configuration.”

“She designed the Binding. A single act of collective sacrifice. Every Qaedri in the world — thousands of them — weaving simultaneously, pulling every thread they could reach, tearing reality in half. Vaelith wove herself into the tear. Became the barrier. Her consciousness, stretched across the membrane between worlds, holding the separation in place through sheer, stubborn, half-insane persistence. Every witch who participated died. Every one. And Vaelith did not die but ceased to live — suspended in the wall she had become, neither alive nor dead, the keystone of a prison built from the bodies of every woman who had ever pulled a thread.”

The glass chamber was silent. Even the singing seemed to have dimmed — the ten thousand voices lowering, as if the trapped souls were listening.

“Ten thousand years,” Tariq said. “The barrier held for thousands of years. The Djinn raged against it. Tested it. Found it absolute. And then the Order — my Order, my colleagues, my friends — tore it open with a transmutation circle and a hundred stolen children.”

He looked at his glass hands. “We didn’t know. We didn’t know what the barrier was. We didn’t know it was a woman. We didn’t know it was built from sacrifice. We thought it was a natural phenomenon — a feature of the universe, like gravity or magnetism. We were wrong. And the wrongness killed ten thousand people.”

Khalil leaned forward. “You said the Qaedri are gone. All of them?”

“Gone,” Tariq said. “But not forgotten. The Order had maps. Fragments, really — copies of copies, translated through six languages, degraded by centuries of transcription. But the geography was consistent. The Qaedri cities, the Djinn dominions, the libraries where the thread-pullers stored their knowledge — all of it existed in what the texts call the Old World. The world before the Binding.”

“Where?” Zahra asked. Her fingers were drumming again — fast, urgent, the rhythm of a mind that had just been given a problem it desperately wanted to solve.

“At the end of the world. Buried under ice.”

“The Binding did not end cleanly,” Tariq continued. “The Djinn retaliated. The Ifrit unleashed what the texts call the Great Fire — a coordinated conflagration that burned the surface of the world. Not natural fire. Djinn-fire. The breath of the Void. Hot enough to melt stone. Hot enough to turn sand to glass — and yes, the irony is not lost on me.” He gestured at the crystal walls. “The Qaedri cities were the primary targets. The Djinn burned the libraries first. They understood that knowledge was more dangerous than any army. Destroy the books and you destroy the capacity to fight.”

“Then the Flood. The Binding’s aftershock — reality recoiling from the wound Vaelith had torn through its center. The oceans rose. All at once. A wall of water that buried everything the fire had not consumed. And then the water froze.”

“Froze,” Zahra repeated. Not a question. The word of a scholar testing a concept against her understanding.

“Miles of ice. Not natural ice — the residue of the Binding itself. The temperature of severed reality. The Order sent three expeditions to the ice fields over the centuries. None returned. The cold is not cold as we understand it. It is the physical manifestation of consequence — the thermodynamic cost of tearing the world in half, frozen into a substance that no alchemy can melt because it is not frozen water. It is frozen debt.”

Khalil thought of his arm. The clay that crept millimeter by millimeter toward his heart. The frozen consequence of his own failed transmutation, advancing with the patience of a creditor who knew the debtor could not run.

“The Old World is under that ice,” Tariq said. “The Qaedri sanctums. The Djinn dominions. The knowledge of how the barrier was built, how it could be repaired, how Vaelith could be freed or destroyed. All of it — entombed. Unreachable. Thousands of leagues of ice in every direction, scoured by winds that carry particles of frozen consequence sharp enough to strip flesh from bone. No human can survive the journey. The knowledge is gone.”

“Gone,” Zahra said. But her fingers were still drumming. The fingers of a woman who had never accepted gone as a final answer.

He rolled up the scroll. Placed it on the table. Looked at Khalil.

“Nasreen has the formula. She’s been refining it for three centuries. She’s close to a version that she believes will work without the catastrophic rebound. And she has something the Order didn’t have — a Djinn ally. A Si’lat named Ghazal, who has been feeding her information from the Djinn side of the barrier for two hundred years.”

“Why would a Djinn help her?”

“Because the Djinn want the barrier torn. They’ve always wanted the barrier torn. Suhail’s plan failed — the Marid was trapped instead of freed. But the other Djinn learned from the failure. They’re patient. They’re ancient. And they’ve been waiting for someone on the human side to try again.”

“And Nasreen is that someone.”

“Nasreen is that someone. And she’s closer than anyone has ever been. And when she attempts the transmutation — when she activates the refined circle with the blood of children as the catalyst — the barrier will tear again. And this time, the tear will be larger. And the Djinn that come through will not be one lonely Marid. They will be legion.”

The glass chamber hummed. The crystal walls vibrated with the singing of ten thousand trapped souls. And in the ruins of Iram, three people — a soldier with a transmuted arm, a scholar with ink-stained hands, and a dying man made of glass — sat in the laboratory where the world had almost ended and contemplated the possibility that it was about to end again.

“So,” Khalil said. “What do we do?”

Tariq smiled. The expression cracked the glass at the corners of his mouth — tiny fractures, spreading like frost on a window, the crystal in his face unable to accommodate the human gesture of a man who had not smiled in a very long time.

“We find the Stone before she does,” he said. “The real Stone. The one that was created for one heartbeat during the catastrophe. It didn’t disappear. It was hidden. By the transmutation itself. The rebound scattered the Stone’s components across the Cradle — encoded in transmutation circles that the Order had placed in ancient sites centuries before the catastrophe, as a failsafe. A way to reassemble the Stone if the first attempt failed.”

“A treasure hunt,” Zahra said. “Across the ancient world. Following clues left by dead alchemists in transmutation circles hidden in ruins.”

“A treasure hunt,” Tariq agreed. “With the fate of the world as the prize. And a three-hundred-year-old immortal alchemist with a Djinn ally as the competition.”

He stood. The glass in his legs ground and chimed. He was shorter than Khalil had expected — the chair had hidden his stature, and the glass had compressed him, the transmutation slowly reducing his body the way erosion reduced a mountain, millimeter by millimeter, year by year.

“We leave at dawn,” Tariq said. “The first site is in the mountains. The Kahf Thul. The Order maintained a research station there — a secondary laboratory, hidden in the rock, protected by transmutation circles that have been active for five centuries.”

He paused. Reached into a pouch at his belt and produced a dried fig. Held it up to the light — the fruit dark and wrinkled, ordinary, the most unremarkable thing in a city of glass. He bit into it. Chewed slowly. Closed his eyes.

“I can’t taste it,” he said. “The Jann took that years ago. But I can feel the texture. The seeds between my teeth. The weight of it on my tongue.” He opened his eyes. Smiled — and this time the smile didn’t crack the glass. It was smaller. Gentler. The smile of a man who had learned to find joy in what remained instead of mourning what was lost. “I eat three figs every evening. I watch the sunset through the western dome — the orange glass turns the light into something that doesn’t exist anywhere else in the world. And I laugh. At least once a day. Usually at something that isn’t funny to anyone but me.”

“What’s funny?” Khalil asked.

“Everything. The absurdity of a glass man eating figs he can’t taste in a dead city. The fact that I’ve been alive for centuries and the thing I miss most is the smell of bread. The cosmic joke of the Balance — that the universe is perfectly fair and that perfect fairness is the cruelest thing imaginable.” He chuckled. The sound was dry, crystalline, but genuine. “You’ll understand eventually. When you’ve been walking long enough, the only sane response is laughter.”

He looked at Khalil. At the clay arm. At the journal in the satchel. At the desperation in the eyes of a man who had broken his sister and would do anything to undo what he had done. “You should know: the Stone cannot restore your sister. Not the way you want. The Stone bypasses the Balance, but it doesn’t reverse it. What was taken cannot be returned. What was lost cannot be found. The Stone creates. It does not uncreate.”

“Then what good is it?”

“It’s good for understanding. For knowing the truth about alchemy — about the law, about the cost, about the relationship between what we give and what we receive. And understanding, in my experience, is the only transmutation that doesn’t require a circle.”

He turned. Walked toward the staircase, his glass legs chiming with each step, his body catching the light and scattering it across the walls in prismatic patterns that were beautiful and terrible and the most honest thing Khalil had seen since he entered the ruins.

A man made of glass. Walking through a city of glass. Carrying the formula for the thing that had made them both.

Khalil looked at Zahra. She looked at him. The coffee eyes and the desperate eyes met across the glass table, across the scroll that contained the most dangerous knowledge in the world, across the distance between a woman who sought understanding and a man who sought his sister.

“Dawn,” Zahra said.

“Dawn,” Khalil agreed.

The glass city sang them to sleep that night — or to something that resembled sleep, the way glass resembled the things it had once been. Close enough. Functional enough. Not the thing itself, but a reasonable exchange.
