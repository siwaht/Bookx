

Chapter Twenty-One: The Allies


The afternoon of the third day was the longest afternoon of Naram’s life.


He spent it in the scriptorium, writing about Hasan the potter, because the forgery that said he was ill had expired and he needed to be visible, normal, unremarkable. A scribe at his desk. A junior doing his work. Nothing to see. Nothing to investigate. Nothing to justify an early execution.


He wrote about Hasan’s kiln. The temperature required for earthenware — 900 degrees, maintained for six hours, the fire fed with dried dung and olive wood because coal was expensive and Hasan was, in his wife’s words, “tighter than a drum skin.” He wrote about the sound the kiln made when it was hot enough — a low, sustained hum, like a sleeping animal, like the Archive’s own breathing scaled down to the size of a clay oven. He wrote about the way Hasan’s hands moved when he shaped the clay — confident, unhurried, the hands of a man who had found the one thing in the world he was good at and had decided that one thing was enough.


The biography was a good one. Naram could feel it — the way a musician feels a melody that’s working, the way a cook feels a dish that’s coming together. Hasan’s life had a shape to it, a narrative arc that was satisfying without being dramatic, meaningful without being grandiose. Born in a village. Apprenticed to a potter. Married a woman who was smarter than him and knew it and loved him anyway. Had four children, three of whom survived. Built a kiln. Made pots. Sold pots. Made more pots. A life that would never be the subject of an epic, never be sung by bards, never be remembered by anyone except the people who had lived alongside it — and that, Naram was beginning to understand, was exactly what made it valuable. Not to Gilgamesh, who valued lives for their narrative connections, their ripple potential, their utility as fuel. But to the Archive itself. The real Archive. The awareness that had woken up when the texts were gathered and that valued truth not for what it could produce but for what it was.


Hasan’s life was true. Completely, boringly, beautifully true. And writing it — documenting the temperature of a kiln, the cost of olive wood, the particular way a potter’s thumb pressed into wet clay to form the lip of a bowl — felt like an act of resistance. A small one. A quiet one. The kind of resistance that no one notices and no one celebrates and that matters anyway, the way breathing matters, the way the heart’s beating matters, not because anyone is watching but because the alternative is stopping.


He wrote about Hasan’s wife, Layla. The way she stood in the doorway of the workshop and watched him work, her arms crossed, her expression the particular mixture of exasperation and affection that is the native language of long marriages. “You love that kiln more than you love me,” she said once, and Hasan had looked up from his wheel and said, “The kiln doesn’t criticize my glazing technique,” and Layla had thrown a rag at his head and they had both laughed, and the sound of their laughter had carried across the courtyard and into the street where a neighbor’s child was playing with a stick and a hoop and the afternoon was golden and ordinary and perfect.


Naram wrote this. The clay held. The Archive accepted every word. And Naram thought about the fact that he was writing a man’s life with one hand and planning to save five lives with the other, and the two activities — the documentation and the rebellion — were not contradictory but complementary, two expressions of the same belief: that lives mattered. That truth mattered. That the act of writing a person’s story with accuracy and care was not just a job but a covenant, a promise that the writer made to the subject: I see you. I record you. I will not let you be consumed.


The scriptorium hummed around him. Forty desks, forty scribes, forty styluses moving across forty tablets. The sound was the Archive’s heartbeat — not the breathing, which was the ventilation, but the heartbeat, the rhythmic pulse of documentation that kept the institution alive. The light came from crystal shafts that channeled sunlight from the surface — a system of mirrors and lenses that Enki maintained with the obsessive care of a man who understood that without light, the scribes couldn’t write, and without writing, the Archive couldn’t breathe, and without breathing, everything died. The light was golden in the morning, white at midday, amber in the afternoon, and at sunset it turned the color of blood, which was either a coincidence or a metaphor, and in the Archive, coincidences and metaphors were the same thing.


The smell of the scriptorium was ink and clay and sweat and the faint, persistent odor of lentils from the kitchen two corridors away, where Shamhat was preparing the evening meal with the grim determination of a woman who had been cooking for two hundred people for fifteen years and had opinions about the quality of the cumin supply that she shared with anyone who stood still long enough to listen. The lentil smell was comforting. It was the smell of normalcy, of routine, of a world that continued to function even when the foundations were rotting. Naram breathed it in and wrote about Hasan’s kiln and thought about the plan.


He wrote, and the clay held, and the Archive accepted every word, and Naram thought about the plan.


The plan had three phases.


Phase one: the smoke. Enki would rig the ventilation at sunset, filling the Great Hall with dense, choking smoke. This would create chaos — scribes coughing, stumbling, unable to see. The masters would be disoriented. The execution ceremony would be disrupted.


Phase two: the swap. In the chaos, Naram and his allies would find the execution books — the five biographies with the death sentences — and replace the execution pages with forgeries. Naram would write the forgeries. He had practiced all morning, in the ventilation shaft, writing false death records with Sarai coaching him through the technique. Each forgery cost him a memory. He had lost the taste of his first meal in the Archive. He had lost the sound of Enki’s painted stars being described to him by a senior scribe whose name he could no longer remember. He had lost the feeling of holding a stylus for the first time — the weight of it, the balance, the thrill of potential.


Small losses. Necessary losses. The currency of survival.


Phase three: the escape. After the swap, the five scribes would disappear into the Unwritten’s network — the hidden corridors, the forgotten chambers, the margins of the Archive where the system couldn’t see. They would hide until the forgeries were accepted, until the masters believed the executions had already occurred, until the immediate danger had passed.


And then — phase four. The phase they hadn’t planned yet. The phase that involved stopping Gilgamesh, saving twenty-three lives, and preventing the birth of a god.


But first: sunset.


Naram set down his stylus and flexed his fingers. The joints ached — the particular ache of a hand that has been writing for hours, the tendons stiff, the knuckles swollen, the fingertips calloused from the pressure of the stylus against the clay. He had been writing about Hasan for six hours. Six hours of earthenware temperatures and kiln maintenance and the domestic negotiations of a potter and his wife. Six hours of truth, of honest documentation, of the kind of writing that the Archive was designed for and that Gilgamesh had perverted.


He looked at the tablet in front of him. Hasan’s biography was growing — not being completed, not being rushed toward its conclusion, but growing the way a real biography grows, one day at a time, one detail at a time, the slow accumulation of a life being lived. Hasan was not on the list. Hasan was not one of the twenty-three. Hasan was safe — for now — because his life was ordinary and his narrative connections were few and his death, when it came, would generate only a small ripple in the Archive’s systems, not enough to be worth harvesting.


The ordinariness of Hasan’s life was his protection. The same ordinariness that made his biography boring to write was the thing that kept him alive. Gilgamesh harvested the connected, the significant, the people whose deaths would ripple through dozens of other biographies. He did not harvest potters. He did not harvest the ordinary. The ordinary was beneath his notice, beneath his ambition, beneath the narrative needs of a man who was trying to write himself into godhood.


There was a lesson in that, Naram thought. A lesson about the value of being unremarkable. About the safety of smallness. About the particular, paradoxical power of a life that no one noticed and no one remembered and no one wrote about except a junior scribe in a sentient library who was using the biography of a potter as cover for a revolution.




Inanna found him in the scriptorium.


He hadn’t expected her. She was a senior scribe — one of the few who had earned the rank through skill rather than seniority, a woman in her forties with the sharp, angular face of someone who had spent her life cutting through nonsense and had developed a face to match. She sat down at the desk next to his — the one with the good leg, the one Naram had coveted for two years — and began writing, and without looking up, she said:


“I know what you’re planning.”


Naram’s stylus stopped. His heart stopped. His breathing stopped. Everything stopped except the Archive, which continued to breathe around them with the indifference of something that had been breathing for three thousand years and would continue breathing long after everyone currently alive was dead.


“I don’t know what you mean,” he said.


“You’re a terrible liar. Which is ironic, given that you’ve apparently learned to forge.” She still wasn’t looking at him. Her stylus moved across her tablet with the fluid precision of a senior scribe — no hesitation, no correction, every stroke exactly where it needed to be. “I’ve been protecting people like you for years. Scribes who discovered the truth. Scribes whose biographies were being completed. I forge their deaths — ‘transferred to another archive,’ ‘retired to the countryside,’ ‘died of natural causes.’ I write the forgeries and the Archive accepts them and the scribes disappear and the masters don’t ask questions because the paperwork is in order and the Archive’s systems confirm the deaths.”


He studied her while she spoke. Her hands were the most remarkable thing about her — not beautiful, not graceful, but precise. The hands of a surgeon or a watchmaker or a woman who had learned that the difference between a forgery that held for three days and a forgery that held for three years was in the pressure of the stylus, the angle of the wrist, the specific, almost imperceptible variations in stroke width that the Archive used to distinguish authentic text from imitation. Her hands had learned to lie at a level of detail that her mouth never needed to. Every stroke was a performance. Every word she wrote was simultaneously true — because she was writing a real biography, a real person’s real life — and false, because the act of writing it was a cover for the conversation she was having with Naram, and the Archive, which could read text but not intention, saw only the truth and missed the lie.


It was, Naram thought, the most elegant piece of deception he had ever witnessed. She was forging in plain sight. Not the content — the context. She was writing truth and using it as camouflage, the way a chameleon uses its environment, the way a spy uses a crowd. The Archive saw a senior scribe writing a biography. The Archive did not see a senior scribe recruiting a junior scribe for a rebellion. Because the Archive, for all its intelligence, for all its three-thousand-year-old awareness, could not read between the lines. It could read the lines themselves — every word, every stroke, every dot of ink — but the spaces between the lines, the silences, the things that were meant but not written, were invisible to it. The Archive was literate but not literary. It understood text but not subtext. And Inanna had built her entire resistance on that gap.


The scriptorium continued its rhythms around them. Two desks away, a scribe named Mushezib was writing about a date farmer in Basra, his stylus moving with the slow, deliberate pace of a man who had been assigned a boring subject and was determined to make it even more boring through sheer force of thoroughness. Behind them, the apprentice who had dropped the tablets earlier was now reorganizing them with the exaggerated care of someone trying to prove that the earlier incident was an anomaly and not a character trait. The duty master had moved to the far end of the room, where he was having a whispered conversation with another master — Tabni’s assistant, a thin man with the perpetually worried expression of someone whose job was to worry on behalf of someone who never worried about anything.


None of them were looking at Naram and Inanna. None of them heard the conversation. The scriptorium was a place of parallel solitudes — forty people working in the same room, each one sealed inside the bubble of their own biography, their own subject, their own small world of documentation. You could plan a revolution at your desk and the scribe next to you wouldn’t notice, because the scribe next to you was writing about a date farmer and the date farmer’s irrigation schedule was, at this moment, the most important thing in the world.


“How many?”


“Thirty-seven. Over the past eight years. Thirty-seven scribes who should be dead and aren’t, because I wrote their deaths before Gilgamesh could.” She set down her stylus and looked at him. Her eyes were not clear like Sarai’s — they were full, overfull, the eyes of a woman carrying thirty-seven secrets and the weight of every memory she’d sacrificed to keep them. “I’m good at this, Naram. Better than you. Better than your Unwritten friend. I’ve been doing it longer and I’ve paid more. I can write a forgery that holds for weeks. Months. I once wrote a death that the Archive accepted for three years before the deeper systems caught the discrepancy.”


“Why are you telling me this?”


“Because your plan is going to fail.”


The words landed like stones. Naram stared at her. She stared back. No apology. No softening. The directness of a woman who had learned that kindness and honesty were sometimes incompatible and had chosen honesty.


“The smoke will work,” she said. “Enki is good. The chaos will work. People are predictable in chaos. But the forgeries — your forgeries — won’t hold. Not long enough. You’ve been practicing for two days. I’ve been practicing for eight years. And even my forgeries fail eventually. The Archive is too smart. Too thorough. Too — hungry. It wants truth. It needs truth. And forgeries, no matter how good, are not truth. They’re approximations. And the Archive can tell the difference.”


“Then what do we do?”


“We don’t forge the deaths. We forge the lives. We don’t write ‘Naram was executed three years ago.’ We write ‘Naram was never a scribe. Naram never lived in the Archive. Naram does not exist.’ We don’t fake a death. We fake an absence. We write you out of the story entirely.”


“That’s — that’s what the Unwritten do.”


“That’s what the Unwritten do by erasing themselves. I’m proposing we do it by writing. Not erasure — rewriting. We don’t remove you from the Archive’s records. We replace you. We write a version of your biography that doesn’t include the heresy, doesn’t include the investigation, doesn’t include anything that Gilgamesh wants to punish. We write a Naram who is boring. Unremarkable. A scribe who writes about potters and eats lentils and has never, in his entire life, done anything worth executing.”


“The Archive will know it’s a lie.”


“The Archive will know it’s a lie if you know it’s a lie. That’s the principle. The forgery works when the writer believes it. So you have to believe it. You have to — for the duration of the forgery — genuinely believe that you are boring. That you never investigated. That you never found the truth. You have to forget, temporarily, everything you’ve learned in the past three days.”


“And the cost?”


“The cost is the memories themselves. The investigation. The discovery. The truth. You forget it — really forget it, not pretend to forget it — and the forgery holds. And when the forgery expires, when the Archive’s deeper systems catch up, the memories come back. If you’re lucky. If the forgery was clean. If the Archive doesn’t decide to keep them.”


“If.”


“If. There are no guarantees. There never are. But it’s better than your current plan, which is to write fake death records in the middle of a smoke-filled hall while a three-hundred-year-old dead man tries to kill you with narrative.”


She had a point.


“Join us,” Naram said. “Tonight. At sunset. Help us.”


Inanna picked up her stylus. She returned to her tablet. She wrote three words — Naram couldn’t see what they were — and the clay held, and she set the stylus down, and she looked at him with the full, heavy eyes of a woman who had been fighting alone for eight years and was tired of it.


“I’ll be there,” she said. “Now go back to your potter. You’re attracting attention.”


Naram went back to his potter. He wrote about Hasan’s kiln. He wrote about the temperature of earthenware. He wrote about a man who had found the one thing he was good at and had decided it was enough.


And he thought about sunset. And forgeries. And the cost of forgetting the truth in order to survive it.




End of Chapter Twenty-One
