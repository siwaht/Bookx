

Chapter Three: The Empty Shelf


Naram did not go back to sleep. He did not go back to the refectory. He went to the stacks.


Not the Lower Registry, where the source files lived in their cedar shelves. Not the active scriptorium, where the current biographies were kept under lock and the watchful eye of whatever duty master had drawn the short straw. He went to the place every scribe knew about and no scribe visited without reason: the Catalog of the Living.


The Catalog occupied an entire wing of the Archive’s middle level — a long, high-ceilinged hall lined with shelves that stretched from floor to the vaulted ceiling, accessible only by rolling ladders that moved on brass tracks set into the stone floor. The shelves held books. Not tablets, not scrolls — books, bound in leather the color of old blood, with spines stamped in gold leaf that had faded to a dull amber. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Each one the biography of a living person.


The biographies of the dead were kept elsewhere — in the deep stacks, sealed and silent, their stories complete. The Catalog of the Living was different. These books were open. Not physically — the covers were closed, the pages bound — but metaphysically. They were being written. Continuously. By the Archive itself.


This was the part of Onomancy that the junior scribes learned last, because it was the part that frightened people. The biographies that Naram and his colleagues wrote — the careful, researched, source-documented biographies of merchants and soldiers and priests — were not the Archive’s primary work. They were supplements. Additions. The Archive wrote its own biographies, constantly, automatically, for every living person within its range. The Blank Books, they were called, though they were anything but blank. They filled themselves with the lives of the living, page by page, hour by hour, a vast and silent documentation of every breath, every choice, every lie and truth and moment of grace and failure.


The scribes’ work was to refine. To add context, nuance, the kind of detail that only human observation could provide. The Archive saw everything, but it saw without judgment, without interpretation. It recorded that a man gave money to a beggar. It did not record whether the giving was generous or performative. That was the scribe’s job. To add the adjectives. To make the record not just accurate but 
true.


Or so they were taught.


Naram moved through the Catalog with his lamp held high, reading spines. The books were organized by region, then by city, then alphabetically. He wasn’t looking for Khalid al-Rashid. He was looking for himself.


He found it on the third shelf from the end, in the Damascus section, between al-Qasim and al-Sabbah. A book like any other — blood-red leather, gold-leaf spine, his name stamped in characters that were neither Arabic nor cuneiform but something the Archive had invented for itself, a script that combined elements of every writing system it had ever consumed.


NARAM. SCRIBE. ARCHIVE.


He pulled it from the shelf. It was warm. Not lamp-warm or stone-warm. Body-warm. The warmth of something alive, something with a pulse, something that knew it was being held.


He opened it.


The pages were covered in the Archive’s own hand — small, precise, inhuman in its regularity. Every letter identical to every other letter. No variation, no personality, no tremor of a living hand. Machine-writing, except there was no machine. Just the Archive, writing itself into the lives of the living with the patience of something that had never known urgency.


He read.


Naram, son of Adad-shuma and Ishtar-belet, born in the Archive, year of the flooding of the eastern shaft. Raised in the apprentice quarters. Mother died when the subject was twelve. Father unknown — the mother never spoke of him, and the Archive’s records of the conception are incomplete, suggesting the event occurred outside the Archive’s range.


He skipped ahead. Childhood. Training. The first time he held a stylus. The first biography he wrote — a fisherman from the Tigris, a simple man with a simple life, and Naram had sweated over every word as if the fate of the world depended on getting the man’s relationship with his nets exactly right. The fisherman had died two years later. Naram had attended the sealing of his biography — the ceremony where a completed book was closed, bound, and carried to the deep stacks. He had cried. He hadn’t known why. He still didn’t.


He skipped further. Recent entries. Yesterday.


The subject attempted to write a false virtue assessment for Khalid al-Rashid, merchant of Damascus. The entry was rejected. The subject lost the memory of his mother’s weaving song (melody in the Phrygian mode, lyrics concerning the passage of seasons, learned from the subject’s maternal grandmother). The subject did not report the rejection. The subject investigated the source files in the Lower Registry after curfew. The subject discovered discrepancies in fourteen files, three of which contain deliberate falsifications written in Ink of First Blood.


Naram’s hands were shaking. Not from fear — from the vertigo of seeing your own life written by something that was not you, in a hand that was not human, with a precision that left no room for the comfortable fictions you tell yourself about who you are and why you do what you do.


The Archive knew he had investigated. Of course it did. The Archive knew everything. It had watched him creep through the stacks after curfew, read the files, discover the lies. It had watched and written and said nothing.


He turned the page.


And stopped.


The next entry was not in the Archive’s hand. It was in a different hand entirely — human, old, beautiful, written in ink that caught the lamplight with a reddish sheen. The same hand he had seen in the falsified files. The same ink.


Someone had written in his book. Someone who was not the Archive.


He read the entry.


On the third day, Naram will be executed for heresy.


The words were simple. Seven words. No elaboration, no context, no explanation. Just a sentence, written in blood-ink, in a hand that belonged to someone who should not have been able to write in a Blank Book at all — because the Blank Books wrote themselves, and no human hand had ever added to them, and the very idea of a person writing in the Archive’s own biography was not just forbidden but supposedly impossible.


And yet. Seven words. Clear as a death warrant. Precise as a scribe’s hand.


On the third day, Naram will be executed for heresy.


Today was the first day.




He found Utu in the ventilation shaft above the scriptorium.


This required explanation. Utu had discovered, during his second year, that the ventilation shafts — Enki’s pride, a network of stone channels that circulated air through the entire Archive — were large enough to crawl through if you were thin and didn’t mind the dark and the occasional rat. Utu was thin. Utu didn’t mind anything. Utu had mapped the entire ventilation network over the course of three months, using a system of chalk marks and string that he claimed was based on Ariadne’s thread and that Naram suspected was based on Utu’s inability to sit still for more than ten minutes at a time.


The shaft above the scriptorium was Utu’s thinking place. He went there when he needed to be alone, which was often, because Utu was the kind of person who loved people in theory and found them exhausting in practice.


Naram climbed up through the access panel — a loose stone that Utu had pried free years ago and replaced with a convincing fake — and crawled along the shaft until he found Utu sitting cross-legged in a wider section, eating dried dates from a pouch he kept hidden behind a ventilation baffle.


“You look terrible,” Utu said.


“Someone is writing my death.”


Utu stopped chewing. In the dim light that filtered up from the scriptorium below — the morning shift had started; Naram could hear the scratch of styluses, the murmur of scribes consulting references — Utu’s face went through several expressions in rapid succession: disbelief, calculation, fear, and finally the particular blank calm that Utu adopted when the situation was too serious for his usual deflections.


“Explain,” he said.


Naram explained. The Catalog. His book. The entry in blood-ink. The seven words. Utu listened without interrupting — again — which confirmed that the situation was, in fact, as bad as Naram thought.


“You’re sure it said ‘heresy,’” Utu said when Naram finished.


“I’m sure.”


“Not ‘error.’ Not ‘misconduct.’ Heresy.”


“Heresy.”


Utu ate a date. Slowly. The way a man eats when he is using the act of chewing to buy time for thinking.


“Heresy is a capital offense,” he said. “The only capital offense. You can steal from the Archive — they’ll break your hands, but they won’t kill you. You can forge an entry — copying room for life. You can even destroy a book, though the punishment for that is — well. You know.”


Naram knew. The punishment for destroying a book was to become one. Your biography was written to completion — every detail, every memory, every moment of your life documented with perfect accuracy — and then you were sealed. Not killed. Sealed. Your body placed in a chamber in the deep stacks, your biography bound and shelved beside you, and the Archive would keep you alive — technically alive, breathing, heart beating — for as long as the book endured. Which was forever. The Archive’s books did not decay.


It was not death. It was worse than death. It was immortality without consciousness, existence without experience, a life reduced to text and preserved in the dark like a specimen in a jar.


“But heresy,” Utu continued. “Heresy is different. Heresy means you’ve challenged the Archive’s authority. Its right to document. Its right to know. And the punishment for heresy is execution. Real execution. They write your death, and the death happens. The Archive makes it true.”


“How?”


“The same way it makes everything true. It writes it. A senior scribe — or a Master — writes your death into your biography, and because the biography is accurate, because the Archive’s power depends on accuracy, the death becomes real. Your heart stops. Your lungs stop. You die, and the biography records the death, and the book is sealed, and that’s the end.”


“That’s murder.”


“That’s Onomancy. The power of the written word. The same power that lets us guide people toward virtue, that lets us document truth, that lets the Archive function at all. The same power, pointed in the other direction.” Utu’s voice was flat. Not angry. Not frightened. Flat, the way a voice goes when the person speaking has thought about something terrible for a long time and has run out of emotional responses. “It’s been done before. Not often. The last execution was — I don’t know. Before our time. But the mechanism exists. The precedent exists. And someone has written it into your book.”


“In blood-ink.”


“In blood-ink. Which means Ink of First Blood. Which means someone with access to the Restricted Section. Which means a Master.”


They sat in the ventilation shaft, in the dark, with the sound of the scriptorium below them — the ordinary sounds of an ordinary morning, scribes writing, apprentices running, someone arguing about desk assignments — and the sound of the Archive breathing around them, and the knowledge that somewhere in the stacks, in a book with Naram’s name on the spine, seven words were waiting to come true.


“I can’t destroy the book,” Naram said. “The punishment—”


“Is worse than death. Yes.”


“I can’t alter the entry. The Archive would know.”


“The Archive knows everything. Yes.”


“So what do I do?”


Utu reached into his pouch and offered Naram a date. Naram took it. It was old and dry and too sweet, the kind of date that had been sitting in a ventilation shaft for longer than was advisable. He ate it anyway. It tasted like dust and sugar and the particular flavor of desperation.


“You find out who wrote it,” Utu said. “And you find out why. And you do it in three days. Because on the third day—”


“I know what happens on the third day.”


“Then you’d better start now.”




The first thing Naram needed to understand was the handwriting.


He had seen it twice now — in the falsified files in the Lower Registry and in his own biography. The same hand. Old, precise, beautiful. Written in Ink of First Blood, which was kept in the Restricted Section under lock and ward and the personal supervision of Master Tabni, who guarded the Restricted Section the way a dragon guards gold: with paranoid devotion and an absolute unwillingness to share.


The Ink of First Blood was the most powerful tool in the Archive’s arsenal. Made from the umbilical cord blood of the biography’s subject — collected at birth, preserved in cedar vials, stored in the Restricted Section alongside the other materials too dangerous for general use — it granted perfect accuracy. A biography written in First Blood could not be wrong. The ink itself was truth, distilled from the first moment of a life, carrying the essence of the person it described.


Which meant that whoever had written in Naram’s book had access to Naram’s First Blood. His umbilical cord blood, collected at his birth in the Archive nineteen years ago, stored somewhere in the Restricted Section in a vial with his name on it.


And they had used it to write his death.


The thought made his skin crawl. Not the death — though that was bad enough — but the intimacy of it. Someone had held a vial of his blood. Had dipped a stylus into it. Had written words that would kill him, using the substance of his own beginning. It was not just murder. It was a violation. A theft of something so personal it didn’t have a name.


He needed to see the Restricted Section. He needed to know who had accessed his First Blood. And he needed to do it without alerting Tabni, who would ask questions, which would lead to attention, which would lead to the masters, who — if the conspiracy was as deep as Naram suspected — were the ones trying to kill him.


He needed help.


He needed someone who could move through the Archive unseen. Someone the Archive couldn’t track. Someone who didn’t exist in the Catalog of the Living.


He needed the Unwritten.




End of Chapter Three
