

Chapter Seventeen: The Cost


The memories left like birds from a tree — one by one at first, then in flocks.


Naram sat in Sarai’s chamber, practicing forgeries, and with each lie that the Archive accepted, a piece of his past departed. Not violently. Not painfully. The memories didn’t tear or rip or shatter. They simply — left. One moment they were there, solid and present and real, and the next moment they were gone, and the space where they had been was not empty but smooth, like a wall where a painting has been removed. You could see the outline. You could see where the painting had hung. But the painting itself was gone, and no amount of staring at the outline would bring it back.


The first to go were the small ones. The color of his apprentice robe — already gone, sacrificed in the first practice session. Then the taste of his first meal in the Archive. Then the sound of rain on the ventilation shafts. Then the name of the senior scribe who had taught him to hold a stylus — a man whose face Naram could still see but whose name had been replaced by a blank, a silence, a space where a word used to live.


Then the medium ones. The feeling of his mother’s hand on his forehead when he was sick — not the knowledge that she had done it, which remained, but the sensation itself, the specific warmth and pressure of her palm against his skin. Gone. The smell of the ink he had used for his first biography — the particular blend of lampblack and gum arabic that the Archive mixed for junior scribes, slightly different from the senior blend, slightly cheaper, slightly less permanent. Gone. The sound of Utu’s laugh — not the knowledge that Utu laughed, which remained, but the specific sound, the particular frequency and rhythm and warmth of it. Gone.


Each loss was small. Each loss was bearable. Each loss was, in isolation, the kind of thing you could shrug off, the kind of thing you could tell yourself didn’t matter, the kind of thing that was a fair price for the ability to fool a sentient library into believing a lie.


But the losses accumulated. And the accumulation was not arithmetic — it was geometric. Each loss made the next loss larger, because each loss removed a piece of the context that gave the remaining memories their meaning. The sound of Utu’s laugh was gone, and without the sound, the memory of the jokes that had caused the laugh lost their flavor. The feeling of his mother’s hand was gone, and without the feeling, the memory of being sick as a child lost its comfort. The name of the senior scribe was gone, and without the name, the memory of learning to write lost its specificity, became generic, became a fact without a face.


He was becoming — thinner. Not physically. Experientially. The richness of his past was being diluted, the vivid colors fading to pastels, the sharp edges softening to curves. He was still Naram. He still knew who he was, where he was, what he was doing and why. But the Naram he was becoming was a simpler Naram. A Naram with fewer reference points. A Naram whose past was a sketch rather than a painting, an outline rather than a portrait.


“Stop,” Sarai said.


He looked up. He had been writing — another forgery, another lie, another small theft from his own history. The tablet before him read: 
Naram has not left the scriptorium today.
 The clay held. The Archive believed it. And somewhere in his mind, a memory departed — the specific feeling of the stone floor beneath his feet in the Lower Registry, the night he had gone looking for Khalid’s file. The feeling was gone. The knowledge remained. But the knowledge without the feeling was like a map without terrain — accurate but flat.


“You’re losing too much,” Sarai said. “I can see it. Your eyes are changing.”


“My eyes?”


“They’re getting clearer. Not like ours — not the full clarity of the Unwritten. But clearer. The opacity that comes from having a full past, a full set of memories, a full life behind your eyes — it’s thinning. You’re becoming — translucent.”


He touched his face. His fingers found his eyes — closed, the lids smooth, the lashes familiar. He couldn’t see the change she described. But he could feel it. The thinning. The lightening. The slow, incremental loss of weight that came from carrying fewer memories, fewer experiences, fewer moments of being alive.


“How much have I lost?” he asked.


“I can’t measure it. No one can. The erasure doesn’t come with a receipt. But I can tell you what I see. You’ve lost — texture. The small details that make a person specific. The things that make you Naram rather than any other nineteen-year-old scribe. You’re still you. But you’re a less detailed you. A lower-resolution you. And if you keep going—”


“I’ll become like you.”


“You’ll become like us. Clear-eyed. Empty-shelved. Present but thin. Alive but — reduced.” She paused. “There’s a threshold. I don’t know exactly where it is — it’s different for everyone. But there’s a point where the losses stop being bearable and start being structural. Where you lose not just memories but the capacity for memory. Where the shelves don’t just empty — they collapse. And once they collapse, they don’t rebuild. The architecture of your mind changes. The rooms where memories used to live close their doors and lock them and the keys dissolve and the rooms become — nothing. Spaces that used to be something and are now just spaces.”


“How close am I?”


“I don’t know. But you’re closer than you were this morning. And this morning you were closer than you were yesterday. The trajectory is — concerning.”


Naram set down the stylus. He looked at the tablet — the forgery, the lie, the small theft that had cost him the feeling of stone beneath his feet. He looked at his hands — ink-stained, trembling slightly, the hands of a scribe who had spent the day lying to a sentient library and paying for each lie with a piece of himself.


“I need to be able to forge,” he said. “The plan depends on it. If I can’t write lies that the Archive accepts—”


“Then we find another way. There is always another way. The question is whether the other way is better or worse than the way that’s eating you alive.”


“What other way?”


Sarai was quiet. The blue-white lamp burned. The bare walls of her chamber reflected the light in patterns that shifted as the flame moved — patterns that looked, if you squinted, like text. Like the Archive’s own writing, projected onto stone by a lamp that burned without fuel and a flame that burned without heat.


“Inanna,” Sarai said.


“Who?”


“A scribe. Senior. She’s been doing what you’re trying to do — forging biographies, fooling the Archive — for years. Longer than you’ve been alive. She’s better at it than you’ll ever be, because she’s had more practice and she’s paid more cost and she’s learned to manage the loss in ways that you haven’t had time to learn.”


“How do you know about her?”


“Because she’s been saving people. Scribes whose biographies were being completed. She forges their deaths — writes them out of the system, makes the Archive believe they’re dead when they’re alive. She’s been doing it for eight years. Thirty-seven scribes. Thirty-seven lives saved. And she’s done it alone, without allies, without the Unwritten, without anyone to share the weight.”


“Why didn’t you tell me about her before?”


“Because I wasn’t sure she was real. The Unwritten hear rumors. We live in the margins — we see things, hear things, notice things that the documented population misses. But we can’t always verify. We can’t check the records because we can’t access the records. We can’t ask questions because we can’t be seen. Inanna was a rumor. A whisper. A story that the margins told about a woman in the text who was fighting the same fight we were fighting, from the other side of the page.”


“And now?”


“And now I’m sure. Because you’ve been practicing forgery for a day and you’ve already lost — how much? A dozen memories? Two dozen? Inanna has been doing this for eight years. She’s lost more than you can imagine. And she’s still functioning. Still writing. Still saving people. Which means she’s found a way to manage the cost that you haven’t found. And we need that way. We need her.”


Naram thought about this. He thought about the memories he’d lost — the small ones, the medium ones, the pieces of his past that had departed like birds from a tree. He thought about the memories he still had — his mother’s face, Utu’s friendship, the feeling of the Archive breathing around him. He thought about how much more he could afford to lose before the losses became structural, before the shelves collapsed, before the rooms closed their doors.


Not much. That was the honest answer. Not much more.


“Find her,” he said. “Find Inanna. Bring her in.”


“I can’t find her. She’s in the text — in the documented world, in the Archive’s awareness. I can’t go there. I’m Unwritten. The moment I step into the inhabited levels, I’m invisible — which is usually an advantage, but in this case it means I can’t interact with documented people. I can’t tap her on the shoulder. I can’t leave her a note. I can’t—”


“I’ll find her.”


“You’ll find her. How?”


“The same way I found the falsified files. The same way I found the Catalog. The same way I found you. I’ll follow the evidence. If Inanna has been forging deaths for eight years, there will be traces. Discrepancies. Biographies that don’t quite add up. Deaths that the Archive accepted but that the Blank Books contradict. I’ll look for the seams. I’ll look for the places where the forgery shows.”


“And the cost? Every hour you spend in the Catalog, every biography you read, every investigation you conduct — the Archive is watching. The Archive is writing. And Gilgamesh is reading.”


“The death sentence protects me until sunset on the third day. Gilgamesh can’t kill me before then. He can make my life difficult. He can have Tabni ask questions. But he can’t stop me from looking.”


“He can stop you from finding.”


“Then I’ll look harder.”


Sarai studied him. The clear eyes, searching. Looking for the thinning she had described — the loss of opacity, the increasing translucence of a mind that was shedding its past like a snake shedding its skin.


“You’re stubborn,” she said.


“I’m a scribe. Stubbornness is a professional requirement. Have you ever tried to write a biography of a man whose most interesting quality is his relationship with his kiln?”


Despite everything — the losses, the thinning, the weight of twenty-three lives and a death sentence and a three-hundred-year-old enemy — Sarai almost laughed. The sound caught in her throat, unfamiliar, rusty, the sound of a mechanism that hadn’t been used in years and was surprised to find itself still functional.


“Go,” she said. “Find Inanna. And Naram—”


“Yes?”


“Stop practicing. No more forgeries. Not until we have a better way. The memories you have left — protect them. Guard them. They’re the most valuable thing you own. More valuable than the plan. More valuable than the investigation. More valuable than anything in the Archive’s collection. Because they’re yours. And in this place, the things that are truly yours are the things that matter most.”


He nodded. He left. He went back to the inhabited levels, back to the scriptorium, back to the world of ink and clay and the ordinary, extraordinary, irreplaceable work of writing other people’s lives.


And he did not practice forgery again that day. He wrote about Hasan the potter instead. He wrote about kilns and clay and the specific, beautiful, unremarkable art of making something useful from something ordinary. And the clay held. And the Archive accepted every word. And the memories he had left — his mother’s face, Utu’s friendship, the feeling of the Archive breathing around him — stayed where they were, on their shelves, in their rooms, behind their doors.


For now. For today. For the hours that remained.




End of Chapter Seventeen
