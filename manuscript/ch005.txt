

Chapter Four: The First Day


Three days. Seventy-two hours. Four thousand three hundred and twenty minutes, if you were the kind of person who counted, and Naram was becoming that kind of person very quickly.


He sat in the scriptorium at his assigned desk — third row, fourth from the left, the one with the wobbly leg that no one had fixed in the two years since Naram had first reported it — and pretended to work. The biography of Khalid al-Rashid had been reassigned. A note on his desk, in Tabni’s cramped handwriting, informed him that the al-Rashid file had been “escalated to senior review” and that Naram should begin work on a new subject: a potter from Mosul named Hasan, whose life, according to the intake documents, was so unremarkable that the most exciting thing that had ever happened to him was a kiln explosion in which no one was injured and only three pots were lost.


Naram wrote about Hasan’s pots. He wrote about the clay Hasan used — river clay from the Tigris, grey-brown, with a high calcium content that made it suitable for cooking vessels but not for decorative work. He wrote about Hasan’s technique — coil-built, not wheel-thrown, because Hasan’s father had been a coil-builder and Hasan’s father’s father had been a coil-builder and the wheel was, in Hasan’s opinion, “a shortcut for people who don’t respect the clay.” He wrote about Hasan’s wife, who sold the pots at market and who was, by all accounts, the actual business mind of the operation, a woman who could calculate profit margins in her head faster than most scribes could write them down.


He wrote, and the clay held, and the Archive accepted every word, because every word was true, and Hasan the potter was exactly as unremarkable as his file suggested, and the writing was easy and mindless and allowed Naram to think about other things while his hands moved.


Things like: how do you find people who don’t exist?


The Unwritten were not a secret. They were a rumor, which in the Archive was almost the same thing but carried less institutional weight. Every scribe had heard of them — people who had erased all records of themselves from the Archive’s systems. Not just their biographies. Everything. Birth records, intake forms, informant reports, the Blank Books’ automatic entries. All of it, gone. Wiped clean. As if they had never been born.


The result, according to the rumors, was threefold. First: invisibility. The Archive could not see what it could not document. An Unwritten person moved through the world like a ghost — present, physical, real, but invisible to the Archive’s awareness. The Blank Books could not write them. The scribes could not find them. They existed in the gaps between words, in the margins of the page.


Second: immortality. The Archive’s power over the living depended on documentation. It could guide, influence, even kill — but only if it could write the event into a biography. An Unwritten person had no biography. The Archive could not write their death because it could not write anything about them at all. They were, in the most literal sense, beyond its reach.


Third: madness. Because the erasure was not selective. You could not erase your Archive records and keep your own memories. The act of unwriting yourself from the Archive unwrote you from yourself. Your memories went with your records. Your past went with your documentation. You became a person without a history, without a childhood, without a name you could be sure was really yours. The Unwritten remembered the present — the current moment, the immediate past — but everything before the erasure was gone. A blank page where a life used to be.


Naram had always thought of the Unwritten as tragic figures. Desperate people who had chosen a terrible freedom over a terrible captivity. Now he thought of them as potential allies. People who could go where the Archive could not see. People who could enter the Restricted Section without Tabni’s knowledge, because Tabni’s knowledge depended on the Archive’s knowledge, and the Archive was blind to the Unwritten.


The problem was finding them.


You could not look for people who didn’t exist. You could not search records that had been erased. You could not ask the Archive to locate someone it couldn’t see. The Unwritten, by definition, were unfindable.


Unless they wanted to be found.




Naram found the first sign at lunch.


The refectory was crowded — the midday meal drew everyone, even the masters, because the alternative was going hungry until dinner, and the Archive did not tolerate hungry scribes any more than it tolerated lying ones. Naram sat at his usual table. Utu was absent — he had been assigned to the Baghdad intake desk, processing new files from the field agents who traveled the cities collecting information for biographies. It was tedious work, but it kept him away from the stacks, which was probably the point. Someone had noticed Utu asking questions about Khalid al-Rashid. Someone was keeping him busy.


Naram ate his lentils. Cumin again. He was beginning to suspect that the cook’s three-recipe rotation was not a limitation but a philosophy — a belief that variety was overrated and that consistency, even in its most monotonous form, was a kind of virtue. He respected this. He did not enjoy it, but he respected it.


He was halfway through his bowl when he noticed the mark.


It was on the table. Scratched into the stone with something sharp — a stylus tip, maybe, or a nail. Small enough to miss if you weren’t looking. A single character, in a script Naram didn’t recognize. Not cuneiform. Not Arabic. Not the Archive’s own writing. Something else. Something that looked like it had been drawn by someone who was remembering how to write and not quite getting it right — the lines uneven, the proportions wrong, the whole thing slightly off-kilter, like a word spoken by someone who has forgotten the language and is working from muscle memory alone.


He touched it. The stone was warm where the mark had been scratched. Not lamp-warm. Body-warm.


He looked around the refectory. Fifty people eating. Conversations, arguments, the clatter of bowls. Old Shamhat banging pots. An apprentice spilling water and being scolded by a senior scribe whose name Naram could never remember because the man was so aggressively unremarkable that his personality seemed to actively resist being noticed.


No one was watching him. No one was paying attention. The mark on the table was invisible to everyone except someone who was looking for it.


Or someone who had been told to look.


Enheduanna. She had said the Archive had chosen him. She had said she didn’t know why. But she had also sent him away without instructions, without guidance, without telling him what to do next. Which was either negligence or strategy. And Enheduanna did not do negligence.


She had pointed him toward the Catalog. She had told him the Archive was hungry. She had told him the Archive chose. And then she had let him go, knowing — because she knew everything, because she was two hundred years old and had not survived that long by being careless — that he would find his book, find the death sentence, and start looking for answers.


And the answers, it seemed, had been scratched into a refectory table by someone who didn’t exist.




He found the second mark in the corridor outside the scriptorium. The third on the wall of the staircase leading to the middle stacks. The fourth on the floor of the Catalog of the Living, near the shelf where his own book sat.


A trail. Breadcrumbs left by someone who couldn’t leave a note — because notes were writing, and writing was the Archive’s domain, and the Unwritten could not write without risking detection. But scratches were not writing. Scratches were damage. The Archive tracked words, not wounds. A scratch on a wall was invisible to a system designed to monitor text.


The marks led down. Past the Catalog. Past the middle stacks. Past the Lower Registry. Into corridors Naram had never seen — narrow, unlit, the stone rough and unfinished, as if the Archive had started carving these passages and then forgotten about them, or decided they were not worth completing, or — and this was the thought that made Naram’s skin prickle — as if the passages had carved themselves, the Archive growing new corridors the way a tree grows new roots, reaching into the dark for reasons only it understood.


The last mark was on a door. A real door this time — wood, old, bound with iron that had rusted to a deep orange. The mark was larger here, more confident, as if the person who had scratched it had been less afraid of being caught this far from the inhabited levels.


Naram pushed the door open.


The room beyond was small — barely larger than his sleeping quarters — and lit by a single lamp that burned with a blue-white flame Naram had never seen before. The walls were bare stone. The floor was bare stone. There was no furniture except a low table, on which sat a clay cup of tea that was still steaming.


And in the corner, sitting cross-legged on the floor with the stillness of someone who had been waiting for a very long time, was a woman.


She was — Naram’s first thought was 
old,
 but that wasn’t right. She was ageless in the way Enheduanna was ageless, but differently. Enheduanna’s agelessness was controlled, deliberate, a choice about how much age to display. This woman’s agelessness was accidental — the result of having lost so many memories that the markers of time had been erased along with everything else. Her face was smooth but not young. Her hair was dark but not black. Her eyes were — empty was the wrong word. Clear. Clear the way water is clear when there is nothing in it. No sediment. No color. No history.


“You followed the marks,” she said. Her voice was quiet. Not soft — quiet. The voice of someone who had learned that volume attracted attention and attention attracted the Archive and the Archive was the enemy.


“You left them for me.”


“I left them for whoever came looking. You came looking. So they were for you.” She gestured to the tea. “Drink. You look like you haven’t slept.”


“I haven’t.”


“Then drink. Tea helps. Not with the problem. With the feeling of having a problem. There’s a difference.”


Naram sat. He picked up the cup. The tea was hot and bitter and tasted of herbs he couldn’t identify — not cardamom, not mint, something wilder, something that grew in places where gardens didn’t reach. He drank. The woman watched him with her clear, empty eyes.


“My name is Sarai,” she said. The walls of the small room shivered — the faintest vibration, as if the Archive had heard a sound it couldn’t process. “Or it was. I’m not sure anymore. Names are the first thing you lose. They’re the most written thing about a person — on documents, on letters, on the lips of everyone who’s ever spoken to you. When you erase yourself from the Archive, the name goes first. I chose Sarai because it means ‘princess’ in a language I think I used to speak, and because it amused me to be a princess in a place where no one can see me.”


“You’re Unwritten.”


“I’m Unwritten. Have been for — I don’t know. Years. Many years. I can’t count them because I can’t remember most of them. I know I was a scribe. I know I worked in the Archive. I know I had a husband, and the husband was cruel, and I erased myself to escape him because the Archive would not let me leave and the husband would not let me live and the only door that was open was the door that led to nowhere.” She paused. “I don’t remember his face. I don’t remember his name. I remember the cruelty. The body remembers what the mind forgets. My hands flinch when I hear a man raise his voice. I don’t know why. But I know.”


Naram set down his tea. “I need your help.”


“I know. I’ve been watching you. Not through the Archive — the Archive can’t see me. Through my own eyes. The old-fashioned way. I saw you in the Lower Registry last night. I saw you in the Catalog this morning. I saw you find your book. I saw your face when you read what was written in it.”


“Then you know—”


“That someone has written your death. Yes. In Ink of First Blood. In a hand I recognize.”


Naram’s breath caught. “You recognize the handwriting?”


Sarai’s clear eyes held his. In the blue-white lamplight, they looked like water over stone — transparent, depthless, hiding nothing because there was nothing left to hide.


“The hand belongs to Master Gilgamesh,” she said. “The founder of the Archive. The first scribe. The man who discovered that writing could capture the essence of a life and who built this place to house that discovery.”


“Gilgamesh is dead. He’s been dead for three hundred years.”


“Gilgamesh is dead,” Sarai agreed. “His tomb is in the deep Archive, sealed and guarded. I attended the sealing. I think. The memory is — thin. Like looking at something through water. But I remember the tomb. I remember the ceremony. I remember the masters saying he was gone.”


“Then how—”


“The handwriting in your book is his. I am certain of this. I spent years — before I was Unwritten, when I was still a scribe — studying the old texts. Gilgamesh’s hand is unmistakable. No one else writes like that. No one else has ever written like that. The precision, the beauty, the absolute control — it is the hand of a man who has written more words than any human being who has ever lived, and who has paid for every one of them in memory, and who has somehow continued writing despite having nothing left to remember.”


She picked up her own cup of tea — Naram hadn’t noticed a second cup — and drank.


“Gilgamesh is dead,” she said again. “And Gilgamesh is writing in your book. Both of these things are true. And if both are true, then something in the Archive is very, very wrong.”


The lamp flickered. The blue-white flame danced, casting shadows that moved like words across the bare stone walls. Somewhere above them — far above, in the inhabited levels where scribes wrote and apprentices ran and the cook banged her pots — the Archive breathed. And down here, in a room that didn’t exist on any map, a woman who didn’t exist in any record poured more tea and waited for Naram to understand.


He was beginning to.




End of Chapter Four
