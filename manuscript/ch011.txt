

Chapter Ten: The Second Day


Naram slept for two hours and woke with the taste of ash in his mouth.


Not real ash. Dream ash. He had been dreaming of the rejected biography — the clay turning to powder, the words dissolving — except in the dream, it wasn’t the clay that turned to ash. It was him. His hands first, then his arms, then his chest, his face, his eyes. He dissolved into white powder and the powder settled on a page and the page was his biography and someone was reading it, and the someone had no face, only hands — beautiful, precise, ink-stained hands that turned the pages with the patience of something that had been reading for three hundred years.


He sat up on his cot. The apprentice quarters were dark and quiet — the other junior scribes asleep, their breathing a soft, irregular chorus. Someone was snoring. Someone else was talking in their sleep, muttering about column widths and margin ratios, the anxieties of the profession leaking into dreams.


Two days left. Less than two. The execution was scheduled for sunset on the third day, and the first day was gone, spent on discovery and horror and a bowl of lamb stew in a cave full of people who didn’t exist.


He needed to think. He needed to plan. He needed to do something other than lie on a narrow cot in the dark and count the hours until someone wrote the last line of his life.


He got up. He dressed. He went to the refectory.


It was empty — too early for breakfast, too late for the night-shift scribes who worked the Blank Books during the dark hours, monitoring the automatic biographies for anomalies. The kitchen was closed, but old Shamhat had left a pot of tea on the stove, still warm, because Shamhat understood that the Archive never truly slept and that someone, at any hour, would need tea. Naram poured a cup. He sat at his usual table — third from the wall, near the kitchen door — and he thought.


What he knew:


Gilgamesh was alive. Or not alive — post-alive. He had transcended the body and become text, a consciousness distributed across hundreds of books, writing continuously, sustaining himself through the act of writing the way a fire sustains itself through the act of burning. He had been doing this for three hundred years. The Archive’s leadership knew. They allowed it — perhaps encouraged it — because Gilgamesh’s continuous writing powered the Archive. His ceaseless biographical output fed the Archive’s hunger, kept it strong, kept it aware. Without Gilgamesh, the Archive might weaken. Might sleep. Might die.


And Gilgamesh needed sacrifices. Lives written to completion — biographies finished, sealed, the subjects dead — to fuel his own continuous existence. He was a fire that burned lives for fuel. And Naram was kindling.


What he didn’t know:


Why him. Why now. Why a junior scribe who had accidentally proven that a Damascus merchant was dishonest. There were easier targets — people outside the Archive, people whose deaths would attract no attention, people who didn’t have friends like Utu or allies like Sarai. Why choose a scribe? Why choose someone inside the institution?


Unless the choice wasn’t about convenience. Unless it was about something else. Something specific to Naram.


He thought about Enheduanna’s words. 
The Archive chose you.
 Not Gilgamesh. The Archive. As if the Archive and Gilgamesh were different things — which they were, or had been, or might still be. Gilgamesh had merged with the Archive, but merging was not the same as becoming. A river that merges with the sea is still, in some sense, a river. Its water is still its water. Its current is still its current. It has not become the sea. It has joined it.


So: the Archive was one thing. Gilgamesh was another. They shared a body — the physical structure of the library, the texts, the shelves, the breathing stone — but they were not the same. And if the Archive had chosen Naram, it had chosen him for a reason that was the Archive’s, not Gilgamesh’s.


The Archive had rejected his lie about Khalid. The Archive had shown him the truth. The Archive had, in its own way, pointed him toward the conspiracy.


Was the Archive trying to help him?


The thought was absurd. The Archive was not a person. It did not have intentions. It did not help or hinder. It knew. That was all. It knew truth from lies, and it acted on that knowledge automatically, the way a body acts on the knowledge that fire is hot — not through decision but through reflex.


But reflexes could be useful. A body that flinches from fire is not trying to help itself. It is simply responding to stimulus. And yet the flinch saves the hand.


Maybe the Archive’s rejection of Naram’s entry was a flinch. A reflex. The Archive tasting a lie and spitting it out. And maybe the flinch, like all flinches, was a signal. A warning. 
Something is wrong. Pay attention.


Naram was paying attention now.




Utu found him in the refectory an hour later, when the first breakfast shift began and the room filled with the sounds of bowls and spoons and the resigned sighs of people confronting lentils at dawn.


“You look worse than yesterday,” Utu said, sitting down with his bowl. “Which is impressive, because yesterday you looked like someone had told you the world was ending.”


“Someone did tell me the world was ending. My world, specifically.”


“Right. The death sentence. I was hoping you’d tell me it was a bad dream.”


“It wasn’t a bad dream.”


“I was hoping.” Utu ate a spoonful of lentils. Garlic again. The cook had broken her rotation, which was either a sign of creative growth or a sign that she’d run out of cumin. “So. What’s the plan?”


Naram told him. Not everything — not the Unwritten, not Sarai, not the tomb. Those were secrets that belonged to people who had paid too much for their privacy to have it shared over breakfast. But he told Utu about the Restricted Section, the empty vial, the ash in the registry. He told him about Gilgamesh — not the full truth, but enough. That someone was using Gilgamesh’s techniques. That someone had access to First Blood. That the conspiracy went higher than a single rogue scribe.


Utu listened. He ate his lentils. He did not interrupt.


“You need to find the other victims,” he said when Naram finished.


“What other victims?”


“You said the tomb had hundreds of books. All open. All being written in. That’s hundreds of biographies being completed simultaneously. Hundreds of lives being written to their ends. You’re not the only one, Naram. You’re just the one who noticed.”


The thought hit Naram like a physical blow. He had been so focused on his own death sentence — the seven words, the blood-ink, the three-day countdown — that he hadn’t considered the scale. Hundreds of books. Hundreds of lives. Hundreds of people walking through their days, eating their lentils, doing their work, not knowing that somewhere in the deep Archive, a dead man was writing the last pages of their stories.


“How do I find them?”


“The Catalog of the Living. If someone’s biography is being written to completion, the book will show it. The pages will be filling faster than normal. The entries will be — denser. More detailed. A biography that’s being completed doesn’t just record events. It records everything. Every breath. Every thought. Every moment. Because the completion has to be perfect. The Archive demands perfection.”


“I can’t spend hours in the Catalog. Tabni will notice.”


“Tabni notices everything. But Tabni also has a schedule, and the schedule has gaps, and the gaps are predictable.” Utu set down his spoon. “I’ll cover for you. I’ll tell the duty master you’re in the Lower Registry doing source verification for the Hasan biography. That buys you three hours. Maybe four, if I’m convincing.”


“You’re always convincing.”


“I’m always lying. There’s a difference. Convincing requires the other person to believe you. Lying only requires you to believe yourself.” He picked up his spoon again. “Go. Now. Before the morning shift fills the stacks.”




The Catalog of the Living was quiet at this hour. The morning light — filtered through Enki’s crystal shafts, golden and warm — fell across the shelves in long diagonal bars, illuminating dust motes that drifted in the still air like tiny, purposeless stars. Naram moved through the stacks, pulling books at random, checking the pages.


Most were normal. The Archive’s automatic writing — small, precise, inhuman — filling pages at the steady, unhurried pace of a system that had all the time in the world. A page a day, roughly. The events of a life, recorded without judgment, without interpretation, without the adjectives that human scribes added. Just facts. Just truth. Just the relentless, comprehensive documentation of existence.


But some books were different.


He found the first one in the Damascus section — a woman named Layla, a weaver. Her book was thick — thicker than it should have been for a woman of thirty-two — and the pages were filling fast. Not a page a day. A page an hour. The writing was denser, more detailed, recording not just events but sensations, thoughts, the texture of the thread between her fingers, the smell of the dye vats, the sound of her children arguing in the next room. A life being documented with the obsessive completeness of a biography approaching its end.


He found the second in the Baghdad section. A man named Rashid, a soldier. His book was even thicker, the writing even denser. The most recent entry described the exact pattern of light on the wall of his barracks at dawn, the precise temperature of the air, the number of breaths he took between waking and rising. The level of detail was inhuman. No scribe could write this fast, this thoroughly. Only the Archive itself — or something that had become part of the Archive — could produce this kind of output.


He found the third. The fourth. The fifth.


By the time he had checked fifty books, he had found twelve that were being completed. Twelve lives being written to their ends. Twelve people who would die — not of disease, not of accident, not of old age, but of narrative. Their biographies would be finished, and the finishing would make the deaths real, and the deaths would feed Gilgamesh’s endless writing, and the cycle would continue.


And then he found the thirteenth book, and the cycle broke.


The thirteenth book was in the Archive section — the shelf reserved for people who lived and worked within the Archive itself. Scribes, apprentices, engineers, cooks. The book was thin — the biography of someone young, someone whose life hadn’t accumulated enough events to fill many pages. The writing was dense. The pages were filling fast.


The name on the spine was UTU.


Naram pulled the book with hands that had gone cold. He opened it to the most recent entry. The Archive’s writing, small and precise and relentless:


The subject ate lentils (garlic preparation) in the refectory at the sixth hour. The subject spoke with Naram regarding the investigation. The subject agreed to provide cover for Naram’s visit to the Catalog of the Living. The subject is unaware that his own biography is being completed. The subject is scheduled to die of fever in seven days.


Seven days. Not three. Utu had a week. A week of lentils and lies and the comfortable illusion that his life was his own, while somewhere in the deep Archive, in an empty tomb full of open books, the dead hand of Gilgamesh wrote the fever that would kill him.


Naram closed the book. He put it back on the shelf. He stood in the golden light of the Catalog, surrounded by the biographies of the living, and he felt something shift inside him — the same shift he’d felt in the Lower Registry, the feeling of a thread attached to something vast. But this time the feeling was not curiosity. It was rage.


Not the hot, explosive rage of a young man who has been wronged. Something colder. Something quieter. The rage of a person who has discovered that the system they trusted — the system they served, the system they believed in — is not just flawed but predatory. That the Archive did not just document lives. It consumed them. That the institution Naram had given his youth to, the institution that had fed him lentils and taught him to write and given him a purpose, was a machine for turning people into fuel.


He would not let Utu die. He would not let Layla the weaver die, or Rashid the soldier, or any of the twelve — thirteen — however many there were. He would find a way to stop Gilgamesh. To stop the completions. To break the cycle.


He had two days. Less than two.


He had better start.




End of Chapter Ten
