Chapter 13: The Peri’s Song

“Memory is a weapon. The Peri don’t need swords. They need your worst day, and they’ll play it for you on repeat until you beg them to stop.” — Samira

The Peri islands rose from the sea like broken fingers — volcanic spires of black glass, sharp and gleaming, arranged in a rough circle around a lagoon of water so still it looked solid. The air above the islands shimmered with heat and something else — a distortion that the bahr-sense identified as concentrated Sihr, a magical field so dense it was almost visible, bending light the way a lens bends light, making the islands appear to shift and breathe.

The Peri lived on the spires. Bird-women — that was the simplest description, and like all simple descriptions, it was inadequate. They had the bodies of women and the wings of raptors, feathered in colors that shifted with their moods — gold when calm, red when angry, black when hunting. Their faces were human but their eyes were avian — large, round, golden, with the fixed, unblinking stare of creatures that saw in spectrums humans couldn’t imagine. They sang in Old Pahlavi — a language that Kian recognized from his archaeological training, the ancient Persian tongue that predated Arabic script, that had been dead in his world for a millennium.

Their song didn’t seduce. It
excavated
.

The Peri’s song was a frequency — a vibration that bypassed the ears and entered the brain directly, targeting the hippocampus, the amygdala, the neural architecture of memory. It found your worst moment — not your saddest, not your most painful, but your
worst
, the moment you were most ashamed of, the moment that defined your failure as a human being — and it played it. On loop. In high definition. With surround sound.

Kian heard the song from a mile out. It hit him like a fist.

He was in Qasim. Not remembering Qasim —
in
Qasim, standing in the village square, watching himself give the tactical assessment to Qadir and the bandit, watching his own mouth form the words that would doom two hundred people. But the song’s version was worse. In the song’s version, he didn’t just provide information. He led the bandits himself. He carried a torch. He set the first fire. He heard the screams — not from a mile offshore but from inside the houses, from the rooms where families huddled, from the places where children hid.

He knew it wasn’t real. The rational part of his mind — battered, diminished, but still functioning — recognized the manipulation. The song was taking his guilt and amplifying it, distorting it, turning a moment of complicity into a fantasy of direct participation. It was a lie. A sophisticated, targeted, neurologically precise lie.

It felt real. It felt more real than reality. It felt like truth.

“Fight it,” Samira said. The witch was beside him on the deck, her hands pressed to her ears — a futile gesture, since the song didn’t enter through the ears. Her storm-green eyes were wide, her face pale. She was fighting her own worst moment, whatever it was. “The song feeds on engagement. Don’t engage. Don’t argue with it. Don’t try to correct it. Just — observe. Watch it like a play. You’re in the audience, not on the stage.”

But Kian was not just an audience. He was an archaeoastronomer who had spent fifteen years studying dead languages, and the Peri were singing in Old Pahlavi, and Old Pahlavi was not dead to him. It was a language he had read on clay tablets and carved stones and the inside of a dead navigator’s skull. He knew its grammar. He knew its cadence. He knew the way its vowels shifted meaning depending on pitch, the way its consonants carried emotional weight that modern Persian had lost.

The song was a weapon. But a weapon in a language you understand is a weapon you can turn.

Kian opened his mouth and spoke back.

Not a counter-song — he couldn’t sing, had never been able to sing, had been asked to leave his school choir at age eleven for the good of the other children. But Old Pahlavi was not just a spoken language. It was a
tonal
language, where meaning lived in the spaces between words, in the rise and fall of syllables, in the breath patterns that connected one phrase to the next. The Peri were using those tonal patterns to excavate memory. Kian used them to
redirect
.

He recited. The opening lines of the Bundahishn — the Zoroastrian creation text, the oldest cosmogony he knew in Old Pahlavi, a text about the world being made from the body of a sacrificed god. The words were academic, memorized from transliteration charts in his office at the University of Tehran, practiced in empty lecture halls where the acoustics were terrible and the audience was imaginary. But the
tones
were right. The pitch patterns of the creation text ran counter to the pitch patterns of the Peri’s excavation — where the song dug down, the recitation built up. Where the song narrowed toward shame, the recitation widened toward origin.

The vision of Qasim flickered. The flames stuttered. For one moment, Kian was not in the burning village but in the space between — the liminal zone where the song’s frequency and his counter-frequency collided, canceling each other out, producing a silence that was not empty but
balanced
.

The Peri queen noticed. Her song shifted — targeting him specifically, the frequency narrowing, intensifying, drilling toward the guilt with surgical precision. The vision of Qasim returned, worse than before. Now he was not just carrying a torch. He was
choosing
to carry it. The song had found the real wound — not the act but the passivity, the silence, the moment when he could have spoken and didn’t.

Kian adjusted. He switched texts — from the Bundahishn to the Yasna, the liturgical prayers, the sections he’d translated for his doctoral thesis on astronomical references in Zoroastrian ritual. The Yasna’s tonal patterns were different: repetitive, cyclical, designed for chanting, for endurance. They didn’t cancel the song. They
outlasted
it. The way water outlasts stone — not by force but by persistence.

The vision dimmed. Not gone — the Peri queen was too powerful for that — but reduced. Manageable. A memory rather than an experience. Kian could see Qasim burning, but he could also see the deck of the Dhow, the coral spires, the sky. He was in both places at once, and the duality was bearable in a way that the total immersion had not been.

“How are you doing that?” Samira gasped. She was on her knees, fighting her own vision, but her witch’s senses had detected the shift in the song’s frequency around Kian. “The song — you’re disrupting it—”

“Old Pahlavi,” Kian said, through gritted teeth, still reciting, the Yasna’s syllables running beneath his words like a bass line. “The Peri sing in Old Pahlavi. I speak Old Pahlavi. The tonal patterns can be countered if you know the liturgical registers—”

“You’re lecturing me about
linguistics
while a bird-woman is trying to drive you insane?”

“I’m an academic. Lecturing under duress is what we do.”

He couldn’t extend the protection to the crew — the counter-frequency was too personal, too dependent on his specific knowledge and his specific voice. But he could hold his own ground, and holding his own ground meant he could function, and functioning meant he could coordinate.

The crew was suffering. Omar was on his knees, weeping, his jovial mask shattered by whatever the song was showing him — the cabin boy, the merchant, the things that had been done to him and the things he’d done in return. Leila was rigid, her hands white-knuckled on the rail, her face a mask of controlled fury that was cracking at the edges. Fatima was still — too still, the surgeon’s composure taken to its extreme, a woman who had locked herself so tightly inside her professional persona that the song couldn’t find the person underneath.

Amir was unaffected.

The cabin boy stood on the foredeck, calm, composed, watching the Peri islands approach with an expression of mild interest. The song washed over him and found — nothing. No worst moment. No shame. No guilt. Or, more accurately, a worst moment so deeply buried, so thoroughly concealed, that the song’s frequency couldn’t reach it.

“Amir,” Kian gasped, fighting through the vision of burning Qasim. “The tooth. It’s on the central spire. The queen’s nest.”

“I know,” Amir said. “I’ll get it.”

“The Peri—”

“Won’t stop me.” He said it with the quiet certainty of someone stating a physical law. Gravity pulls down. Water is wet. The Peri won’t stop me. “Stay here. Keep the crew alive. I’ll be back.”

He dove off the bow. Not into the water — into the air. He
flew
. Not with wings, not with magic that Kian could identify through the bahr-sense, but with a lightness that defied gravity, a buoyancy that carried him upward and forward, toward the central spire, toward the Peri queen’s nest, toward the sixth tooth.

The Peri saw him. They rose from their spires — dozens of them, gold and red and black, their wings spreading, their songs intensifying. The sound hit the Dhow like a wall. Omar collapsed. Two sailors screamed. Leila drew her swords, though there was nothing to fight, and the drawing was reflex, the body’s response to a threat the mind couldn’t process.

Amir flew through them. The Peri parted around him — not attacking, not fleeing, but
deferring
, their wings folding, their songs faltering, their golden eyes tracking the cabin boy with an expression that Kian, even through the agony of the song, recognized.

Recognition. Respect.
Obedience
.

The Peri knew Amir. The Peri
bowed
to Amir.

He reached the central spire. The queen’s nest was at the top — a structure of woven feathers and volcanic glass, large enough to hold a dozen Peri, glittering with treasures collected over centuries. The queen herself was there — the largest Peri, her wings black as midnight, her song the loudest, the most devastating, the frequency that was driving the crew to madness.

Amir landed in the nest. He spoke to the queen. Kian couldn’t hear the words — the song was too loud, the distance too great — but he could see the exchange through the bahr-sense: a conversation conducted in Old Pahlavi, in gestures, in a language of deference and authority that suggested a relationship far older and far more complex than a cabin boy negotiating with a bird-woman.

The queen gave him the tooth. She placed it in his hand — gently, carefully, the way you hand a precious thing to someone you trust — and the song stopped.

Silence. Absolute, ringing, deafening silence. The absence of the song was as overwhelming as the song itself — a void where the worst moments had been, a space that the mind rushed to fill with anything, everything, just to escape the quiet.

Amir flew back to the Dhow. He landed on the deck, light as a leaf, and placed the sixth tooth in Kian’s palm.

“I asked nicely,” he said.

No one believed him. No one pressed.

That night, Kian couldn’t sleep. The song’s echoes lingered — not the full vision, not the burning village, but fragments. Flashes. The smell of smoke. The sound of a child crying. The weight of a torch in his hand that he’d never actually held.

He wasn’t the only one awake. The crew was scattered across the deck like survivors of a shipwreck — which, in a sense, they were. The Peri’s song had wrecked something inside each of them, and the wreckage was visible in the way they avoided each other’s eyes, in the way they sat alone in their separate pools of darkness, each one carrying the weight of whatever the song had shown them.

Omar was sick. Not metaphorically — physically, violently sick, hanging over the rail and emptying his considerable stomach into the Sea of Tears. The song had shown him something that his body couldn’t contain, and the body was rejecting it the only way it knew how. Between bouts of vomiting, he muttered curses in three languages and made promises to gods he didn’t believe in.

“I will never eat again,” Omar groaned. “I will become thin. I will become ascetic. I will live on air and prayer and the satisfaction of moral superiority.”

“You said that last time you were sick,” Amir said, sitting nearby with the calm of someone who had been unaffected by the song and was now serving as the crew’s designated functional person. “You were eating dates within the hour.”

“Dates are medicinal. Dates don’t count.”

“Everything counts with you, Omar. That’s your whole philosophy.”

“My philosophy is that everything has a price. The price of the Peri’s song is apparently my dinner. And my dignity. And my will to live.” He vomited again. “I take it back. I will eat again. I will eat
specifically
to spite the Peri. I will eat an enormous meal and I will enjoy it and the enjoyment will be my revenge.”

Umm Hani brought him ginger tea. She didn’t say anything — just pressed the cup into his hands and sat beside him on the deck, her hip against his shoulder, the way a mother sits with a sick child. Omar drank. The ginger was sharp and warm and it settled his stomach, and he leaned against Umm Hani, and she let him, and for a moment the quartermaster was not the quartermaster but a large, frightened man being comforted by a small, fierce woman, and the comfort was the most ordinary thing in the world, and the ordinary was, after the Peri’s song, the most extraordinary thing imaginable.

A flying fish landed on the deck. It flopped twice, its wings catching the moonlight like wet silk, and Amir picked it up and held it to the lantern. “Look,” he said. “The scales are blue. I’ve never seen blue ones.” He held it out to Omar, who was still green-faced and miserable. Omar looked at the fish. The fish looked at Omar. Its tiny mouth opened and closed with the mechanical persistence of a creature that had not yet realized it was out of its element.

“It’s judging me,” Omar said.

“It’s a fish. It doesn’t have the cognitive capacity to judge you.”

“It’s judging me. I can see it in its eyes. It’s thinking:
this man is pathetic. This man was defeated by a song. I am a fish and I have more dignity than this man.
” He took the fish from Amir’s hands, gently, and tossed it back over the rail. It hit the water with a small splash and was gone. “Go. Be free. Tell the other fish that Omar ibn Khalid was brought low by bird-women and ginger tea, and that he will recover, and that his recovery will be magnificent.”

Leila was sharpening her swords. She’d been sharpening them for an hour — long past the point of necessity, into the territory of compulsion. The rhythmic scrape of stone on steel was her version of Omar’s vomiting: a physical response to a psychological wound, the body doing something — anything — to process what the mind couldn’t.

“What did you see?” Kian asked her. He shouldn’t have asked. He knew he shouldn’t have asked. But the song had stripped away his social filters along with his worst memories, and the question came out before his better judgment could stop it.

Leila’s sharpening didn’t pause. “I saw the barrel,” she said. Flat. Final. The two words contained an entire history of drowning and survival and the specific, intimate terror of water in the lungs, and the two words were all she would give, and the giving was more than she’d given anyone in years.

“I’m sorry I asked.”

“Don’t be sorry. Be quiet.” She tested the blade’s edge against her thumb. A line of blood appeared. She watched it with the detached interest of a woman who had a complicated relationship with her own pain. “The song showed me the barrel. It also showed me what I did to the man who held me under. After Leila was freed. After the chains came off.” She paused. “I went back for him. I found him in a tavern in Iskandar. He was old. Drunk. He didn’t recognize me. I sat across from him and I told him who I was, and I watched his face change, and the change was — satisfying. The most satisfying thing I’ve ever felt. More satisfying than food. More satisfying than freedom. The satisfaction of watching a man realize that the girl he’d drowned was sitting across from him with a sword.”

“Did you kill him?”

“No. I bought him a drink. I sat with him while he drank it. I let him see that I was alive and free and strong and that he was old and drunk and nothing. And then I left. And the leaving was better than killing. Because killing ends the fear. Leaving lets it grow.”

She resumed sharpening. The conversation was over.

Fatima found him on deck. She didn’t ask what he’d seen. She sat beside him on the foredeck and took his remaining hand, and they watched the moons — silver and amber, whole and broken — and they said nothing.

Fatima’s hand was warm. Her grip was firm. Her fingers interlaced with his, and the touch was not romantic, not yet, but it was
real
, and in a world made of memory and magic and illusion, real was the rarest and most valuable thing.

“I saw something,” she said. “When I held the tooth. In Morgana’s court. A vision.”

“What did you see?”

She was quiet for a long time. The sea glowed beneath them. The ship hummed.

“I saw the ending,” she said. “Not all of it. A piece. A moment.” She squeezed his hand. “I’m not going to tell you what I saw. Not because I don’t trust you. Because knowing the ending changes how you live the middle, and we’re still in the middle, and the middle is where the living happens.”

“That’s either very wise or very evasive.”

“It’s both. I’m a surgeon. We’re trained in both.” She leaned against his shoulder. Her weight was slight, warm, real. “Sleep, Kian. Tomorrow we hunt. The teeth won’t wait.”

“The teeth have been waiting for centuries.”

“And they’re getting impatient. I can feel it. Can’t you?”

He could. Six teeth in the hold, whispering their sextet of hunger, and the harmony was growing richer, more complex, more beautiful. The beauty was the danger. The beauty was the trap. The teeth sang, and the song was lovely, and the loveliness was the sound of a mouth learning to open.

He didn’t sleep. Not yet.

Fatima’s breathing slowed beside him, her grip loosening as sleep took her, and Kian sat in the dark and watched Amir.

The cabin boy was alone on the bow, cross-legged, watching the stars with an expression that Kian had seen on the faces of old professors at the University of Tehran — the expression of someone revisiting familiar material, checking it against memory, confirming that the constellations were where they were supposed to be. It was not the expression of a twelve-year-old. It had never been the expression of a twelve-year-old.

Kian eased Fatima’s hand from his and went forward.

Amir didn’t turn. He knew Kian was coming — he always knew, the way he always knew where the wind would shift and when the crew would wake and which questions to deflect with a joke. Kian sat beside him on the bow, and for a while they watched the stars together, and the silence between them was not the comfortable silence of friends but the loaded silence of two people who both knew a conversation was overdue.

“You flew,” Kian said.

“Yes.”

“The Peri bowed to you.”

“Yes.”

“The song couldn’t touch you. A frequency designed to excavate the worst moment of any living mind, and it found nothing in yours. Not because you don’t have a worst moment — everyone has a worst moment — but because whatever you are, the song couldn’t reach it.”

Amir said nothing.

“In the Sunken Caliphate, you breathed underwater without explanation. On the Nasnas island, you moved faster than a human child can move. At the slave ship, you appeared on the Dhow without anyone seeing you board. You read Attar and caught yourself. You speak Old Pahlavi — I heard you, through the bahr-sense, when you spoke to the queen. You speak a language that’s been dead for a thousand years, and you speak it fluently, and you are
twelve
.”

“I’m charming,” Amir said. “Even dead languages appreciate charm.”

“Don’t.” Kian’s voice was quiet but the word landed hard. “Don’t deflect. Not tonight. The song stripped everyone on this ship down to their worst selves, and you sat through it like someone watching rain through a window. I have earned the right to ask you directly: what are you?”

Amir was still for a long time. The stars turned overhead. The sea glowed. The six teeth hummed their sextet in the hold, and the humming was the only sound, and the sound was the sound of a world slowly being eaten.

“I can’t tell you,” Amir said. Not
I won’t
.
I can’t
. The distinction was important, and Kian heard it. “Not because I don’t trust you. Because the telling would change things. It would change how you treat me, how the crew treats me, how the mission unfolds. And the mission needs to unfold the way it unfolds. The spiral has a shape, Kian. You know this — you calculated it. The shape can’t be forced.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the only answer I can give you right now.” Amir turned to look at him, and in the starlight, his eyes were — old. Not metaphorically old, not wise-beyond-his-years old, but
old
the way the sea was old, the way the stones on Mount Qaf would be old, the way things are old when they have existed for longer than the word
old
has existed to describe them. The look lasted a fraction of a second. Then the boy was back — twelve, small, sharp-featured, ordinary.

“I will tell you,” Amir said. “When it’s time. When the spiral reaches the place where my secret and your mission intersect. You’ll understand then. You’ll understand everything.”

“And if I don’t like what I understand?”

“You won’t.” Amir’s voice was gentle. “But you’ll see why it had to be this way. And seeing why is not the same as liking it, but it’s close enough to live with.”

He stood, brushed the salt from his trousers, and walked below deck with the light, silent step of someone who weighed less than a child should weigh, or perhaps more than a child could carry.

Kian sat on the bow alone and thought:
I am sailing a ship with a crew of broken people and a cabin boy who is not a cabin boy, toward the mouth of a sleeping god, guided by a star chart carved into a dead man’s skull, and the only person who might know how this ends is a twelve-year-old who speaks dead languages and flies.

He slept. Fitfully. The teeth sang. The sea remembered.

In the morning, Kian spread the star chart across the deck — not the physical chart, which existed only in his memory, but a version he’d scratched onto a piece of sailcloth with charcoal, the stellar positions marked in his cramped academic handwriting. The crew gathered around it the way they gathered around Umm Hani’s cooking pot: warily, hungrily, needing what it offered but not trusting it entirely.

“Six teeth,” Kian said. “Twenty-four to go. The spiral continues inward.” He traced the path with his finger — the nautilus curve, tightening, each loop smaller than the last. “The next cluster is here — three teeth within a week’s sail of each other. A sea-serpent’s graveyard. A merchant prince’s vault. And something the chart calls the Bride’s Garden, which Ibn Majid’s notes describe as—” He paused, reading his own translation. “‘The place where the sea’s grief grows flowers.’ Whatever that means.”

“It means we’ll need more bandages,” Fatima said.

“It means we’ll need more food,” Omar said.

“It means we’ll need more swords,” Leila said.

They looked at each other — the surgeon, the quartermaster, the first mate — and something passed between them that was not quite a smile but was adjacent to one. The shared recognition that they were still here, still arguing, still themselves, despite everything the sea had thrown at them. The Peri’s song had shown them their worst moments. The morning had shown them that worst moments end, and breakfast follows, and the spiral continues, and you keep going because the alternative is stopping, and stopping is not something this crew knew how to do.

“Three teeth in a week,” Qadir said from the helm. “Then we’ll see what the spiral demands next.”

The Dhow sailed south. The crew ate Umm Hani’s breakfast. The star chart burned in Kian’s mind. And the spiral tightened.

End of Chapter 13
