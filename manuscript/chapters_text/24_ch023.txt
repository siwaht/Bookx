Chapter 20: The Simurgh

“Wisdom is the wound that never heals. The wise man bleeds knowledge the way the fool bleeds ignorance — constantly, and without knowing he’s dying.” — Inscription on the gates of Mount Qaf

Mount Qaf stood at the edge of the world.

Not metaphorically. The Sea of Tears had borders — Kian had learned this the way he learned everything in this world, through pain and proximity. Mount Qaf was one of them. Beyond the mountain, reality thinned to gossamer and then to nothing, a white absence that was not fog or cloud or light but the simple nonexistence of things that had not been imagined yet. The mountain itself was the last argument the world made before giving up on coherence.

They had sailed for nine days after leaving Dar al-Salaam, the Dhow cutting through waters that grew progressively stranger. On the third day, the sea turned the color of mercury. On the fifth, it became transparent — not clear, but genuinely transparent, as if the concept of opacity had been revoked, and the crew could see straight through the water to the seabed miles below, where things moved that were too large and too deliberate to be fish. On the seventh day, the water began to taste of numbers. Kian couldn’t explain it better than that. Each wave that broke against the hull carried a flavor that his mind translated not as salt or mineral but as equations — the Fibonacci sequence tasted of copper, prime numbers tasted of anise, and pi tasted of something infinite and slightly burnt, like bread left too long in the oven.

Omar, who had been seasick for the first time in his life since the water turned transparent, stood at the rail and spat. “The sea tastes like mathematics,” he said. “I hate mathematics. Mathematics is what merchants use to cheat other merchants, and I say this as a merchant who cheats other merchants.”

“It’s the proximity to the world-border,” Samira said. She was wrapped in a shawl despite the heat, her eyes distant, her voice carrying the particular flatness it had acquired since Fatima’s capture and rescue. She spoke less now. When she did speak, it was with the precision of someone rationing words. “Reality is thinner here. The rules that govern things — physics, you’d call them, Kian — they’re suggestions rather than laws. The mountain exists at the boundary between what is and what could be.”

“Wonderful,” Omar said. “A mountain made of could-be. Can I could-be somewhere else?”

Kian stood at the prow and stared at Mount Qaf and felt the fourteenth pearl calling to him from somewhere inside that impossible geography. The mountain rose from the sea without preamble — no foothills, no gradual ascent, just a vertical eruption of stone that punched through the clouds and kept going, its peak lost in a sky that had shifted from blue to violet to a color that didn’t have a name in any language Kian spoke, including the ones the sea had given him. The stone was white — not marble-white or chalk-white but the white of bone, of teeth, of things that had once been alive and were now architecture. Veins of crystal ran through it like frozen lightning, pulsing with a light that was not quite light, that was closer to
attention
, as if the mountain were watching them approach and forming opinions.

“The Simurgh lives at the summit,” Amir said quietly. The cabin boy — and Kian still thought of Amir as a cabin boy, despite the accumulating evidence that Amir was something else entirely, something that wore the shape of a child the way a hermit crab wears a shell — stood beside Kian with an expression that might have been reverence or might have been fear. With Amir, the distinction was often academic. “The Conference of the Birds. Attar wrote about it. Thirty birds seek the Simurgh and discover that they
are
the Simurgh.
Si murgh
— thirty birds.”

“You’ve read Attar?” Kian asked.

“I’ve read everything,” Amir said, and then caught himself, and added, “that Omar has in his cabin,” which was a lie so transparent that even the transparent sea would have been embarrassed by it.

“The mountain rejects those without questions,” Samira continued. “It’s not a test of strength or courage. It’s a test of curiosity. The mountain wants to know if you want to know. If you climb without questions, the mountain simply… isn’t there. You walk and walk and never ascend.”

Kian looked at his hands. His hand. His left wrist ended in a stump that Fatima had shaped into something almost elegant — the scar tissue smooth, the bone filed, the skin grafted from his thigh with a surgeon’s precision and a lover’s care. His right hand was still his, still whole, still capable of holding a pen, a cup, a sword, a woman’s face. He flexed the fingers and felt the tendons move and the joints articulate and the nerves fire, and he thought about what it meant to have one hand in a world that demanded two.

“I’ll go alone,” he said.

“No,” Fatima said. She had come up behind him — she moved quietly now, had moved quietly since the inquisitors, as if she’d learned that being heard was dangerous. Her face was thinner than it had been in Iskandar. The burns on her arms had healed into pale rivers of scar tissue that she no longer bothered to hide. Her eyes were the same — dark, intelligent, furious — but the fury had a different quality now. It was colder. More patient. The fury of someone who had learned to wait.

“The mountain rejects those without questions,” Kian repeated. “I have questions. I have nothing but questions. I’m the most qualified person on this ship to climb a mountain that runs on curiosity.”

“And if the Simurgh doesn’t want to negotiate?”

“Everything negotiates. Omar taught me that.”

“Omar negotiates with merchants and slavers. The Simurgh is neither.”

“The Simurgh is a creature that hoards knowledge. Knowledge is currency. Currency can be exchanged. I have something it wants — a perspective from another world, a mind trained in sciences this world has never seen. I am, for the first time since I arrived here, the richest man in the room.”

Fatima stared at him. The wind caught her hair — she’d stopped braiding it, another change, another small surrender of the person she’d been — and for a moment she looked like the woman he’d met in the Mulberry Tree, the surgeon with steady hands and a laugh that could fill a room. Then the moment passed, and she was the woman she’d become, and the distance between those two people was measured in scars.

“Come back,” she said. Not a request. Not a command. A statement of what she required from the universe, delivered with the certainty of someone who had stopped believing the universe listened but refused to stop speaking.

“I’ll come back,” Kian said.

He was not lying. He would come back. He would come back diminished, transformed, carrying knowledge that would poison every remaining day of his life. But he would come back. That was the Simurgh’s particular cruelty — it always let you come back. It just made sure you wished you hadn’t.

The mountain’s base was a beach of white stones, each one perfectly round, each one warm to the touch, each one humming at a frequency just below hearing. Kian stepped onto them and felt the hum travel up through his bare feet — he’d removed his boots; the stones demanded it, though he couldn’t have said how he knew — and into his bones, and the hum became a question.

Why are you here?

Not words. A vibration that his mind translated into meaning the way his mind had translated the slavers’ language on his first day in this world. The mountain spoke in resonance, in harmonics, in the mathematics that flavored the sea.

“I’m here for the pearl,” Kian said aloud. His voice sounded strange against the stone — flattened, stripped of echo, as if the mountain absorbed sound the way the sea absorbed memory.

Why?

“Because a god is sleeping and his teeth are scattered and if I don’t collect them, someone worse will.”

Why does that matter?

“Because people will die.”

People always die. Why does it matter?

Kian paused. The question was not rhetorical. The mountain genuinely wanted to know. It was asking the way a child asks — not to challenge, but to understand. Why does death matter? Why does suffering matter? Why does any of it matter, when the sea is made of tears and the tears are made of memory and memory is made of salt and salt preserves nothing forever?

“Because a boy died in a slave ship’s hold,” Kian said. “And I didn’t tell him a story. And I’ve been carrying that silence ever since. And if I can prevent other silences — other boys dying without stories — then the carrying has a purpose. And if the carrying has a purpose, then maybe the silence does too. And if the silence has a purpose, then maybe the boy’s death wasn’t meaningless. And I need it to not be meaningless. I need that more than I need hands or memories or a way home.”

The stones shifted. The path opened. The mountain accepted his answer — not because it was correct, but because it was honest, and honesty, Kian was learning, was the only currency that worked at the edge of the world.

He climbed.

The mountain tested him.

Not with monsters or traps or the crude physical challenges of the Crawling Reef or the Karkadann’s island. Mount Qaf tested with
perception
. The path — if it could be called a path; it was more a suggestion of upwardness, a gradient of intention — wound through terrain that refused to behave.

The first illusion came an hour into the climb. The stone beneath his feet became glass, and through the glass he could see downward into the mountain’s interior, and the interior was a library. Shelves of books stretching in every direction, infinite, organized by a system he almost understood. He could read the spines — titles in Arabic, Persian, Greek, Sanskrit, languages he’d never seen, languages that might not exist yet. The books contained everything. Every thought ever thought, every equation ever solved, every poem ever written, every lie ever told. The library was the mountain’s heart, and it was beating, and each beat sent a pulse of knowledge through the glass that made Kian’s teeth ache with wanting.

He kept climbing. The library was not the test. The test was whether he would stop.

The second illusion came two hours later. The path split into three, and each path showed him a different version of himself. The left path showed Kian as he had been — Dr. Farahani, tenured professor, published author, respected in his field, married to Sara, living in a house with a garden and a cat and a mortgage and the quiet, grinding contentment of a life lived within its means. The middle path showed Kian as he was — one-handed, tattooed, scarred, carrying thirteen fragments of a dead god’s jaw, in love with a woman who was becoming something dangerous, captain of a dying ship, a man who had traded his past for a future he might not survive. The right path showed Kian as he would be — but this he could not see clearly. It was obscured, not by fog or darkness but by
possibility
, by the sheer density of what-might-be, and the density was so great that looking at it directly caused a pain behind his eyes that was not physical but ontological, the pain of a mind trying to contain more future than it was designed to hold.

He took the middle path. Not because it was correct — all three paths led to the same place; the mountain was not a maze but a mirror — but because it was true. He was what he was. The man he had been was gone. The man he would become was not yet real. The man he was — broken, determined, afraid, curious — was the only man who could climb this mountain, because this man had questions, and the mountain fed on questions the way the sea fed on tears.

The third test was not an illusion. It was a conversation.

The mountain spoke to him. Not in vibrations now — in a voice. A woman’s voice, low and warm, speaking Persian with the accent of Isfahan, the accent of his mother, the accent of home.

“Kian-jan. You’re tired. Sit. Rest. Tell me what you’ve learned.”

He sat. Not because the voice commanded it — because his legs were shaking and his stump was throbbing and the altitude, if altitude was the right word for a mountain that existed at the edge of reality, was making his head swim. He sat on a ledge of white stone and looked out over the Sea of Tears, which from this height was a disc of silver stretching to every horizon, and the Dhow was a splinter of black on its surface, and the crew were specks, and the world was vast and indifferent and beautiful in the way that only vast, indifferent things can be beautiful.

“I’ve learned that memory is currency,” he said to the voice that sounded like his mother. “That power costs what you can least afford to lose. That love doesn’t protect you from anything — it just gives you something to lose. That food shared is the closest thing to prayer I’ve found in this world. That a boy died because I was silent, and I will never be silent again, and that promise is the only thing I’ve kept.”

“What haven’t you learned?”

“How it ends. What I become. Whether any of it matters.”

“Do you want to know?”

“Yes. That’s why I’m climbing.”

“Knowing the ending changes the story. You understand this. You’re a scholar. You know that observation alters the observed. If I show you how it ends, you will live differently. You will love differently. You will die differently. The ending I show you will not be the ending you get, because the showing changes the getting. This is the paradox of prophecy. This is why the wise are miserable and the ignorant are brave.”

“Show me anyway.”

“Why?”

“Because I’d rather be miserable and prepared than brave and blind. Because the people on that ship—” He pointed at the Dhow, the splinter, the speck. “—deserve a captain who knows what’s coming. Even if knowing breaks him.”

The voice was silent for a long time. The wind at this altitude tasted of ozone and old paper and something sweet — mulberries, he thought, though there were no trees on this white mountain, no life at all except the crystal veins that pulsed with their not-quite-light. The silence stretched until it became a presence, a third party in the conversation, and Kian understood that the silence was the mountain thinking, and the mountain thought slowly, because the mountain thought in geological time, in the patient accumulation of sediment and pressure that turns sand into stone and stone into something that can ask questions.

“Continue climbing,”
the voice said.
“The Simurgh is waiting. It has been waiting for you specifically. It has been waiting for a very long time.”

“How long?”

“Since before you were born. Since before your world was born. The Simurgh exists outside of time the way a reader exists outside of a book — present at every page simultaneously, knowing the ending before the beginning, loving the characters despite their fate. It read you, Kian. It read you the way you read artifacts — with care, with curiosity, with the knowledge that understanding a thing does not save it.”

Kian stood. His legs had stopped shaking. The voice had given him something — not strength, not courage, but
clarity
, the particular clarity that comes from knowing you are walking toward something that will change you irreversibly and choosing to walk anyway.

He climbed.

The summit of Mount Qaf was not a peak.

It was a garden.

Kian had expected stone, ice, the barren grandeur of extreme altitude. Instead, he stepped through a final arch of crystal — the veins converging, braiding, forming a gateway that hummed with a chord so complex it contained every note ever played — and into a space that was green and warm and alive in a way that nothing on the mountain had been alive. Trees grew here — not the twisted, salt-stunted trees of the Sea of Tears’ islands, but trees of impossible variety and health. A cedar that smelled of Lebanon. A cherry tree in full blossom, its petals falling in a wind that existed only around its branches. A mulberry tree — and Kian’s heart clenched, because the mulberry tree looked exactly like the one in the tavern in Iskandar, the one where he’d eaten kabsa and laughed with his crew and held Fatima’s hand for the first time, and he understood that the garden was not showing him trees but showing him
memories of trees
, the Platonic ideals from which all earthly trees were imperfect copies.

A stream ran through the garden, clear as thought, cold as mathematics. Flowers grew along its banks in colors that had no names — not because they were supernatural but because the human eye had never evolved to see them, and Kian’s brain, struggling to process input it wasn’t designed for, translated them into sensations instead: this flower tasted of Tuesday, that one smelled of the number seven, the one by the stream felt like the moment before a sneeze.

And in the center of the garden, on a branch of the mulberry tree, sat the Simurgh.

It was not a bird.

It was a concept that had chosen to look like a bird because concepts, when they wish to be perceived by mortal minds, must wear shapes, and the shape of a bird was the closest approximation to what the Simurgh actually was — which was
knowledge
, not as abstraction but as entity, not as noun but as verb, the living, breathing, feathered act of knowing.

It was enormous. Its body filled the garden the way a symphony fills a concert hall — not by occupying space but by saturating it, by making every cubic inch of air vibrate with its presence. Its feathers were every color, and each color was a language, and each language was a library, and each library was a civilization’s accumulated understanding rendered in keratin and light. Its eyes — two, then four, then thirty, then two again, the number shifting with Kian’s attention — contained not pupils but
texts
, scrolling endlessly, the collected works of every mind that had ever asked a question and received an answer.

It looked at Kian. All of its eyes looked at Kian. And Kian felt himself being read — not his thoughts, not his memories, but his
structure
, the architecture of his mind, the way his neurons connected and his synapses fired and his consciousness emerged from the electrochemical soup of his brain like a flame emerges from fuel and oxygen: inevitable, fragile, beautiful, temporary.

The Simurgh spoke. Its voice was every voice — his mother’s, his father’s, Sara’s, Fatima’s, Omar’s, the dead boy’s, Zulfaqar’s, his own. It spoke in all of them simultaneously, and the effect was not cacophony but harmony, a chord of human experience that resonated in Kian’s chest like a second heartbeat.

“You have come for my tooth.”

Not
pearl
. Tooth. The Simurgh knew what the pearls were. Of course it did. The Simurgh knew everything. That was its nature and its curse.

“Yes,” Kian said.

“You know the price.”

“No. That’s why I’m here. To learn the price and decide if I can pay it.”

The Simurgh’s feathers rustled — a sound like ten thousand pages turning simultaneously. Something that might have been amusement moved through its eyes, a flicker of text that Kian almost caught, almost read, almost understood.

“The others who came before you — and there have been others, Kian, so many others, an endless procession of the desperate and the ambitious and the mad — they always asked the same question: ‘What must I give?’ As if sacrifice were a transaction. As if the universe operated on the principle of equivalent exchange. It does not. The universe is not fair. It is not a market. It is not a game with rules. It is a story, and stories demand not fairness but
meaning
.”

“Then what does the story demand?”

“That depends on the story. Your story—”
The Simurgh tilted its head, and the garden tilted with it, reality bending around the bird’s attention like spacetime bending around mass.
“Your story is about a man who came to a world he didn’t understand and tried to understand it, and in the trying, lost the tools of understanding one by one. Your wedding. Your mother’s voice. Your father’s face. Your left hand. The taste of coffee. The smell of rain. You have been paying for knowledge with the capacity to enjoy it. This is the scholar’s tragedy — to know everything and feel nothing.”

“I still feel,” Kian said. And he did. He felt the garden’s beauty like a blade between his ribs. He felt the absence of his left hand like a phantom limb that ached in weather that didn’t exist. He felt Fatima — not here, not present, but
felt
her, the way you feel the sun through a window, warmth without contact, presence without proximity.

“Yes. You still feel. That is why you are interesting. The others stopped feeling long before they reached me. The pearls ate their hearts before they ate their hands. But you—”
The Simurgh leaned closer, and its breath was warm and smelled of ink and cedar and the particular dusty sweetness of very old books.
“You love. Still. Despite everything. You love a woman who is becoming a weapon. You love a crew that is becoming a legend. You love a world that is not yours and never will be. Love is not a tool of understanding, Kian. It is the opposite. Love is the willingness to not understand — to accept mystery, to embrace the unknowable, to hold something you cannot catalogue or classify or explain. And that—”
The feathers rustled again.
“That is why I will give you what I have never given anyone.”

“The pearl?”

“The pearl. And the vision. And the price.”

“What’s the price?”

“Your remaining hand.”

The garden was silent. The stream stopped flowing. The flowers stopped their impossible colors. Even the wind — the localized, tree-specific wind — held its breath.

Kian looked at his right hand. His only hand. The hand that held Fatima’s face when he kissed her. The hand that gripped the Dhow’s rail in storms. The hand that turned pages, that wrote notes, that gestured when he talked, that reached for things, that
held
things, that was the last physical connection between his mind and the world, the final tool of the toolmaker, the last finger on the hand of the archaeologist who had once catalogued artifacts with the precision of a man who believed that touching the past could save the future.

“Why?” he asked. Not a refusal. A question. The mountain demanded questions, and Kian had learned to feed it.

“Because knowledge requires sacrifice. You have given memories — the currency of the mind. Now give capability — the currency of the body. What will you do when you cannot hold a sword? A pen? A lover’s hand? What will you be when you cannot
grasp
? The word itself — to grasp, to understand, to hold — your language makes no distinction between physical and intellectual possession. To lose your hands is to lose your grip on the world. And that—”
The Simurgh’s eyes blazed with text, scrolling faster now, urgent.
“That is what the story demands. A man who cannot hold anything, holding everything. A captain with no hands steering a ship through the end of the world. The paradox is the point. The impossibility is the meaning.”

“And the vision?”

“I will show you how it ends. All of it. The pearls, the god, the sea, the woman you love, the ship you sail, the world you’re trying to save. I will show you the last page of your story. And you will carry that knowledge — handless, gripless, unable to hold even the memory of holding — for the rest of your life. Which will be shorter than you think and longer than you want.”

Kian stood in the garden at the top of the world and looked at his hand and thought about Fatima.

He thought about her hands — surgeon’s hands, steady and precise, hands that had cut a pearl from a mermaid’s stomach and cauterized his stump and built him prosthetics from driftwood and wire. He thought about how she fed him when he couldn’t feed himself after losing the first hand, the intimacy of it, the humiliation and the tenderness braided together like rope. He thought about how she would feed him again, now, after this, if he did this, and how the feeding would become their language, their communion, their version of the karak chai she’d poured at the Mulberry Tree —
first cup for the guest, second for friendship, third to seal the deal
.

He thought about Omar, who would make a joke. Something about Kian being “all thumbs” except without the thumbs. Something terrible and perfect and exactly what Kian would need to hear.

He thought about the dead boy in the slave ship. The boy who had asked for a story. The boy Kian had failed with his silence. And he thought:
I have no hands. I have no memories. I have no home, no career, no wife, no world. But I have a voice. I have a story. And if I can carry thirteen pearls and a crew of broken people to the end of this impossible quest, then the boy’s death means something. Then my silence means something. Then all of it — the hands, the memories, the coffee, the rain, the father’s face, the mother’s voice — all of it was the price of a story worth telling.

“Take it,” Kian said.

The Simurgh extended one talon — iridescent, gentle, precise as a surgeon’s scalpel — and touched Kian’s right wrist.

There was no pain.

That was the cruelty of it. The Crawling Reef had taken his left hand with violence — the crush of coral, the scream, the blood, the cauterization. That loss had been honest. It had hurt the way loss is supposed to hurt, with fire and noise and the body’s animal protest against its own diminishment.

The Simurgh took his right hand with kindness. The talon touched his wrist, and the hand simply
wasn’t there anymore
. No blood. No wound. No stump. The wrist ended in smooth skin, sealed and healed, as if the hand had never existed, as if Kian had been born this way, as if the universe had quietly revised his blueprints and removed a feature that was no longer supported.

He looked at his arms. Two stumps. Two absences. Two places where the world ended and Kian began, or where Kian ended and the world began — the distinction, he realized, was becoming academic.

“Show me,” he said. His voice was steady. He was proud of that. Later, alone, he would scream. Later, on the Dhow, he would weep. Later, Fatima would hold him — hold his arms, his shoulders, his face, everything she could reach, everything that was left — and he would shake against her the way he had shaken after the reef, and she would be patient, and Omar would bring arak, and Umm Hani would bring food, and the crew would surround him with the only thing they had to offer, which was presence, which was enough, which was everything.

But now, in the garden, he was steady. And the Simurgh showed him.

The vision was not a dream. It was not a prophecy. It was a
reading
— the Simurgh opening the book of Kian’s future and letting him see the pages. But the pages were torn, and the ink ran, and the language shifted between scripts he could read and scripts he couldn’t, and the reading was like trying to see through deep water — shapes and light and the suggestion of meaning, never the meaning itself.

He saw the pearls. All thirty. Arranged in a shape he recognized but couldn’t name — something anatomical, something hungry, something that clicked and locked and hummed with a vibration that was not sound but
appetite
, and the appetite was waking, and the waking shattered something he couldn’t see.

He saw Fatima. Older. Her hand was steady — it was always steady. She was doing something precise. Something surgical. Something that required the steadiness. He couldn’t see what. The vision blurred around her hands, as if the future itself refused to be specific about what those hands would do.

He saw himself. Not dying —
changing
. His edges dissolving. His blood thinning into something that tasted of salt and smelled of cardamom. His memories — the boy, the village, the Mulberry Tree, Fatima’s laugh, Omar’s jokes — becoming something
else
, something larger, something that was not a man but was not nothing either. The vision wouldn’t show him what he became. It could only show him the becoming — the process, not the result.

He saw water. He saw loneliness. He saw a vastness that ached.

And he saw a small boat on a silver sea. Someone pouring something into the water. The water rising to meet it. The rising was tender. The rising was terrible. He couldn’t see who was in the boat. He couldn’t see what was being poured. He could only feel the love in it — a love so fierce it crossed the boundary between forms, between states of being, between the living and whatever came after living.

The vision ended.

Kian was on his knees in the garden. His face was wet. He hadn’t felt himself crying — without hands, he couldn’t wipe the tears, and they ran freely down his cheeks and into his beard and onto the grass, where they were absorbed instantly, the garden drinking his grief the way the sea drank everything.

The Simurgh placed the pearl before him. Black, perfect, whispering its fragment of a dead god’s hunger. Fourteen now. The jaw was learning to bite. Kian could feel it in his sleep now — the closing, the hunger, the patience of a mouth that had waited millennia and could wait a little longer.

“You see now,”
the Simurgh said. Its voice was gentle. The gentleness was worse than cruelty.
“You see the shape of the ending. Not the details — the details are yours to discover. But the shape. You will walk toward it anyway. This is not bravery. This is not stupidity. This is love — the willingness to be destroyed by something you cannot hold, for the sake of something you cannot keep. The scholars call it sacrifice. The poets call it devotion. I call it the only interesting thing your species has ever done.”

“How do I carry this?” Kian whispered. “How do I go back to that ship and look at them and eat their food and laugh at their jokes and love them, knowing what I know? Knowing that I’m walking them toward—”

“Toward what? Their deaths? They were always walking toward their deaths. Every living thing walks toward its death from the moment it draws breath. You have not changed their destination. You have only seen the road. And seeing the road—”
The Simurgh spread its wings, and the garden blazed with color, with language, with the accumulated knowledge of every civilization that had ever looked up at the stars and asked
why
.
“Seeing the road is the gift. Not because it makes the journey easier. Because it makes the journey
meaningful
. You will love them harder now. You will taste the food more deeply. You will laugh louder and hold tighter and forgive faster, because you know what’s coming, and knowing what’s coming is the only thing that makes the present precious.”

The Simurgh folded its wings. The garden dimmed. The stream resumed its flow. The flowers returned to their impossible colors.

“Go,”
the Simurgh said.
“Take the tooth. Climb down. Feed your crew. Tell them nothing. Let them have their ignorance — it is kinder than your knowledge, and kindness, in the end, is the only wisdom worth having.”

Kian picked up the pearl. Not with his hands — he had no hands. He pressed his forearms together, trapping the pearl between his stumps, and lifted it, and the gesture was awkward and graceless and human in a way that made the Simurgh’s thirty eyes blink in something that might have been admiration.

He turned to go.

“Kian.”

He stopped.

“The boy in the slave ship. The one who asked for a story.”

Kian’s throat closed.

“His name was Darius. He liked dates. He wanted to be a sailor. He would have been a good one.”
A pause.
“You couldn’t have saved him. But you could have told him a story. Remember that. When the end comes — and it will come, and it will hurt, and you will be afraid — remember that the story is the thing. Not the ending. The telling.”

Kian descended the mountain with no hands and a pearl trapped between his arms and a vision of his own death burning behind his eyes like a star that had already exploded but whose light hadn’t yet arrived.

The crew was waiting on the beach.

Fatima saw him first. She saw the arms — both ending in stumps now, symmetrical in their absence, a man unmade from the extremities inward — and her face did something that Kian would remember for the rest of his life, which was not long enough and was too long. Her face
broke
. Not into tears, not into anger, but into a grief so pure it bypassed expression entirely and rearranged the architecture of her features.

She didn’t cry. She walked into the water — knee-deep, waist-deep, not caring about the wet, not caring about anything except reaching him — and she took his arms, both of them, the stumps, the absences, and she held them against her chest, and she pressed her forehead to his, and she breathed.

Her hands were cold. Not the cold of the water — a different cold, the cold that lived inside her now, the cold that made her healing precise and her touch clinical and her love something that cut as cleanly as it held. Kian felt it. He didn’t say anything. But he felt it — the difference between the warmth she’d had before the volcano and the efficiency she had now.

“I’m here,” she said. “I’m here. I’m here.”

“I know,” he said. “I know.”

Omar stood on the beach with his arms crossed and his jaw working and his eyes bright with something he would deny was tears. He said nothing for a long time. Then he said, “Well. At least you’re symmetrical now.” And his voice cracked on the last word, and he turned away, and Kian loved him so fiercely in that moment that it felt like a physical force, like gravity, like the pull of the sea.

Amir brought the pearl aboard. Umm Hani was already cooking.

That night, in the captain’s cabin — Qadir’s cabin, though Kian still couldn’t think of it as his own — Fatima built him new prosthetics. She worked by lamplight, her surgeon’s hands steady despite everything, fashioning two devices from driftwood and copper wire and strips of leather cut from an old belt. They were crude. They were functional. They were, in their rough-hewn practicality, the most beautiful things Kian had ever owned.

“They won’t hold a sword,” Fatima said, fitting the left one to his stump, adjusting the straps, testing the grip mechanism she’d designed — a spring-loaded clamp that could close around objects when he pressed his forearm forward. “They won’t write. They won’t—” She stopped. Swallowed. “They won’t hold me.”

“They don’t need to hold you,” Kian said. “You hold me. That’s how it works. That’s how it’s always worked.”

She looked at him. The lamp threw shadows across her face, and in the shadows he could see the burns, the scars, the missing tooth, the map of everything this world had done to her. And beneath the map — beneath the damage, beneath the fury, beneath the cold patience that had replaced her warmth — he could see
her
. The woman who had poured karak chai in a tavern built around a mulberry tree and said,
“Curiosity is just hunger wearing a scholar’s robe.”
She was still there. Buried, but there.

“What did the Simurgh show you?” she asked.

“The ending.”

“Was I in it?”

He looked at her. He thought about the vision — the knife, the dissolution, the sea, the small boat, the karak chai poured into waves that rose to meet her. He thought about the Simurgh’s instruction:
Tell them nothing. Let them have their ignorance.

“Yes,” he said. “You were in it.”

“Was it—” She stopped. Started again. “Was it a good ending?”

Kian thought about this. He thought about what
good
meant, in a story about a man who became the sea. He thought about Tiamat, who had done the same thing — died to become a prison, sacrificed her existence to cage a god — and whether Tiamat would have called her ending good. He thought about the boy, Darius, who had wanted to be a sailor and had died of dirty water in a slave ship’s hold, and whether any ending that gave Darius’s death meaning could be called anything other than good, even if the meaning was purchased with Kian’s life.

“It was an ending,” he said. “I think that’s the best any of us can hope for. Not good or bad. Just — complete.”

Fatima studied him. She knew he was holding something back.

She didn’t press. She finished fitting the prosthetics, tested them, adjusted them, and then she did something she hadn’t done since before the inquisitors. She leaned forward and kissed him. Not on the mouth — on the forehead. The way a mother kisses a child. The way a priest blesses a pilgrim. The way you say goodbye to someone who is already leaving, even though they’re still in the room.

“Eat,” she said. “Umm Hani made mansaf.”

Umm Hani had made mansaf.

The lamb had been slow-cooked in fermented yogurt until it fell apart at the touch of a spoon — which was how Kian ate now, with spoons strapped to his prosthetics, the clamps gripping the handles with a mechanical determination that was nothing like the fluid grace of fingers but was, in its own way, sufficient. The yogurt sauce was rich and tangy, poured over a bed of rice studded with almonds and pine nuts, the whole thing served on a sheet of flatbread that soaked up the sauce and became, in the soaking, something greater than bread — a vessel, a foundation, a thing that held other things together.

Fatima fed him the pieces he couldn’t manage. The lamb that required tearing. The bread that required folding. She did it without comment, without ceremony, without the performative tenderness that would have made it charity. She fed him the way she did everything — practically, efficiently, with a precision that was its own form of love.

The crew ate in the captain’s cabin, crowded around Qadir’s table, and the table was too small for all of them, and they sat with their elbows touching and their knees bumping and their voices overlapping, and the closeness was not comfortable but it was necessary, the way a bandage is not comfortable but is necessary, the way pressure on a wound is not comfortable but keeps you alive.

Omar ate three servings. “The mansaf is exceptional,” he told Umm Hani. “The lamb is tender. The sauce is perfect. The rice is—” He paused, searching for the word. “The rice is
architectural
. Each grain supports the others. It’s a community of rice. A rice civilization.”

“You’re drunk,” Umm Hani said.

“I am not drunk. I am
appreciative
. There is a difference. Drunk men slur. Appreciative men use metaphors about rice.”

“You’re drunk and appreciative.”

“This is also possible.”

Amir ate quietly, watching Kian with those too-old eyes. Kian watched him back. Since the Peri islands, something had shifted between them — the unspoken acknowledgment that Amir’s secrets had a shape, and the shape was getting harder to ignore.

Samira didn’t eat. She sat in the corner with her shawl pulled tight and her eyes on Fatima.

Kian ate the mansaf. He tasted the lamb and the yogurt and the rice and the almonds, and each flavor was sharper than it had been before the mountain, more vivid, more present, as if the Simurgh’s vision had recalibrated his senses, had turned up the volume on the world to compensate for the hands he’d lost. The food was extraordinary. The food was ordinary. The food was mansaf, made by a Yemeni woman on a pirate ship at the edge of the world, and it was the best thing Kian had ever eaten, and it was just food, and the contradiction was the point.

He couldn’t hold the cup of karak chai. Fatima held it for him. She tipped it to his lips — cardamom, saffron, condensed milk — and he drank, and she watched him drink, and neither of them spoke.

Fourteen pearls. Fourteen teeth in a jaw that was learning to bite.

And Kian, handless, scarred, carrying a vision of his own death like a stone in his chest, looked at his crew — his family, his found and broken and beautiful family — and thought:
I will walk toward the ending. I will walk toward it with these people. And when it comes, I will not be silent. I will tell the story. I will tell all of it — the boy, the village, the reef, the mountain, the sea. And the telling will be enough. The telling has to be enough.

Outside, the Sea of Tears lapped against the Dhow’s hull, patient and hungry and made of everyone who had ever wept.

Inside, Umm Hani poured more chai.
