Chapter 5A: The Mulberry Tree

“A full stomach is a fortress against despair.” — Umm Hani

They stayed at the Mulberry Tree until the lanterns burned low and the oud player switched from melancholy to something slower, softer, a melody that wound through the branches of the tree like smoke and settled into the bones like warmth.

It was Omar who ordered the second round of food. Not because anyone was still hungry — but because, as he explained with the gravity of a man delivering a philosophical treatise, “the first round feeds the body. The second round feeds the soul. And the soul, unlike the body, has no stomach to tell it when to stop.”

“That’s not philosophy,” Leila said. “That’s gluttony.”

“Gluttony is philosophy for people who understand that life is short and food is good. I refuse to apologize for my appetites. They are the most honest thing about me.” He waved to Umm Salma, who was already approaching with a tray, because Umm Salma had been feeding Omar for years and knew his patterns the way a meteorologist knows weather — predictable in broad strokes, surprising in specifics, and always, always excessive.

The second round was different from the first. The kabsa and harees had been substantial — main courses, anchor dishes, the kind of food that fills you and grounds you and reminds you that you have a body and the body has needs. The second round was intimate. Smaller dishes, richer flavors, the kind of food you eat slowly, with your hands, while talking about things that matter.

Fatima ordered for the table this time. She did it with the quiet authority of someone who understood food not as sustenance but as language — each dish a word, each combination a sentence, the meal as a whole a conversation conducted in flavor and texture and scent.

“Mutabbaq,” she said, as Umm Salma set down a plate of thin pastry parcels, golden and crisp, filled with spiced lamb and onions and a whisper of egg. “My mother made these on Fridays. She said the folding was a meditation — you fold the dough, you fold your thoughts, you fold your worries into something small enough to eat.” She picked one up, broke it in half. Steam escaped, fragrant with cumin and black pepper. “Eat it while it’s hot. Cold mutabbaq is a tragedy.”

Kian bit into one. The pastry shattered — not crumbled,
shattered
, like thin glass, giving way to a filling that was savory and rich and slightly sweet from the caramelized onions. The lamb was tender, spiced with a blend that his bahr-sense identified as cumin, coriander, cinnamon, and something floral — rosewater, maybe, or the memory of rosewater, which in this world might have been the same thing.

“Good?” Fatima asked.

“Good.”

“Good.” She almost smiled. Almost. The corners of her mouth moved, and her eyes warmed, and then the expression was gone, replaced by the surgeon’s neutral assessment. But Kian had seen it, and the seeing felt like finding a coin in the sand — small, bright, worth keeping.

Omar was already on his third mutabbaq. “The secret,” he said, chewing with the enthusiasm of a man who believed that food deserved to be heard as well as tasted, “is the onions. You cook them until they surrender. Until they give up their sharpness and become sweet. This is a metaphor for life, but I’m too full to explain it properly.”

“Everything is a metaphor for life with you,” Leila said. She had eaten one mutabbaq, precisely, and was now drinking tea with the controlled sips of someone who rationed pleasure the way other people rationed water.

“Because life is a meal, Leila. A long, complicated, occasionally indigestible meal, and the only question is whether you eat it or it eats you.” He reached for a fourth mutabbaq. Leila moved the plate out of his reach. He looked at her with genuine betrayal. “That is an act of war.”

“That is an act of mercy. Your stomach will thank me.”

“My stomach has never thanked anyone for anything. It is an ungrateful organ. Like the heart, but louder.”

The next dish was balaleet — sweet vermicelli noodles cooked with sugar, cardamom, and saffron, topped with a thin omelet folded like a blanket over the golden nest of pasta. It was a breakfast dish served at night, which Fatima said was deliberate.

“Balaleet is for beginnings,” she said. “For the first meal of the day, when everything is possible and nothing has gone wrong yet. Eating it at night is — an act of faith. A declaration that tomorrow will come, and it will be worth waking up for.”

Kian tasted it. The noodles were sweet and fragrant, the saffron turning them the color of sunrise, the cardamom adding a warmth that was almost physical. The omelet was thin and delicate, barely set, more suggestion than substance. Together, they were — he searched for the word —
hopeful
. The dish tasted hopeful. In a world of bleeding maps and burning villages and gods sleeping in the sea, someone had made a dish that tasted like hope, and that was either the bravest or the most foolish thing Kian had encountered in Al-Khaliq.

“My mother—” Fatima began, and then stopped. She looked at her plate. The surgeon’s composure flickered — a crack in the professional facade, quickly sealed, but visible. “My mother made balaleet every morning. Every single morning. Even when we had nothing else. Even when the flour was gone and the sugar was gone and the cardamom was a memory. She’d find a way. She’d trade for it, beg for it, steal for it if she had to. Because breakfast mattered. Because the first thing you eat in the morning tells your body what kind of day it’s going to be, and my mother refused to let any day begin without sweetness.”

“She sounds remarkable,” Kian said.

“She was a kitchen witch. Every dish she made contained a spell. The magic was love. The cost was hunger — hers, not ours. She ate last. She ate least. She gave us everything and kept nothing, and when she died, she weighed less than the pots she cooked in.” Fatima’s voice was steady. Her hands were steady. But her eyes were bright, and the brightness was not the lantern light. “I became a surgeon because I couldn’t be her. I couldn’t cook the way she cooked — with that faith, that generosity, that willingness to pour yourself into a pot and serve yourself to others. I could cut. I could stitch. I could fix what was broken. But I couldn’t
feed
. Not the way she did.”

The table was quiet. The oud player had stopped. Even Omar was still, a mutabbaq halfway to his mouth, frozen in the rare posture of a man who has encountered something more important than food.

“You feed us now,” Amir said softly. “Not with food. With — with keeping us alive. With stitching us back together when the world tears us apart. That’s feeding, Fatima. That’s the same thing.”

Fatima looked at the cabin boy. Something passed between them — a recognition, a gratitude, a connection that Kian couldn’t fully read but could feel through the bahr-sense as a warmth, a brightness, a small flame in the dark.

“Eat your balaleet,” she said. “It’s getting cold.”

They ate. The oud player resumed. The mulberry tree swayed in a breeze that smelled of fruit and salt and the particular, indefinable scent of a moment that would be remembered — not by the sea, not by magic, but by the people who lived it, in the ordinary, mortal, irreplaceable way that humans remember the moments that matter.

Omar told a joke.

This was not unusual — Omar told jokes the way other people breathed, constantly and without apparent effort. But this joke was different. This joke was
performed
, with the full weight of Omar’s considerable theatrical talent, delivered to the table with the timing and commitment of a man who understood that comedy, like cooking, was an act of generosity.

But first, there was the matter of the bill.

Umm Salma brought it on a copper tray, folded once, the way executioners fold the warrant. Omar opened it. His face underwent a transformation that Kian would later describe as geological — layers of emotion shifting, colliding, forming new and terrible landscapes. Joy became confusion. Confusion became outrage. Outrage became a kind of philosophical despair that only a man who understood the precise value of money could achieve.

“This is robbery,” Omar said. “This is — this is
art
. The artistry of the theft is almost admirable. She’s charging us for the air. She’s charging us for the
ambiance
.”

“You ate enough for six people,” Leila said.

“I ate enough for
one
Omar. There is a difference. The world has not yet calibrated its portions to accommodate men of my appetites, and this is a failure of civilization, not of my stomach.”

“Pay the bill, Omar.”

“I will pay the bill. I will pay it with the dignity of a man being robbed in broad lamplight. But I want it noted —
noted
, Kian, you’re a scholar, note this — that Umm Salma has charged us double for the balaleet. Double. For a breakfast dish served at night. She’s invented a surcharge for temporal displacement. The woman is a genius of extortion.”

Umm Salma, who had been listening from behind the bar with the serene expression of a woman who had been overcharging Omar for years and intended to continue, called out: “The surcharge is for the saffron. Saffron is expensive. Your complaints are free.”

Omar paid. He paid with the theatrical suffering of a martyr, counting each coin as if it were a drop of his own blood, and when he was done, he looked at the empty purse and then at the table and then at the ceiling, and he said, with the gravity of a man delivering a eulogy: “I have been murdered. Financially murdered. My ghost will haunt this tavern. My ghost will eat for free.”

“Your ghost would still find a way to complain about the portions,” Leila said.

Amir, who had been watching this exchange with the bright-eyed attention of a child learning how adults performed the ritual of commerce, said: “Omar, you have three more purses hidden in your robe. I can see the bulges.”

The table went silent. Omar looked at Amir. Amir looked at Omar. The cabin boy’s expression was innocent in the way that a knife is innocent — technically blameless, functionally dangerous.

“The boy,” Omar said slowly, “is a menace. A small, observant, financially literate menace. I am going to have to start hiding my money in more creative locations.”

“You could try honesty,” Fatima suggested.

“Honesty is the most expensive hiding place of all. No one looks there because no one believes it exists.” He tucked the purse away — the empty one, conspicuously, while the three full ones remained where Amir had identified them. “Now. May I tell my joke? Or does someone else wish to audit my personal finances?”

“Tell the joke,” Kian said. “Before Amir finds your fourth purse.”

Omar’s eyes narrowed. “There is no fourth purse.”

There was absolutely a fourth purse.

“A djinn, a sailor, and a merchant walk into a tavern,” he began, settling into his cushions with the air of a man preparing for a long and important journey. “The tavern is much like this one — beautiful, warm, full of food and music and the smell of good things. The djinn is hungry. The sailor is thirsty. The merchant is calculating the tavern’s profit margins.”

Leila sighed. “I’ve heard this one.”

“You have not heard this one. This is a new version. I have improved it. The improvements are significant.”

“Your improvements are never significant.”

“My improvements are always significant. You lack the aesthetic sensitivity to appreciate them. This is not your fault. You were raised by wolves.”

“I was raised by slavers.”

“Same thing, but wolves have better table manners. May I continue?”

Leila waved a hand. Omar continued.

“The djinn says to the tavern keeper: ‘I will grant you one wish in exchange for a meal.’ The tavern keeper, who is wise, says: ‘What kind of meal?’ The djinn says: ‘The best meal you have.’ The tavern keeper brings kabsa — magnificent kabsa, with saffron and lamb and raisins and almonds, the kind of kabsa that makes angels weep and poets give up poetry because no words can do it justice.”

“This is just you describing kabsa,” Fatima observed.

“All great stories are, at their core, descriptions of kabsa. This is a literary truth that scholars have overlooked. May I
continue
?”

“Please.”

“The djinn eats the kabsa. The djinn is satisfied. The djinn says: ‘Your wish.’ The tavern keeper thinks. He thinks for a long time. He thinks about wealth, about power, about love, about immortality. And then he says: ‘I wish for the recipe.’ The djinn is confused. ‘The recipe?’ ‘For the kabsa. I want to make it myself. I want to make it every day. I want to feed people this kabsa until the end of time.’ The djinn grants the wish. The tavern keeper makes the kabsa. It is perfect. It is always perfect. People come from across the sea to eat it. The tavern keeper becomes the most famous cook in Al-Khaliq.”

Omar paused. The table waited.

“The sailor,” Omar continued, “says to the djinn: ‘I want a wish too.’ The djinn says: ‘You didn’t feed me.’ The sailor says: ‘I’ll buy you a drink.’ The djinn considers this. ‘What kind of drink?’ The sailor buys arak. Good arak. The djinn drinks. The djinn is satisfied. ‘Your wish.’ The sailor says: ‘I wish for fair winds, forever.’ The djinn grants it. The sailor sails. The winds are always fair. He becomes the greatest sailor in Al-Khaliq. He never faces a storm. He never faces a challenge. He never faces anything. He sails in circles, bored, until he throws himself into the sea out of sheer tedium.”

“That’s not funny,” Kian said.

“It’s not finished. The merchant says to the djinn: ‘I want a wish.’ The djinn says: ‘You didn’t feed me or buy me a drink.’ The merchant says: ‘I’ll give you something better. I’ll give you advice.’ The djinn laughs. ‘What advice could a mortal give a djinn?’ The merchant says: ‘Stop granting wishes. Every wish you grant makes someone miserable. The tavern keeper is a slave to his kitchen. The sailor is a slave to his boredom. Wishes are prisons. Stop building prisons.’ The djinn thinks about this. The djinn thinks for a very long time. And then the djinn eats the merchant and takes the tavern.”

Silence.

“This is why I never trust wishes,” Omar said, reaching for his arak. “Only invoices. Invoices are honest. Invoices tell you exactly what you owe. Wishes tell you what you want and charge you what you can’t afford.”

Kian laughed. It surprised him — the sound erupting from his chest like a cough, unexpected and uncontrolled. He laughed, and the laugh was real, and it was the first real laugh he’d produced in this world.

Fatima looked at him. She was smiling — actually smiling, not the almost-smile of the surgeon but a real smile, wide and warm and slightly crooked because of the broken nose.

“You should laugh more,” she said. “It suits you.”

“There hasn’t been much to laugh about.”

“There’s always something to laugh about. That’s the secret the darkness doesn’t want you to know — that laughter exists in the same world as horror, and neither one cancels the other out. You can laugh at a funeral. You can cry at a wedding. The emotions don’t compete. They coexist.” She poured more tea. “My mother laughed every day. Every single day, even the worst ones. She said laughter was the only spell that cost nothing.”

“Your mother was wise.”

“My mother was hungry, and tired, and poor, and she laughed anyway. That’s not wisdom. That’s defiance.” She set the teapot down. “Third cup, scholar. The deal-sealing cup. What deal are we sealing?”

Kian looked at the table — at Omar, who was pouring arak with the ceremony of a priest performing a sacrament; at Leila, who was watching the room with one eye and the table with the other, her vigilance a form of care she would never admit to; at Amir, who was eating dates with the single-minded focus of a child who has learned that food is not guaranteed and should be consumed while available; at Fatima, whose smile was fading back into the surgeon’s composure but whose eyes were still warm.

“A crew that eats together fights together,” he said. He didn’t know where the words came from. They felt borrowed — from Omar, maybe, or from the tree, or from the sea itself, which remembered everything and occasionally shared.

Omar raised his glass. “A crew that eats together fights together. A crew that fights together dies together. But at least we die full.”

They drank. The arak was sharp and sweet and tasted of anise, and it burned going down, and the burn was good — a small, controlled fire in the chest, a warmth that spread outward and met the warmth of the tea and the food and the company, and for one evening, in a tavern built around an impossible tree in a city floating on a sea of tears, the horror was far away.

Not gone. Never gone. But far away. And sometimes, far away is enough.

The night deepened. The arak flowed. And the crew, loosened by drink and warmth and the particular honesty that only comes when you’re far enough from danger to pretend it doesn’t exist, began to talk about each other.

It started with Leila, who had been watching a merchant at the next table flirt badly with a serving girl, and who said, without preamble: “Qadir snores.”

“Everyone snores,” Omar said.

“Not like Qadir. Qadir snores like a man trying to swallow the sea in his sleep. The ship vibrates. I have timed it. The snoring follows the tides. At high tide, it is unbearable. At low tide, it is merely terrible. I have considered smothering him with a pillow. I have decided against it only because he is the captain and I would have to steer the ship, and I hate steering.”

“You could ask him to sleep on his side,” Kian offered.

“I have asked him. He said, and I quote: ‘The captain sleeps however the captain sleeps, and the crew adjusts.’ He then rolled over and the snoring resumed. I went to the deck and slept in the rigging. The rigging is uncomfortable. The rigging does not snore.”

“Fatima talks in her sleep,” Amir said, with the casual cruelty of a child who has not yet learned that some observations are better kept private.

Fatima’s head turned. Slowly. The way a cannon turns. “What.”

“You talk in your sleep. Medical terms, mostly. Last night you said ‘lateral incision’ three times and then ‘more sutures’ and then something in a language I don’t speak. It might have been a prayer. It might have been a curse. With you, the distinction is unclear.”

“I do not talk in my sleep.”

“You do. Omar heard it too.”

Omar, who had been trying to become invisible — a difficult feat for a man of his dimensions — held up his hands. “I heard nothing. I was asleep. I sleep deeply. I sleep the sleep of the innocent.”

“You sleep the sleep of the guilty,” Leila said. “You moan about debts. Last week you said ‘the interest rate is criminal’ in your sleep and then rolled over and said ‘but I’ll take it.’”

“That is — that is a private financial matter between myself and my subconscious, and I will thank you to—”

“And Kian,” Amir continued, because the cabin boy had apparently decided that tonight was the night for a full accounting of the crew’s nocturnal habits, “Kian doesn’t sleep at all. He sits on the deck and stares at the map on his arm and whispers to the Marid. It’s unsettling. The night watch has complained.”

“The night watch can mind their own business,” Kian said.

“The night watch says you sometimes laugh. Alone. In the dark. At nothing. They find this more disturbing than the whispering.”

Kian opened his mouth to defend himself and realized, with a sinking feeling, that the night watch was right. He did laugh. In the dark. At Zulfaqar’s jokes, which were terrible — the Marid’s sense of humor was approximately four thousand years old and had not aged well — but which were, in the small hours of the night, when the sea was dark and the teeth were whispering and the weight of the mission pressed down like a physical thing, the only thing that kept him from screaming.

“We are,” Omar said, surveying the table with the expression of a man taking inventory of damaged goods, “a crew of insomniacs, sleep-talkers, snorers, and men who laugh alone in the dark. We are, in short, exactly the kind of people you would expect to find hunting the teeth of a dead god on a sea made of tears. Normal people do not do this. Normal people sleep soundly and wake refreshed and eat breakfast without existential dread. We are not normal people. We are
this
.” He gestured at the table. “And I would not trade this for all the normal sleep in the world.”

“That’s because you snore too,” Leila said. “You just can’t hear yourself.”

“I do not snore.”

“You snore like a walrus giving birth. The entire ship knows. The ship itself knows. I believe the ship has developed a list to one side specifically to escape the sound.”

Omar looked genuinely wounded. “A walrus? A
walrus
? I am a man of considerable dignity and you compare my sleeping sounds to a walrus?”

“A walrus giving birth,” Leila corrected. “The distinction is important. Regular walrus snoring would be an improvement.”

The table dissolved into laughter — real laughter, the kind that hurts your stomach and makes your eyes water and leaves you gasping and, for a few moments, completely free.

The night deepened. The crowd thinned. The oud player packed his instrument and left, replaced by the sound of the sea against the barge’s hull — a gentle, rhythmic lapping that was, Kian realized, the sound of memories washing against wood, the Sea of Tears’ endless, quiet narration of lives lived and lost.

Kian and Fatima were the last two at the table. Omar had retired to the Dhow, claiming that his stomach required horizontal contemplation. Leila had gone to check the ship’s watch, because Leila’s idea of relaxation was ensuring that no one was about to attack. Amir had fallen asleep under the mulberry tree, curled in a nest of cushions, looking for once like the child he was supposed to be.

The lanterns were low. The tree’s branches made shadows on the reed walls that looked like writing in a language Kian almost understood. The air smelled of mulberries and salt and the ghost of cardamom from the tea.

“Why did you touch the map?” Fatima asked.

The question was quiet, casual, the kind of question you ask at the end of a long evening when the defenses are down and the truth is closer to the surface than usual.

“Curiosity,” Kian said.

“That’s what you tell yourself. What’s the real reason?”

He thought about it. Really thought, in the way that the Mulberry Tree seemed to encourage — honestly, without the protective layers of rationalization and self-justification that he usually wrapped around his motivations.

“Loneliness,” he said. “I was alone. I’d been alone for a long time — not physically, but in the way that matters. My wife left. My career stalled. My colleagues respected me but didn’t like me. My students needed me but didn’t know me. I was surrounded by people and connected to none of them. And then I found a map that bled, and it was the most interesting thing that had happened to me in years, and I touched it because — because being pulled into an impossible world felt less frightening than spending another evening alone in a cargo hold, cataloguing dead people’s garbage.”

Fatima was quiet for a long time. The sea lapped. The tree swayed.

“Curiosity is just hunger wearing a scholar’s robe,” she said.

Kian looked at her. “That’s — yes. That’s exactly right.”

“I know. I said it.” The almost-smile. “You were hungry, scholar. Not for food — for connection. For something real. For a world that demanded something of you, that needed you, that wouldn’t let you hide in your books and your artifacts and your safe, dead, catalogued past.” She met his eyes. “This world is many things. Cruel, dangerous, unfair, full of monsters and magic and gods that want to eat you. But it is not boring. And it will not let you be alone.”

“Is that a promise or a threat?”

“In Al-Khaliq, they’re the same thing.”

He laughed again. Softer this time, quieter, but real. And she smiled — the real smile, the one that made her beautiful — and for a moment, in the low lantern light, with the mulberry tree swaying overhead and the sea whispering beneath them and the sleeping cabin boy curled in his cushions like a question mark, Kian felt something he hadn’t felt since before the map, before the divorce, before the slow erosion of his capacity for connection.

He felt
present
. Not thinking about the past, not worrying about the future, not cataloguing or analyzing or rationalizing. Just here. In this moment. With this woman. In this impossible place.

Fatima reached across the table and took his hand. Her grip was firm — surgeon’s grip, strong and precise — and her skin was warm, and her fingers were calloused from years of holding scalpels and suturing wounds, and the touch was not romantic, not yet, but it was
real
, and in a world made of memory and magic and illusion, real was the rarest and most valuable thing.

“You should sleep,” she said. “Tomorrow we hunt. The captain has a lead on the next tooth — something in the southern waters, guarded by something unpleasant. You’ll need your strength.”

“I don’t want to sleep. I want to stay here.”

“Here will be here tomorrow. The tree doesn’t move. The tea doesn’t expire. And I—” She paused. Released his hand. The surgeon’s composure returned, but gently, like a door closing softly rather than slamming. “I’ll be here tomorrow too. I’m the ship’s surgeon. I’m not going anywhere.”

She stood. She gathered the sleeping Amir in her arms — the boy was light, bird-boned, and he didn’t wake, just nestled against her shoulder with the trust of a child who had found a safe place — and she carried him toward the gangplank.

At the bridge, she turned back. “Scholar?”

“Yes?”

“The tea. The third cup. The deal-sealing cup.” She looked at him across the tavern, across the low tables and the scattered cushions and the mulberry tree’s spreading branches, and her eyes were warm and her voice was steady and her words were precise, as always, chosen with a surgeon’s care. “The deal is this: we keep each other human. In a world that wants to turn us into monsters, we remind each other what we were before the teeth and the magic and the sea. That’s the deal. Can you keep it?”

“I can try.”

“Trying is enough. Trying is always enough.” She shifted Amir’s weight on her shoulder. “Goodnight, Kian.”

It was the first time she’d used his name.

He sat alone in the Mulberry Tree for a long time after she left, listening to the sea and the tree and the distant sounds of a city that never fully slept, and he thought about hunger and curiosity and loneliness and the way a woman’s hand felt when it held yours in a world where everything was trying to eat you.

And he thought:
This is what we’re fighting for. Not the teeth. Not the world. This. A table. A meal. A name spoken by someone who means it.

The mulberry tree swayed. A fruit fell into his lap — purple, ripe, warm from the lantern’s heat. He ate it. It tasted like the moment. Like the evening. Like the beginning of something that would end in tragedy and was worth beginning anyway.

He ate the mulberry, and he went back to the ship, and he slept without dreams for the first time since arriving in Al-Khaliq.

In the morning, they sailed south. The teeth were waiting. The sea was waiting. The world was waiting to be saved or devoured, and the crew of the Black Dhow — fed, rested, human — sailed to meet it.

Omar’s voice carried from the galley: “A crew that eats together fights together. Now eat your breakfast. We have a god to stop and I refuse to do it on an empty stomach.”
