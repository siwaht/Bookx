Chapter 7: The Cartographer’s Bones

“Understanding is the most expensive thing in the world. More expensive than gold, more expensive than magic, more expensive than love.” — Zulfaqar

Part II: The Hunt

The map on Kian’s arm was changing.

It had been a static thing — a tattoo, a chart, a fixed representation of a world he was learning to navigate. But since the contract with Zulfaqar, since the second tooth had joined the first in the captain’s chest, the map had become
dynamic
. It shifted with the tides, updated with the currents, and — most usefully, most disturbingly — it responded to pain.

Kian discovered this by accident. A rope burn on his palm, earned during a clumsy attempt to help with the rigging (Omar had been right: sailing was for sailors, and Kian’s contributions to the rigging were more hazard than help), and the map flared. The lines brightened, the coastlines sharpened, and for a brief, vivid moment, he could see the locations of every tooth in the world — scattered points of black light, pulsing with Ahriman’s hunger, spread across the Sea of Tears like a constellation of malice.

Then the pain faded, and the map dimmed, and the teeth vanished back into the cartographic dark.

“Pain,” Zulfaqar said, with the tone of a teacher explaining something obvious to a slow student.
The map is written in Tiamat’s blood. Blood responds to blood. Pain opens the channels. You want to read the map? Bleed.

“That’s barbaric.”

That’s physics. The physics of a world where magic runs on sacrifice and the currency is suffering. You wanted to understand this world, scholar. Understanding costs. Everything costs. I thought we’d established this.

Kian didn’t bleed. Not deliberately. But he kept the rope burn uncovered, and when the wind shifted and the salt air stung the raw skin, the map flickered, and he caught glimpses — fragments of a larger picture, pieces of a puzzle that was also a jaw.

“We need a cartographer,” he told Qadir. “Someone who mapped the teeth. Someone who knew the pattern.”

“Ibn Majid,” Qadir said. The name was spoken with the weight of legend — the way you’d say
Magellan
or
Columbus
in Kian’s world, except that Ibn Majid had navigated not oceans but the borders between the possible and the impossible. “The greatest navigator Al-Khaliq has ever produced. He mapped the Sea of Tears when it was young. He charted the teeth’s locations when they were first scattered. He understood the pattern.”

“Where is he?”

“Dead. Three hundred years dead. Buried on an island in the Nasnas Wastes — a desert archipelago where the half-men live.” Qadir’s expression suggested that the half-men were not a metaphor. “His tomb is there. His bones are there. And his knowledge — the map of all thirty teeth, the sequence in which they must be gathered, the pattern that Ahriman’s jaw follows — is there too. Locked in his skull. Waiting for someone with the right questions and the right price.”

“You want to talk to a dead man.”

“I want
you
to talk to a dead man. You’re the one with the Marid. Zulfaqar can raise the ghost — temporarily, painfully, expensively. But the ghost will speak. The dead always speak, scholar. They just need someone willing to listen.”

The Nasnas Wastes were three weeks’ sail to the east, through waters that grew progressively stranger as the Dhow left the main currents of the Sea of Tears and entered the shallows — regions where the sea was thin, where the memories were sparse and ancient, where the water was more water than memory and the world felt almost normal, almost like the world Kian had left behind.

Almost. The two moons still hung in the wrong sky. The stars still formed alien constellations. And the creatures that surfaced in the shallows — long, pale, eyeless things that might have been eels and might have been something worse — reminded Kian that
almost normal
was, in Al-Khaliq, as close to normal as you got.

The three weeks were not wasted. They were a renovation — his mind being gutted and rebuilt to accommodate knowledge that his old world had no framework for.

Samira taught him about witchcraft.

The witch had recovered with a speed that Fatima attributed to magic and Samira attributed to Umm Hani’s cooking. (“The bread,” Samira said, tearing a piece of kubaneh with fingers that were still thin but no longer trembling. “The bread is doing more for me than any spell. Tell your cook she’s a better healer than any witch I’ve known.” Umm Hani, when informed of this, said nothing but baked an extra loaf the next morning and left it outside Samira’s cabin door.)

Samira’s knowledge of Sihr was different from Zulfaqar’s. The Marid understood magic as a transaction — power exchanged for payment, services rendered for memories consumed. Samira understood magic as a
relationship
— a conversation between the mortal and the supernatural, conducted in a language of gesture, intention, and sacrifice.

“Nazia’s coven practices the old Sihr,” Samira explained. They were on the foredeck, the sun low, the sea calm. Samira sat cross-legged, her scarred hands resting on her knees, her storm-green eyes focused on something Kian couldn’t see. “Not the brute-force summoning that your Marid represents — no offense to Zulfaqar—”

Considerable offense taken,
Zulfaqar murmured from inside Kian’s chest.

“—but the subtle arts. The witchcraft of Tiamat’s daughters. We don’t bind spirits. We
speak
to the world. The sea, the wind, the stone, the fire — they’re not dead matter. They’re alive. They remember. And if you know how to ask, they’ll answer.”

“Ask how?”

“With sacrifice. Always with sacrifice — that part is universal. But the sacrifice doesn’t have to be memory. It can be blood. Hair. Tears. A song you’ll never sing again. A secret you’ll never tell. The currency is flexible. The exchange rate varies.” She held up her hand. On her palm, a scar — old, white, deliberate. “I gave the sea a secret to learn how to read the tides. I gave the wind a song to learn how to call storms. I gave the fire—” She closed her hand. “The fire took more than I offered. Fire always does.”

“What did it take?”

“My ability to feel warmth. I can see fire. I can hear it. I can smell it. But I can’t feel it. My skin registers heat as information, not sensation. I haven’t felt warm in fifteen years.” She looked at the sun — bright, hot, pouring warmth onto the deck. “I know the sun is warm. I can’t feel it. It’s like knowing a joke is funny but being unable to laugh.”

Kian thought about his wedding day — the gap in his memory, the blank space where the ceremony should be. “I understand.”

“I know you do. That’s why I’m teaching you.” She met his eyes. “The captain wants my knowledge of Nazia. He’ll get it. But I’m giving you something else — an understanding of Sihr that doesn’t require a Marid’s mediation. You’re the key, Kian. The map on your arm connects you to Tiamat’s system. You don’t need Zulfaqar to access it. You need to learn to access it yourself.”

She’s trying to make you independent of me,
Zulfaqar observed.
How inconvenient. And how wise.

Fatima joined the lessons. Not as a student — as a natural. The surgeon’s hands, trained to heal, adapted to witchcraft with a fluency that startled Samira and terrified Kian. Fatima could speak to fish within a week. She could read the wind within two. She could heal a wound by transferring it to a piece of driftwood — the wood would split and bleed, and the wound would close, and the cost was a whispered secret that Fatima breathed into the wood like a prayer.

“She’s gifted,” Samira told Kian, privately, on the foredeck at night. The witch’s voice was careful — the voice of someone delivering a diagnosis that was both good news and bad. “More gifted than anyone I’ve trained. More gifted than me. More gifted than Nazia, possibly, though Nazia has four centuries of practice.”

“That’s good, isn’t it?”

“It’s dangerous. Talent without training is a fire without a hearth — it burns everything, including the person who lit it. Fatima learns fast. Too fast. She’s not building foundations. She’s building towers. And towers without foundations—”

“Fall.”

“Spectacularly.” Samira looked at the sea. “I’ll train her. Properly. Slowly. But she’s impatient. She wants power — not for its own sake, but because she’s a healer, and healing in this world requires power, and she’s seen too many people die from things she could have fixed if she’d had the tools.” She paused. “That’s the most dangerous motivation for learning Sihr. Not greed. Not ambition. Compassion. Compassion makes you willing to pay any price, because the price is always less than watching someone die.”

Kian thought about the boy on the slave ship. About the village of Qasim. About all the prices he hadn’t paid and all the people who had paid instead.

“I know,” he said.

The Nasnas Wastes appeared on the horizon as a line of brown against the blue — low islands, flat and featureless, baked by a sun that seemed closer here, hotter. The sea around the islands was shallow and clear, the water so transparent that Kian could see the bottom — white sand, scattered coral, and bones. Human bones, picked clean and bleached by sun and salt.

“The Nasnas feed,” Omar said, standing beside Kian at the rail with the expression of a man who has been to a place before and did not enjoy the visit. “They eat travelers. Not for nutrition — for
completion
. The Nasnas are half-men. Literally. Split vertically — one arm, one leg, one eye, half a mouth. They hop. They’re fast. And they eat human flesh to become whole. Temporarily. The wholeness lasts a few hours, and then they split again, and they’re hungry again, and the cycle continues.”

“That’s Tuesday in Al-Khaliq,” Omar said. “The important thing is: they can be fought. They’re strong but fragile — one good blow and they split further, into quarters, into eighths. The problem is numbers. There are hundreds of them on these islands. Maybe thousands.” He looked at Kian. “The captain wants a small team. In and out. Find the tomb, raise the ghost, get the information, leave before the Nasnas realize we’re here.”

“And if they realize we’re here?”

“Then Leila earns her keep. Which she does enthusiastically.”

Leila, who had been sharpening her swords on the foredeck with the meditative focus of a woman performing a religious ritual, looked up. “I always earn my keep. Unlike some people who earn their keep by eating the keep.”

“I am the quartermaster. My keep is the keep. Eating it is quality control.”

“Quality control does not require eating an entire wheel of cheese in one sitting.”

“It was a small wheel. And the cheese was suspicious. I was investigating.”

“You were
gorging
. There is a difference. The difference is approximately one wheel of cheese.”

Amir, who had been listening from his usual position of being everywhere and nowhere simultaneously, said: “Omar also ate the dried figs that were meant for the Nasnas expedition. All of them. I packed them this morning. They’re gone.”

Omar’s face underwent a rapid series of expressions that ended, improbably, at righteous indignation. “The figs were substandard. I was protecting the expedition from substandard provisions. This is a quartermaster’s duty.”

“The figs were perfect. I selected them myself.”

“Your standards are those of a child. My standards are those of a professional. The figs were substandard. I have eaten the evidence. The matter is closed.”

Kian looked at the island of half-men and the tomb of a dead navigator and the mission that would determine the order in which they hunted the teeth of a sleeping god, and he thought:
We are going to die on an island of cannibals because our quartermaster ate all the figs.

“Can we focus?” he said.

“I am focused,” Omar said. “I am focused on the fact that we are about to visit an island of man-eating half-creatures with no figs, no cheese, and a scholar who can’t hold a sword. My focus is absolute. My focus is also terrified.”

“You don’t look terrified.”

“I look fat. Fat and terrified are visually similar. The difference is internal.”

The team was small: Kian, Leila, Amir, and two sailors — hard men with scarred hands and the quiet competence of people who had survived things they didn’t talk about. They went ashore at dawn, in a small boat, on a beach of white sand and white bone.

The island was flat and dry and empty — or appeared empty. The bahr-sense told a different story. Kian could feel them — the Nasnas, dozens of them, scattered across the island in clusters, their life-signatures strange and incomplete, like sentences missing their verbs. They were underground, mostly. Burrowed into the sand. Sleeping, or waiting, or whatever half-men did when they weren’t eating travelers.

“The tomb is inland,” Kian said, reading the map on his arm. The pain from the rope burn had faded, but the map retained some of its enhanced clarity — enough to show a structure at the island’s center, a concentration of old magic that could only be Ibn Majid’s resting place. “Half a mile. Maybe less.”

“Then we move fast,” Leila said. “And quietly. The Nasnas hunt by sound. They can hear a heartbeat at fifty paces.” She looked at Omar’s sailors. “Control your breathing. Control your steps. If you see one, don’t scream. Screaming is dinner bell.”

They moved. Across the white sand, between outcroppings of bleached coral, past the scattered bones of travelers who had come before and not left. The sun was brutal — a physical weight on Kian’s shoulders, pressing him into the sand. The air shimmered with heat. Nothing moved.

The tomb was a hole in the ground.

Not a grand mausoleum, not a monument, not the kind of structure that a legendary navigator might merit. A hole. A shaft, maybe three feet across, dropping straight down into the earth, lined with stones that were carved with the same impossible script that decorated Kian’s arm. The stones were old — older than the island, older than the sea, old enough that the carvings had been worn smooth by time and were readable only through the bahr-sense, which translated them not as words but as
warnings
.

Here lies Ibn Majid, Navigator of the Impossible, Cartographer of the Divine. He mapped the teeth of God and the bones of the Goddess and the distance between salvation and damnation, which is shorter than you think. Do not wake him. He is angry. He has been angry for three hundred years. The dead do not mellow with age.

“Cheerful,” Leila said, reading over Kian’s shoulder. She couldn’t read the script, but she could read Kian’s expression. “How do we get down?”

“We climb.” Kian looked at the shaft. It was narrow, dark, and deep — the bahr-sense showed it descending maybe twenty meters into the island’s bedrock, opening into a chamber at the bottom. “I go first. Alone. The ghost won’t speak to a crowd.”

“You go alone into a hole in the ground on an island full of man-eating half-men.” Leila’s tone suggested she found this plan suboptimal. “And if the Nasnas come while you’re down there?”

“Then you do what you do.”

She looked at him. Something in her expression shifted — not softening, exactly, but acknowledging. The scholar was learning. The scholar was willing to go into the dark alone. This was not bravery — Leila knew bravery, and this wasn’t it. This was necessity accepted without complaint, which was, in her experience, more useful than bravery and considerably rarer.

“Go,” she said. “Fast. I’ll hold the surface.”

Kian descended.

The shaft was tight — tight enough that Kian’s shoulders scraped the carved stones, tight enough that claustrophobia was a physical reality, the walls pressing in from all sides. He climbed down using handholds carved into the stone — ancient, worn, designed for smaller hands — and the darkness closed over him like water.

At the bottom, the shaft opened into a chamber.

It was circular, maybe ten meters across, with a domed ceiling carved from the bedrock and a floor of packed earth. In the center, a sarcophagus — not stone, not wood, but coral. Living coral, grown into the shape of a coffin, still alive, still growing, its surface covered in polyps that pulsed with bioluminescent light. The coral was the same deep red as the veins in the Dhow’s hull, and it hummed with the same low vibration, and Kian understood that the ship and the tomb were connected — both grown from the same source, both alive in the same way, both part of a system that was older than human civilization and more complex than human understanding.

Inside the coral sarcophagus, visible through the translucent growth, were bones. A skeleton, arranged in the traditional position — on its right side, facing Mecca, or whatever direction Mecca occupied in a world with two moons and a broken sky. The bones were dark with age, stained by the coral’s minerals, and they were — Kian’s bahr-sense confirmed — saturated with Sihr. Ibn Majid had been a navigator of the impossible, and the impossible had soaked into his bones the way salt soaks into wood, preserving them, sustaining them, keeping them ready for a conversation that the navigator had known, three centuries ago, would eventually need to happen.

“Zulfaqar,” Kian said. “I need to speak to him.”

The cost will be significant. Raising a ghost of this power — a ghost that has been dead for three centuries and is, as the warning suggested, angry — requires more than a pleasant memory. It requires something structural. Something load-bearing.

“Name it.”

The memory of your mother’s voice. Not her face — you’ll keep that. Not her name — you’ll keep that too. But her voice. The sound of her calling you for dinner. The sound of her singing in the kitchen. The sound of her saying your name the way only she said it, with that particular inflection that meant ‘I love you’ and ‘I’m worried about you’ and ‘eat your vegetables’ all at once.

Kian closed his eyes. He could hear it — his mother’s voice, warm and slightly hoarse from years of teaching, calling
Kian-jan
from the kitchen, the Persian diminutive that she used only with him, that no one else in the world used, that was as unique and irreplaceable as a fingerprint.

“Take it,” he said.

The voice went. Like the wedding day — gently, completely, irreversibly. One moment he could hear her. The next, silence. He knew she had a voice. He knew she called him
Kian-jan
. He could not hear it. The sound was gone, replaced by a gap that was shaped exactly like love and filled with exactly nothing.

The coral sarcophagus cracked.

Not broke — cracked, the way an egg cracks, a single line running from end to end, and from the crack, light — not the coral’s bioluminescence but something colder, bluer, the light of a mind that had been thinking in the dark for three hundred years and had a great deal to say.

The ghost of Ibn Majid rose from his bones like steam from a cup.

He was — had been — a small man. Slight, dark-skinned, with a beard that in life had probably been neat and in death was a wisp of blue-white light that trailed from his chin like smoke. His eyes were the most solid thing about him — dark, sharp, furious, the eyes of a man who had been woken from a very long sleep and was not pleased about it.

“WHO,” the ghost said, and the word was not spoken but
projected
, filling the chamber with a force that made the coral vibrate and the earth tremble and Kian’s teeth ache, “DARES DISTURB THE REST OF IBN MAJID, NAVIGATOR OF THE SEVEN SEAS AND THE SEVEN HEAVENS AND THE SEVEN HELLS AND THE SPACES BETWEEN?”

“Kian Farahani,” Kian said. His voice was steady. He was proud of that. “Maritime archaeologist. I need your maps.”

The ghost stared at him. The fury in its eyes was joined by something else — confusion, curiosity, the disorientation of a consciousness that had been dormant for centuries and was trying to process a world that had changed in its absence.

“An archaeologist,” Ibn Majid said, and the volume dropped from thunderous to merely loud. “A digger of graves. A reader of bones. You’ve come to read mine.”

“I’ve come to read your maps. The map of the thirty teeth. The sequence. The pattern.”

“The pattern.” The ghost’s expression shifted — from fury to something more complex, more layered. Bitterness, perhaps. Or regret. “I spent forty years mapping the pattern. Forty years sailing the Sea of Tears, charting the locations of Ahriman’s teeth, calculating the sequence in which they must be gathered to prevent the jaw from closing prematurely. Forty years of my life, and do you know what I found?”

“Tell me.”

“I found that the pattern is a trap. The teeth are not scattered randomly. They are arranged in a sequence — a mathematical sequence, a progression that follows rules as precise and as merciless as the rules of navigation. Gather them in the right order, and the jaw assembles slowly, controllably, giving the gatherer time to prepare for the final tooth. Gather them in the wrong order, and the jaw snaps shut — instantly, irrevocably, and the gatherer becomes the first thing Ahriman eats.”

Kian’s blood went cold. “We’ve already gathered two. Out of order?”

“How would I know? I’ve been dead for three centuries. Show me what you have.”

Kian extended his arm. The map glowed — brighter in the ghost’s presence, responding to the navigator’s Sihr the way a compass responds to a magnet. Ibn Majid leaned close, his spectral face inches from Kian’s skin, his dead eyes reading the living map with an intensity that was almost physical.

“The first tooth — the one your captain carries. Where did he find it?”

“I don’t know. He’s had it for eleven years.”

“And the second?”

“The Isle of Safar. A merchant prince wore it as a ring.”

Ibn Majid was quiet for a long time. The ghost’s form flickered — dimming, brightening, the way a candle flickers in a draft. He was thinking. Three hundred years of death had not diminished his capacity for calculation.

“The sequence,” he said finally, “is based on the positions of the stars at the moment of Ahriman’s imprisonment. The teeth were scattered according to a celestial pattern — each tooth placed at a location that corresponded to a specific star’s position in the sky at the moment Tiamat became the sea. To gather them safely, you must follow the same pattern — star by star, tooth by tooth, in the order that the sky dictated.”

“But the stars have moved,” Kian said. “In three hundred years, stellar positions shift. Precession of the equinoxes. Proper motion. The pattern you mapped is no longer accurate.”

Ibn Majid stared at him. “You understand precession?”

“I’m an astronomer. Well — an archaeologist who uses astronomy. Archaeoastronomy. I study how ancient cultures used the stars for navigation and calendar-keeping.”

The ghost’s expression underwent a transformation that Kian would remember for the rest of his life — however long that turned out to be. The fury drained away. The bitterness drained away. What remained was something raw and unexpected:
hope
. The hope of a man who had spent forty years solving a problem and died before he could account for the one variable he couldn’t control — time — and who had just met someone who could.

“You can recalculate,” Ibn Majid said. “You can adjust my maps for three centuries of stellar drift. You can find the current positions.”

“I can.” Kian paused. The word sat in the air between them — small, certain, and for the first time since he’d arrived in Al-Khaliq,
powerful
. Not the power of a Marid or a witch or a captain with a god’s tooth. The power of a man who knew something no one else in this world knew. “And I will. But I need you to understand something first.”

The ghost flickered. Impatient. “I have limited time, archaeologist. The energy your memory purchased is—”

“This is important.” Kian stepped closer to the sarcophagus. “Since I arrived in this world, I’ve been a passenger. A tool. The captain uses my knowledge when it’s convenient. The Marid feeds on my memories. Everyone around me has power — magical power, martial power, the power of experience in a world I don’t understand. I have none of that. What I have is
this
.” He tapped his temple. “Fifteen years of training in how stars move. How ancient navigation systems work. How to read a celestial chart and project it forward or backward through time. In my world, this knowledge was academic. Interesting at conferences. Useless everywhere else.”

He looked at the ghost. “Here, it’s the difference between saving the world and ending it. So I’m not going to just
recalculate
your chart. I’m going to
own
it. I’m going to understand the pattern better than you did, because I have three centuries of stellar drift that you couldn’t account for, and I have the mathematical tools to account for it, and I am going to use every scrap of training I have to make sure we get this right. Not because the captain told me to. Because I’m choosing to. Because this is the one thing I can do that no one else in this world can do, and I am done being a passenger.”

Ibn Majid was quiet. The ghost’s form steadied — the flickering stopped, the blue-white light holding firm, as if the navigator’s spirit had found something worth focusing on.

“Good,” the ghost said. “Good. A navigator who doesn’t own his chart is a passenger with a compass. Own it. Master it. And for the love of whatever god you pray to, get the sequence right.”

“Give me the original data.”

“The data is in my skull. Literally. I carved it into the inside of my cranium before I died — a final act of navigation, mapping the most important chart of my career onto the only surface I knew would survive.” He looked at the coral sarcophagus. “Open it. Read my bones. The last person who gathered the teeth out of order became the sea itself. Ask yourself — who sent you the map?”

The ghost flickered. Dimmed. The energy that Kian’s memory had purchased was running out.

“Wait,” Kian said. “Who sent me the map? Who—”

“The sea sent you the map. The sea always sends the map. The sea is lonely, archaeologist. All that memory, and no one to share it with. It finds someone — someone curious, someone hungry, someone
lonely
— and it pulls them in. You are not the first. You will not be the last.” The ghost was fading, its form dissolving into the blue-white light, its voice thinning. “Get the sequence right. Gather the teeth in order. And when you hold the thirtieth — when the jaw is complete and the mouth is open and Ahriman is awake — remember this: the cage needs a new lock. Tiamat was the first. Someone must be the second.”

“Who?”

But the ghost was gone. The light faded. The coral sarcophagus sealed itself, the crack closing like a wound healing, and Kian was alone in the dark with a dead navigator’s bones and a warning that echoed in the chamber like the last note of a song.

The cage needs a new lock. Someone must be the second.

He opened the sarcophagus. He read the bones — the carvings on the inside of Ibn Majid’s skull, tiny and precise, a star chart rendered in bone, the most important map ever made inscribed on the most intimate surface imaginable. He memorized it. His archaeologist’s training — the ability to record, catalogue, and retain visual information with photographic precision — had never been more useful.

Then he climbed out of the tomb, into the sunlight, where Leila was standing over the bodies of seven Nasnas — split, quartered, twitching — with her swords bloody and her expression suggesting mild inconvenience.

“You took too long,” she said.

“I was talking to a ghost.”

“Next time, talk faster. These things multiply.” She kicked a Nasnas quarter. It hopped away on half a leg. “Did you get what you needed?”

“I got a star chart carved into a dead man’s skull and a warning that the fate of the world depends on my ability to do three centuries’ worth of astronomical calculations in my head.”

Leila looked at him. “Can you?”

Kian thought about it. The star chart was in his mind — clear, precise, complete. The mathematics of precession were in his training. The tools were in his knowledge. The question was whether a maritime archaeologist from the University of Tehran could recalculate a celestial navigation chart created by a legendary navigator in a world with two moons and alien constellations, using nothing but memory and mental arithmetic.

“Yes,” he said. “I think I can.”

“Then let’s go. Before more of them come.”

They ran for the boat. Behind them, the island stirred — Nasnas emerging from the sand, hopping on single legs, their half-mouths open in half-screams that were somehow worse than whole ones. Leila fought a rearguard action that was less combat than choreography — precise, efficient, almost beautiful in its economy of motion. Amir ran ahead, clearing the path with a speed that was not entirely natural. The sailors fought beside Leila, competent and grim.

They reached the boat. They reached the Dhow. They sailed.

And Kian sat on the deck with a dead man’s star chart burning in his mind and began the calculations that would determine the order in which they hunted the teeth of a sleeping god.

The math took three days. The answer, when it came, was elegant and terrifying.

The teeth had to be gathered in a spiral — starting from the outermost and working inward, following the pattern of a nautilus shell, each tooth closer to the center than the last. The center was the deepest point of the Sea of Tears — the place where Tiamat had died, where the sea had begun, where the thirtieth tooth waited in the skull of a dead goddess.

The first two teeth — Qadir’s original and Faris’s ring — were, by luck or by the sea’s design, the correct starting points. The spiral began at the periphery. They were on track.

But the spiral was tightening. Each tooth would be harder to reach, more dangerous to claim, more costly to carry. And at the center, at the end of the spiral, at the bottom of the sea — the thirtieth tooth, and the choice that Ibn Majid’s ghost had warned him about.

The cage needs a new lock.

Kian looked at the sea. The sea looked back. And somewhere in its depths, in the place where the oldest memories lived and the hungriest things waited, a jaw of black teeth shifted, and the spiral tightened, and the mouth began — slowly, patiently, inevitably — to close.
