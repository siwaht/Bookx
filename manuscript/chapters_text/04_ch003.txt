Prologue: The Map That Bled

“The sea is made of tears, and the tears are made of memory, and the memory is made of salt, and salt preserves nothing forever.”

The copper scroll case was the last thing they pulled from the wreck.

Kian Farahani had been cataloguing artifacts for eleven hours straight, hunched over a folding table in the research vessel’s cargo hold. Around him, the spoils of a ninth-century dhow lay in ordered rows — ceramic jars still sealed with pine resin, glass trade beads from East Africa, a bronze astrolabe so perfectly preserved it might have been made yesterday. Three years of grant proposals, two years of diving, and six months of excavation had yielded what his department chair called “a career-defining find” and what Kian called “not enough to pay off my student loans.”

He was thirty-four years old. He had a PhD in maritime archaeology from the University of Tehran, a visiting fellowship at the Smithsonian that had expired without renewal, and a divorce that had cost him an apartment in Alexandria and the ability to sleep through the night. His ex-wife, Sara, had told him during their last argument that he loved dead things more than living ones. He hadn’t argued. He wasn’t sure she was wrong.

“Dr. Farahani.” Hassan, the dive team leader, appeared at the cargo hold’s hatch, still dripping. He had the unsettling habit of smiling when delivering bad news. He was smiling now. “We found something in the stern section. Under the ballast stones.”

“Another jar?”

“No.” Hassan’s smile widened. “Something strange.”

Strange, in archaeology, usually meant broken. Kian pulled off his nitrile gloves and followed Hassan up the ladder to the dive deck.

The evening call to prayer drifted across the water from the coast, thin and beautiful in the salt air. The research vessel
Persepolis
sat at anchor two kilometers offshore. The Gulf was flat as hammered bronze, the sky a gradient of copper and violet. Somewhere to the west, the lights of Ras al-Khaimah were beginning to flicker on.

Hassan led him to the wet table where the dive team processed finds before they came below. Under a portable UV lamp, nestled in a bed of waterlogged sand, sat a copper cylinder roughly the length of Kian’s forearm. It was green with verdigris, sealed at both ends with what appeared to be beeswax darkened by centuries of submersion. But it was the inscriptions that stopped Kian’s breath.

He leaned closer. Adjusted the lamp. Leaned closer still.

The script was wrong.

Not wrong as in damaged or illegible — wrong as in impossible. Kian had spent twelve years studying the written languages of the medieval Islamic world. He could read Kufic, Naskh, Thuluth. He could puzzle through Pahlavi and make educated guesses at Sogdian. The script on this cylinder was none of those. It shared characteristics with several — the flowing connectivity of Arabic, the angular precision of Old Persian cuneiform, something that might have been Phoenician if Phoenician had been written by someone having a nightmare — but it was, definitively, nothing he had ever seen.

“When did this come up?” he asked.

“Twenty minutes ago. It was under the keel ballast, Dr. Farahani. Not in the cargo hold. Under the ship itself. Wedged between the stones like someone hid it there.”

Kian picked up the cylinder. It was heavier than it should have been — not dramatically, not supernaturally, just enough that some part of his brain noted the discrepancy and filed it under
odd
. The beeswax seal was intact. Nine hundred years under the Persian Gulf, and the seal was intact.

“Has anyone opened it?”

“We were waiting for you.”

“Good.” He turned the cylinder in his hands. The inscriptions seemed to shift in the UV light — a trick of the verdigris, obviously, the way old copper oxidation could create iridescent effects. Obviously. “Get me a scalpel and a clean tray. And tell Dr. Nazari I need her down here — her Pahlavi is better than mine.”

Hassan nodded and left. Kian stood alone on the dive deck with the cylinder in his hands and the call to prayer fading over the water, and he felt something he hadn’t felt in years.

Curiosity. Real curiosity. The kind that had made him choose this career in the first place, before the grant applications and the departmental politics and the slow erosion of wonder by bureaucracy. The cylinder was a mystery, and Kian could not leave a mystery alone.

He carried it below.

Dr. Nazari never came. She was asleep in her cabin, and Hassan declined to wake her. So Kian worked alone, which was how he preferred it.

The cargo hold was quiet. The ship’s generator hummed. Kian set the cylinder on his work table, positioned his magnifying lamp, and began.

The beeswax yielded to the scalpel with surprising ease — not crumbling, as ancient wax should, but parting cleanly, almost eagerly, like a mouth opening to speak. Inside, the cylinder was lined with what appeared to be oiled silk, black and supple, preserving its contents against moisture. Kian’s hands were steady. They were always steady.

He unrolled the silk.

Inside was a map.

The material was not paper. It was not papyrus, or vellum, or any of the standard writing surfaces of the medieval world. It was skin. Not leather — skin. Tanned to a pale amber, supple as living flesh, with the faintest trace of pores visible under magnification. Kian had handled enough organic artifacts to know the difference between processed hide and something that had merely been… removed.

He should have stopped. He should have photographed it in situ, called Dr. Nazari, followed protocol. Instead, he spread the map flat on the table and looked at it.

It showed a sea.

Not the Persian Gulf. Not the Arabian Sea, or the Indian Ocean, or any body of water that existed on any chart Kian had ever studied. This sea filled the entire map, coastlines suggesting a world that was mostly water, with scattered islands and archipelagos rendered in ink that was — he leaned closer — not ink. The lines were brown-red, the color of old blood, applied with a precision that no machine in the ninth century could have produced.

In the center of the sea, written in the same impossible script as the cylinder’s exterior, was a name. Kian couldn’t read it. He shouldn’t have been able to read it. But as he stared at the characters, they rearranged themselves — not on the map, but in his mind, like a word on the tip of his tongue finally arriving — and he understood.

Bahr al-Dumou.

The Sea of Tears.

“That’s not possible,” he said to the empty hold. His voice sounded strange. Thin. As if the air had thickened.

He reached out and touched the map.

The map bled.

Not metaphorically. The map bled. From the point where his index finger met the skin-surface, a bead of red welled up — warm, bright, unmistakably blood — and ran down the contour of a coastline like a river finding its course.

Kian jerked his hand back. The blood — the map’s blood, not his; his finger was uncut — continued to flow, tracing the outlines of islands, filling in the channels between landmasses, pooling in the center where the Sea of Tears was drawn. The smell hit him: copper and salt and something older, something that made the hindbrain — the lizard part, the part that remembered being prey — scream
run
.

He didn’t run. He was a scientist. Scientists don’t run from bleeding maps. Scientists—

The blood reached the edges of the map and kept going. It ran off the table, onto the floor, and the floor was wrong. The floor of the cargo hold was steel plate, painted gray, bolted to the ship’s frame. But where the blood touched it, the steel became water — not rusted, not corroded, but
transformed
, as if the concept of steel had been replaced by the concept of sea. Kian watched a puddle of ocean appear in the middle of the cargo hold, complete with tiny waves, and his scientific mind offered one final, desperate hypothesis:
I am having a stroke.

He was not having a stroke.

The water spread. It climbed the table legs. It swallowed his notes, his laptop, his carefully labeled artifact bags. It was warm — the precise temperature of blood, of the inside of a body, of a place that was alive. It had reached his knees and he was, he realized with a calm that terrified him more than the water, sinking.

Not drowning. Sinking. The way you sink into a bed after a long day, the way you sink into a memory you’d rather forget. The water didn’t fight him. It welcomed him. It pulled him down with the gentle insistence of a current that has been waiting a very long time.

The cargo hold was gone. The
Persepolis
was gone. The Persian Gulf, the evening, the call to prayer, the world — gone. There was only the water, and the water was not water. It was something else — similar enough to fool you, different enough to kill you.

Kian sank, and as he sank, he remembered.

Not his memories.

A woman braiding her daughter’s hair in a courtyard of white stone. The daughter was laughing. The woman’s hands were rough from lye soap and gentle in the way that only a mother’s hands can be gentle, and Kian knew —
knew
, with the certainty of lived experience — that the daughter would die of fever before the next moon, and the woman would unbraid the hair from a corpse and keep it in a cedar box for thirty years until her own death, and the box would be buried with her, and the hair would outlast them both.

A soldier on a wall. The wall was high and white and overlooked a city of minarets and gardens, and the army below was vast, and the soldier knew he would die today, and he was afraid, and he was also hungry because he had skipped breakfast, and the absurdity of dying on an empty stomach struck him as funny, and he laughed, and the man beside him laughed too, and they were still laughing when the first volley of arrows came.

A child eating dates in a courtyard. Ajwa dates, small and dark, the kind that taste of caramel and earth. The child ate them one by one, savoring each, because his father had told him that the Prophet, peace be upon him, ate dates in odd numbers, and the child was trying to decide if seven was enough or if he should push for nine. The sun was warm. The dates were sweet. The child was happy in the uncomplicated way that only children and saints can be happy, and Kian wept because he could not remember the last time he had felt anything so simple.

These memories were not his. They belonged to the sea. The Sea of Tears —
Bahr al-Dumou
— was made of them, composed of them, sustained by them. Every drop was a moment lived and lost, every current a life’s narrative flowing toward an ending, every wave a crest of joy or trough of grief. The sea was liquid memory, and Kian was drowning in other people’s lives.

He screamed. The scream became someone else’s laughter. The laughter became a lullaby. The lullaby became a battle cry. The battle cry became a prayer. The prayer became silence.

And then he was through.

He hit the sand face-first.

It was black. The sand was black — not dark gray, not volcanic brown, but the absolute, light-eating black of a moonless night, of closed eyes, of the space between stars. It was fine as powdered charcoal and warm as skin, and it filled his mouth and nose and eyes, and he coughed and spat and clawed his way to his knees and looked up at a sky that was wrong.

Two moons. One full and silver, hanging where a moon should hang. The other half-shattered, a crescent of broken rock trailing debris like a comet’s tail, glowing faintly amber from some internal fire. The stars were different — denser, closer, arranged in constellations he didn’t recognize. The air smelled of cardamom and rot, of incense and low tide, of a world that was ancient and alive and not his.

Kian knelt on the black sand and breathed.

His phone was dead. Not out of battery — dead, as if the concept of electricity had been politely declined by the local physics. His watch had stopped at 9:47 PM — the moment he’d touched the map. His clothes were dry. His shoes were gone.

The map was tattooed on his left forearm.

He stared at it. The same coastlines, the same impossible sea, the same script — but now rendered in blue-black ink beneath his skin, as if it had always been there. When he flexed his wrist, the lines moved. The tiny waves in the Sea of Tears rippled. A dot — his location, he realized — pulsed on a black shore at the map’s eastern edge.

“Okay,” he said. His voice cracked. “Okay. I’m — this is—” He stopped. There was no sentence that could contain what was happening.

He stood. His legs shook. The beach stretched in both directions, black sand meeting dark water under the broken sky. Behind him, cliffs of pale stone rose like teeth from a jaw, riddled with caves that exhaled warm air smelling of sulfur and cinnamon. There was no sign of the
Persepolis
, no sign of the Gulf, no sign of anything he recognized.

He was alone for approximately four minutes.

They came from the caves.

Six men, moving with the practiced coordination of people who had done this many times before. They wore loose trousers and tunics of undyed linen, and they carried weapons — short curved swords, the kind Kian recognized from museum collections as
saif
, and wooden clubs studded with shark teeth. Their eyes were flat with the professional disinterest of men conducting business.

Slavers. Kian knew this the way you know a dog is dangerous — not from analysis, but from the posture, the formation, the way they spread to cut off escape routes before he’d even thought about running. He’d never expected to experience the primary sources.

“Wait,” he said. “I’m — I don’t—”

The lead slaver said something in a language Kian didn’t speak. It sounded like Arabic but wasn’t — the vowels were wrong, the consonants too sharp, as if Arabic had evolved in a different direction, in a different world. The man gestured with his sword. The meaning was universal:
move
.

“I don’t understand,” Kian said. “I’m an archaeologist. I’m — I had a phone—”

The slaver hit him. Not hard — a professional blow, calculated to stun without damaging merchandise. The club caught Kian across the shoulder and he went down, and the black sand filled his mouth again.

They chained him. Iron manacles, heavy and crude, connected by a length of chain to a wooden yoke across his shoulders. The metal was warm from someone else’s body heat.

As they marched him toward the caves, the lead slaver spoke again — the same alien language, a command to the others — and Kian understood him.

Not gradually. Not through context clues or linguistic analysis. The words simply
arrived
in his mind, fully formed, as if they had always been there, waiting behind a door that the sea had opened.

“Another one from the deep. Skinny. Soft hands. Scholar or merchant?”

“Scholar,”
another slaver replied.
“Look at his fingers. Ink stains. No calluses. He’ll fetch less than the last one.”

“The Iskandar market takes scholars. The flesh-scribes always need literate stock.”

Kian’s blood went cold. The sea had put this language in him, the way the map had put itself on his arm. The crossing had changed him in ways he hadn’t begun to catalogue.

He said nothing. A scholar who understands his captors’ language is more valuable than one who doesn’t — but a scholar who
pretends
not to understand is more dangerous than either.

They led him through the caves — a network of tunnels carved by water and widened by hands, lit by bioluminescent moss that cast everything in a sickly green — and out the other side, where a cove sheltered a ship.

Not a ship. A
vessel
. Kian’s archaeological mind catalogued it automatically: a boom-type dhow, maybe twenty meters, lateen-rigged, with a hull of teak planks sewn together with coconut fiber rather than nailed. It was also, unmistakably, a slave ship. The hold was open, and from it rose the smell of too many bodies in too small a space, and the sound — quiet, resigned, barely human — of people who had stopped hoping.

They put him in the hold.

There were thirty-one of them. Kian counted because counting was something his mind could do while the rest of him shut down. Thirty-one people chained in a space designed for cargo, breathing air that was more exhalation than oxygen. Men, women, a handful of children. All of them had the same expression: a blankness that was not peace but its opposite, the face a person makes when feeling anything at all has become too expensive.

Kian was chained to a ring bolt in the hull. The manacles bit his wrists. The yoke pressed his shoulders. He sat in the dark and tried to think.

Facts.
He was in another world. The map had brought him here. The sea was made of memories. He could understand the local language. He was a slave.

Hypotheses.
He was hallucinating (unlikely — hallucinations don’t have consistent internal logic). He was dead (possible, but the pain in his shoulder argued against it). He had been transported to a parallel dimension (the two moons ruled out any version of Earth). He was inside the map (this felt closest to true).

Plan.
Survive. Observe. Escape. Find the map — the original, the one on the skin, the one that bled. If it brought him here, perhaps it could take him back.

The boy was chained next to him.

He was young — twelve, maybe thirteen, with the gangly proportions of a body growing faster than it could coordinate. His eyes were enormous in a face that hadn’t yet decided what shape it would be. He wore the remnants of what might have been fine clothing — silk, once, now reduced to rags. He’d been here longer than Kian. His wrists were raw.

“Where are we going?” Kian asked. His voice was a rasp. The new language felt strange in his mouth — the consonants required positions his tongue wasn’t used to, the vowels sat differently in his throat. But it worked. The words came out and meant what he intended.

The boy looked at him. “Iskandar,” he said. “The floating city. They sell us there.”

“Sell us to whom?”

“Anyone who pays.” The boy’s voice was flat. Not brave — emptied. “Flesh-scribes. Quarry masters. The fighting pits. The pearl divers, if you’re lucky. Pearl divers die fast, but they die in the sun.”

Kian’s stomach turned. “How long have you been here?”

“The days blur. There was a moon when they took me. A different moon.” He paused. “You came from the sea. I saw them bring you in. You were dry.”

“Yes.”

“The sea brings people sometimes. From other places. Other…” He searched for a word. “Other
wheres
. My grandmother said the sea is a door with no handle. You can’t open it. But sometimes it opens for you.”

“Your grandmother sounds wise.”

“She’s dead. The slavers killed her when she fought back.” He said this with the same flatness. A fact.

Kian opened his mouth to say something — comfort, solidarity, the kind of empty reassurance that adults offer children when the truth is unbearable — and found that he had nothing. So he sat in the dark and said nothing.

The boy died three days later.

Dysentery. The water they were given was brackish, scooped from the bilge and cut with vinegar that did nothing to kill whatever lived in it. The boy’s body rejected it — cramps, fever, a dehydration so rapid that by the second day his skin hung loose on his bones. Kian held his head. Kian gave him his own water ration. Kian did everything a man with no medical training and no resources could do, which was nothing.

The boy asked Kian to tell him a story. “Anything,” he whispered. “A story from your
where
. Something with a happy ending.”

Kian tried. He opened his mouth to tell the boy about — what? Movies? Airplanes? Everything from his world seemed absurd, trivial, impossible to translate.

He said nothing.

The boy died in the night, quietly, without complaint, as if he’d simply decided that being alive was no longer worth the effort. The slavers unchained the body in the morning and threw it overboard. It hit the water without a splash — the Sea of Tears absorbed it, the way it absorbed everything, and for a moment Kian thought he saw the boy’s face in the waves, not dead but
remembered
, preserved in liquid memory like an insect in amber.

He never learned the boy’s name.

This was the first thing Kian carried. The silence. The moment when a dying child asked for a story and Kian gave him nothing. He sat beside an empty set of chains and stared at the map on his arm and felt something new.

Rage.

The voice came on the fifth night.

Kian was half-asleep — the shallow, fitful unconsciousness that passes for rest when you’re chained to the inside of a ship that smells like a mass grave. The hold was dark. The other captives were silent, or moaning, or praying in languages he could now understand and wished he couldn’t.

The voice came from below. From the bilge — the lowest point of the hull, where seawater collected and stagnated and bred things that thrived in darkness. It didn’t speak in words, not exactly. It spoke in
sensations
. A pressure behind Kian’s eyes. A taste on his tongue — storm-water, ozone, the electric tang of air before lightning. And with the taste, emotions that were not his own.

Hunger. Vast, ancient, patient hunger — the hunger of something that had been waiting in the dark for a very long time and had learned to enjoy the waiting.

Amusement. The specific amusement of a cat watching a mouse try to climb a wall.

Contempt. Not for Kian specifically — for everything. The contempt of something that had seen civilizations rise and fall and found the whole process tedious.

And then, finally, words. Not spoken —
placed
in his mind, like coins on a counter.

I can break your chains.

Kian’s eyes opened. The dark was absolute. He couldn’t see the voice’s source. He couldn’t see anything. But he could feel it — a presence in the bilge water, something vast compressed into something small, like a hurricane folded into a bottle.

The price is small. A memory. Something you won’t miss.

“Who are you?” Kian whispered. The captive chained beside him stirred but didn’t wake.

I am Zulfaqar. I am Marid. I am the storm that sank the fleet of Suleiman. I am the wave that drowned the city of Iram. I am very old, and very bored, and you are the most interesting thing to fall into my waters in decades.

“You’re a djinn.”

I am a Marid. Djinn is a category. Marid is a rank. The difference is the difference between ‘fish’ and ‘leviathan.’ Do not insult me with generalities.

Despite everything — the chains, the dark, the dead boy, the impossible world — Kian felt a flicker of something that might have been dark humor. “My apologies. I’m new here.”

Yes. You smell of the other side. Of machines and doubt and that peculiar loneliness your kind carries like a disease. You are a scholar.

“I’m an archaeologist.”

You dig up the dead and read their mail. Close enough.
A pause. The hunger pulsed.
I can break your chains. I can kill every slaver on this ship. I can part the sea and walk you to shore on dry land. I can do anything you ask, scholar. The laws of your world do not apply here. The laws of this world are… negotiable.

“And the price?”

A memory. The color of your mother’s kitchen, perhaps. The sound of rain on a window you loved. Something small. Something you’ll barely notice is gone — like a tooth extracted under anesthesia. One moment it’s there. The next, a gap. You’ll know something is missing. You won’t know what.

Kian’s rational mind — battered, disoriented, but still functioning, still
him
— assembled the proposition into familiar terms. A transaction. An exchange of value. The djinn — the Marid — offered services in exchange for payment. The payment was denominated in memory. The exchange rate was unclear. The terms were vague. The fine print was almost certainly lethal.

“No,” Kian said.

Silence. Then:

No?

“I don’t make deals I don’t understand. I don’t sign contracts I haven’t read. And I don’t trust anything that lives in a bilge and offers miracles to strangers.”

A sensation that might have been laughter — appreciative, the way a chess player appreciates an unexpected move.

Interesting. The last one said yes immediately. He’s a fish now. Well — part of a fish. The digestive part.
Another pause.
You’ll change your mind, scholar. They always do. The chains get heavier. The dark gets darker. And I am very, very patient.

The presence receded. Not gone — Kian could still feel it, a weight in the water beneath him. But it was quiet now, waiting with the patience of something that measured time in centuries.

Kian sat in his chains and stared at nothing and thought:
I will find another way.

He was wrong, of course. But he didn’t know that yet. He was still, in the most important sense, a scientist.

The sea would cure him of that.

The storm came on the seventh day.

It announced itself with a drop in pressure that made Kian’s ears pop. The ship began to pitch — gently at first, then with increasing violence, the hull groaning as waves struck it broadside. The ship heeled hard to port, and everything in the hold — people, chains, waste buckets, hope — slid sideways and crashed against the hull.

Kian braced himself against the ring bolt. The chains cut his wrists. Seawater poured through the deck seams — not ordinary seawater but the Sea of Tears, liquid memory, and where it touched his skin he saw flashes: a woman giving birth, a man setting fire to a library, a child learning to swim, a king signing a death warrant. The memories came faster as the storm intensified, overlapping, contradicting, a deluge of human experience compressed into salt water.

The ship was breaking.

He could hear it — the coconut-fiber stitching that held the hull together was snapping, one cord at a time, like a garment unraveling. The teak planks flexed and separated. Water rushed in — not leaking but
entering
, as if the sea had decided it was tired of being outside and wanted in. The captives screamed. Some prayed. Some were silent, having passed beyond the reach of either prayer or panic.

Kian was going to die. He knew this with the same certainty he’d known the boy would die. He was going to drown in a sea of other people’s memories, chained to a ship that was coming apart, and no one in his world would ever know what happened to him.

And then he saw the ship.

It came out of the storm like a knife through silk — not fighting the waves but riding them, not resisting the wind but using it. It was black. Not painted black —
made
of blackness, as if someone had carved a ship from the night sky and set it on the water. Its sails were dark as bruises, torn and mended and torn again. Its hull was scarred with barnacles and blade marks and something that might have been claw marks from something very large.

The Black Dhow.

It cut through the waves toward the slave ship with the directness of a predator that has spotted prey. On its prow, a figure stood — not braced, not holding on, simply
stood
as if the storm were a mild inconvenience and gravity a suggestion he’d chosen to follow out of politeness.

The figure was laughing.

Kian could hear it over the storm, over the screaming, over the crack of the slave ship’s hull finally giving way — a laugh that was not mad but
delighted
. The figure raised a hand, and the storm — the entire storm, wind and wave and lightning and rain —
paused
.

Not stopped. Paused. Like a held breath. Like the moment between a question and its answer.

In the silence, the figure spoke. His voice carried across the water with unnatural clarity, each word arriving in Kian’s ears as if whispered from inches away.

“Well, well. What has the sea coughed up tonight?”

The slave ship broke in half.

It happened almost gently — the hull separating along its keel line, the two halves folding outward like a book opening, spilling cargo and captives and slavers into the Sea of Tears. Kian went under. The memories hit him again — a thousand lives, a thousand deaths, a thousand moments of joy and grief and boredom and terror — and he fought them, fought the sea, fought the chains that dragged him down.

Something grabbed him.

Not a hand — a
force
. A current that wrapped around his chest and pulled him upward, through the water, through the memories, through the border between drowning and breathing, and deposited him on a deck of black wood that was warm under his back and humming with a vibration he could feel in his teeth.

He lay on the deck of the Black Dhow and coughed up the Sea of Tears — someone else’s wedding, someone else’s funeral, someone else’s first kiss — and looked up at the broken sky and the two moons and the face of the man who had pulled him from the water.

Captain Qadir looked down at him. He was old — not elderly, but
old
in the way that mountains are old, worn by time into something essential and irreducible. His skin was deeply lined, mapped with scars that told stories Kian couldn’t yet read. He was missing his left ear. His eyes were black and flat and utterly without mercy, and they were also amused.

“A scholar,” Qadir said. He said it the way a fisherman says
mackerel
— identifying the catch, assessing its worth. “The sea sends me scholars now. I asked for warriors.” He looked at the sky. “You have a terrible sense of humor,” he told the moons.

Kian tried to speak. Coughed. Tried again. “Where—”

“You’re on my ship. You’re alive. These are the only two facts that matter.” Qadir crouched beside him. Up close, he smelled of tar and tobacco and something sweet — dates. “Can you sail?”

“No.”

“Can you fight?”

“No.”

“Can you cook?”

“No.”

“Can you do anything useful, scholar, or has the sea sent me another mouth to feed and nothing more?”

Kian looked at the captain. He looked at the black ship, at the crew — shadows moving in the storm-light, pulling survivors from the water with hooks and ropes. He looked at the map on his arm, still pulsing, still alive.

“I can read,” he said. “I can read things that haven’t been read in a thousand years. I can read the map on my arm. And I can hear the voice in your bilge.”

Qadir’s expression didn’t change. But something shifted behind those flat black eyes — a recalculation, a reassessment, the look of a man who has just discovered that the mackerel might be a shark.

“Zulfaqar spoke to you.” It wasn’t a question.

“It offered me a deal. I refused.”

“You refused a Marid.” The amusement was open now. Qadir stood and offered Kian his hand. “You’re either very brave or very stupid, scholar. In my experience, the distinction is academic.” He pulled Kian to his feet. “Welcome aboard the Black Dhow. Try not to die. I hate cleaning up.”

The storm resumed. The Dhow cut through it like a blade through water, and Kian stood on the deck of a pirate ship in a world that shouldn’t exist, chained and bleeding and terrified and alive, and in the bilge below his feet, something ancient and hungry laughed.

In the slave pen, an old man had whispered:
The sea brought you. The sea always brings someone. The last one became a god. The one before became a meal.

Kian gripped the rail with both hands — both hands, still whole, still his — and wondered which one he would be.
