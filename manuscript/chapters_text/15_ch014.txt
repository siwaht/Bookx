Chapter 11: The Underwater Feast

“The first drink is for truth. The second for lies. The third — for the truth you wish were a lie.” — Al-Rashid

The feast was real.

This was the first thing Kian confirmed, and the most unsettling. In a drowned city populated by ghosts, served by spectral servants on ghostly trays, the food was
real
— solid, warm, fragrant, possessing weight and texture and flavor in a way that nothing else in the Sunken Caliphate did. Al-Rashid’s tooth could compel obedience. It could also, apparently, compel reality — forcing matter into existence through sheer will, conjuring food from memory with a fidelity that bordered on the divine.

“The tooth remembers,” Al-Rashid explained, settling onto cushions at the head of a low table that had been set in the palace’s central courtyard — a space open to the sea above, where fish drifted like living chandeliers and the bioluminescent coral cast everything in shifting blue and gold. “It is a fragment of Ahriman, and Ahriman consumed everything, and everything he consumed, he remembered. The tooth contains the memory of every meal ever eaten, every dish ever prepared, every flavor ever tasted. I simply… select.” He waved a hand. A ghost servant set down a platter. “The fish course.”

Samak mashwi. Grilled fish — a whole fish, scored and seasoned, cooked over a fire that existed only in the tooth’s memory but produced heat and smoke and char that were indistinguishable from the real thing. The fish was laid on a bed of tahini sauce, white and creamy, drizzled with olive oil and scattered with pine nuts that had been toasted to a deep gold. Beside it, lemon halves, charred on the cut side, their juice sharp and bright.

“This is the samak mashwi of the Caliphate’s golden age,” Al-Rashid said, serving Kian with his own hands — the hands of a host, not a captor, though the distinction was, as Qadir had noted, theological. “The recipe belonged to the caliph’s personal chef, a man named Harun, who was the greatest cook in the history of Al-Khaliq and who drowned with everyone else when the bubble collapsed. I preserved his recipes. In my memory. In the tooth’s memory. They are the same thing now.”

Kian tasted the fish. It was perfect. Not good —
perfect
. The flesh was white and flaky, seasoned with cumin and coriander and a hint of something smoky that might have been paprika. The tahini was smooth, nutty, balanced with lemon and garlic. The pine nuts added crunch and richness. It was, objectively, the best fish Kian had ever eaten.

It was also, he realized, a memory. Not his memory — the tooth’s. He was eating a dish that had last been cooked five hundred years ago, by a chef who had been dead for five centuries, recreated by a god’s tooth from the memory of perfection. The food was real. The experience was real. But the origin was spectral, and the knowledge of this gave the meal a melancholy that no amount of tahini could mask.

“Extraordinary,” Omar would have said, if Omar had been there. Omar was on the Dhow, three hundred meters above, probably eating dried fish and complaining about it. Kian missed him. He missed the quartermaster’s commentary, his jokes, his ability to transform any meal into a social event. Al-Rashid’s feast was magnificent. It was also lonely — the loneliness of a host who had been waiting five centuries for someone to appreciate his table.

“You’re quiet,” Al-Rashid said. He was watching Kian with the intensity of a man who had forgotten what living faces looked like and was memorizing this one. “The fish displeases you?”

“The fish is perfect. That’s the problem.”

“Perfection is a problem?”

“Perfection from memory is. This fish was cooked five hundred years ago. The chef is dead. The kitchen is drowned. The recipe exists only in your tooth’s recollection. It’s a ghost meal. Beautiful, flawless, and fundamentally hollow.”

Al-Rashid stared at him. The bright, mad eyes flickered — not with anger but with something more dangerous: recognition. The recognition of a truth he’d been avoiding for five centuries.

“You are unkind,” the vizier said softly. “And you are correct. And I hate you for both.” He clapped his hands. “The next course.”

Torshi. Pickled vegetables — turnips dyed pink with beet juice, carrots cut into coins, peppers stuffed with garlic and herbs, all preserved in a brine of vinegar and salt and time. The pickles were sharp, sour, crunchy, a counterpoint to the fish’s richness that was as much textural as it was gustatory.

“Preservation,” Al-Rashid said, crunching a turnip with the enthusiasm of a man who had been eating his own conjured food for five centuries and still found pleasure in it, which was either admirable or insane. “The only art that matters. I have preserved this court for three hundred years. The pickles are older.”

Fatima ate carefully, her surgeon’s mind cataloguing the food’s properties — nutritional content, potential allergens, the magical signature that the bahr-sense revealed in every bite. The food was real, but it was also Sihr — conjured from a god’s tooth, sustained by a madman’s will — and Fatima trusted Sihr the way she trusted a sharp knife: useful, necessary, and capable of cutting you if you weren’t careful.

Samira didn’t eat. The witch sat at the table with her hands in her lap and her storm-green eyes fixed on Al-Rashid with an expression that Kian’s bahr-sense read as
calculation
. She was assessing the vizier — his power, his madness, his vulnerabilities. She was looking for the angle. The weakness. The way in.

Amir ate everything. The cabin boy consumed food with the single-minded focus of someone who had learned that meals were not guaranteed and should be exploited when available. He ate the fish, the pickles, the bread, the hummus that arrived next — creamy, smooth, drizzled with olive oil and paprika, served with warm flatbread that tore like silk.

“Hummus,” Al-Rashid said, watching Amir eat with the delight of a host whose guest appreciates the spread. “The great equalizer. Kings and beggars eat hummus. It is the most democratic food in the world. Also the most contentious — every nation claims to have invented it, and every nation is wrong. Hummus was invented by hunger. Hunger is the only chef who never takes credit.”

Qadir ate slowly. The captain’s appetite had diminished — the tooth’s drain was visible in his thinning frame, his graying skin, the way he chewed as if the act of eating required more energy than the food provided. But he ate, because eating was a social contract, and social contracts were the currency of negotiation, and negotiation was why they were here.

“The tooth,” Qadir said, between bites of hummus. “You’ve had it for five centuries.”

“Five hundred and twelve years. I count. Counting is the only hobby available to the immortal. That and conversation, which requires guests, which I haven’t had in forty years, which is why I’m so delighted to see you and so reluctant to let you leave.”

“The tooth is killing you.”

“The tooth is keeping me alive. The distinction is — well, it’s not a distinction at all, is it? The tooth keeps me alive so that it can continue to feed on me. I keep the tooth so that I can continue to exist. We are parasites of each other. A symbiosis of mutual destruction.” Al-Rashid smiled. The smile was brilliant, warm, and completely disconnected from the horror of what he was describing. “I am aware that I am insane. Insanity is the natural consequence of five centuries of solitude. I am also aware that my insanity makes me dangerous. Dangerous hosts are the most generous — we have nothing to lose.”

“You don’t seem insane,” Amir said, through a mouthful of hummus.

“That’s because you’re twelve and your standards for sanity are based on a pirate crew. By your standards, I’m the most well-adjusted person you’ve met.” Al-Rashid leaned forward. “I talk to the fish. Not metaphorically — I have conversations with them. The grouper is a bore. The octopus is a philosopher. The jellyfish are nihilists, which is understandable given their lack of brains. I have named every fish in this palace. There are four thousand, seven hundred and twelve. I know this because I count them every morning. Counting is the only thing that keeps me from screaming, and screaming is the only thing that keeps me from counting, and the cycle is — well. You see the problem.”

“That does sound insane,” Amir admitted.

“Thank you. Honesty is so rare among the living. The dead are honest — they have nothing left to lie about — but the living lie constantly, about everything, to everyone, including themselves. Especially themselves.” He clapped his hands. “More arak. The conversation requires lubrication.”

The arak came — clear, anise-scented, turning milky when Al-Rashid added water from a crystal decanter. He poured with ceremony, with the precise, ritualized movements of a man who had performed this act ten thousand times and found comfort in the repetition.

“The first drink is for truth,” he said, raising his glass. “The second for lies. The third — for the truth you wish were a lie.”

They drank. The arak was perfect — of course it was perfect, it was conjured from a god’s memory of perfection — and the perfection was, as Kian had noted, the problem. But the arak loosened tongues, even ghost-conjured arak, and the loosening was what Al-Rashid wanted.

“Tell me about the world above,” the vizier said. “Tell me everything. I have been down here for five centuries. I have missed — everything. Wars, I assume. Empires rising and falling. New foods. New music. New ways for humans to be terrible to each other.” He leaned forward with the desperate eagerness of a man who had been starving for information the way his body had been starving for real food. “Tell me about the terrible things. The terrible things are always the most interesting.”

Omar, who had been eating hummus with the focused determination of a man on a mission, looked up. “You want terrible? I once saw a man sell his own mother at the Iskandar slave market. Not because he needed the money — because she’d criticized his wife’s cooking. He sold her for twelve dinars and a goat. The goat died the next week. The mother outlived them both.”

Al-Rashid clapped his hands with delight. “Magnificent. The pettiness. The specificity. The goat. Tell me more.”

“I once cheated a blind merchant out of his entire stock of saffron,” Omar continued, warming to his audience. “I told him the saffron was contaminated with sand. It wasn’t. I sold it at triple the price in the next port. I felt guilty for approximately three hours, which is my personal record for guilt. Then I ate the profits in the form of an extremely expensive biryani, and the guilt dissolved in the ghee.”

“You’re a terrible person,” Fatima said.

“I’m an honest terrible person. There’s a difference. A dishonest terrible person pretends to be good. An honest terrible person admits he’s terrible and charges you for the admission.” Omar ate more hummus. “Also, I sent the blind merchant a crate of real saffron the following year. Anonymously. Because guilt has a longer shelf life than I expected.”

“Redemption through spice delivery,” Al-Rashid said. “I love it. What else? What other terrible, wonderful, human things have happened in my absence?”

Qadir spoke. This was unusual — the captain rarely contributed to social conversation, preferring to observe from the edges, his flat black eyes recording everything and revealing nothing. But the arak had reached even him, and the arak, combined with the tooth’s drain and the knowledge that he was dying, had loosened something in the old pirate that was usually kept locked.

“I left a woman,” Qadir said. “Thirty years ago. In a port town on the western coast. She was pregnant. I knew she was pregnant. I sailed anyway.” He drank. “I told myself it was the mission. The teeth. The world. I told myself that the world was more important than one woman and one child. And maybe it was. Maybe the math was right. But the math doesn’t account for the fact that somewhere in Al-Khaliq, there is a person who is half me, and I have never seen their face, and I will die without seeing their face, and the dying is — the dying is the easy part. The not-seeing is the hard part.”

The table was silent. Al-Rashid’s bright, mad eyes were wet.

“Five centuries,” the vizier whispered. “Five centuries alone, and I have never heard anything as terrible and as beautiful as a pirate admitting he abandoned his child. More arak. Immediately. This conversation requires significantly more arak.”

They drank. The ghost servants refilled the glasses. The fish drifted overhead, and the dead court repeated its eternal drowning, and the living sat among the dead and told each other terrible truths, and the telling was a kind of feast, and the feast was the only real thing in a palace of ghosts.

“We need the tooth,” Kian said.

The table went quiet. The ghost servants froze mid-step. The fish in the courtyard above seemed to pause. Al-Rashid’s smile didn’t waver, but something behind it shifted — a door closing, a wall rising, the defenses of a man who had been alone for five centuries and had just been told that the one thing keeping him alive was being taken away.

“Of course you do,” Al-Rashid said. “Everyone who comes here wants the tooth. The last guests wanted it too. I offered them a deal. They accepted. They’re still here.” He gestured to a corner of the courtyard, where — Kian now noticed — a group of people sat at a separate table, eating ghost food with ghost utensils, their eyes blank, their movements mechanical. The previous guests. Forty years of captivity, sustained by the tooth’s power, their minds eroded by loneliness and compulsion until they were barely distinguishable from the ghosts around them.

“I don’t want to keep you,” Al-Rashid said. “I want to keep
someone
. The tooth keeps me alive, but it doesn’t keep me sane. Company keeps me sane. Or — closer to sane. Sane-adjacent. Within shouting distance of sane.” He leaned forward. “I will give you the tooth. Freely. Without combat, without trickery, without the tedious violence that usually accompanies these transactions. I will give it to you if someone stays.”

“Stays,” Qadir repeated.

“Stays. Here. With me. Forever. One person. One companion. One voice in the dark to remind me that I was human once, that the world above the water still exists, that food is meant to be shared and conversation is meant to be had and loneliness is not the natural state of being but an aberration, a wound, a thing that can be healed if only someone would stay.”

Kian looked at Qadir. Qadir looked at the table. The captain’s face was unreadable — the flat black eyes giving nothing away, the scarred features as still as stone. But Kian could feel, through the bahr-sense, the calculation happening behind those eyes. The cold, pragmatic arithmetic of a man who had spent decades making decisions that cost other people their lives.

“I’ll arrange it,” Qadir said.

The arrangement was this: one of the rescued slaves — a mute boy, maybe fifteen, pulled from Faris’s dungeon during the Samira rescue — would stay with Al-Rashid. The boy couldn’t speak. He couldn’t object. He couldn’t negotiate. He was, in the brutal calculus of Qadir’s decision-making, the least valuable member of the crew — not because he was worth less as a person, but because he contributed less to the mission, and the mission was everything, and everything that was not the mission was expendable.

Kian objected.

“No,” he said. They were in a side chamber of the palace — Qadir, Kian, Fatima. The ghost servants had been dismissed. The tooth’s glow was the only light. “We’re not leaving a boy here. We’re not trading a person for a pearl.”

“We’re trading a person for the world,” Qadir said. His voice was calm. Patient. The voice of a man explaining something obvious to someone who was being deliberately obtuse. “The tooth is necessary. Al-Rashid’s price is a companion. The boy is the companion. The math is simple.”

“The math is monstrous.”

“The math is always monstrous. That’s why it’s math and not poetry. Poetry can afford to be kind. Math cannot.”

“Then let me stay.”

The words came out before Kian had fully decided to say them, but once they were in the air, he found he meant them. The logic was clean — cleaner than Qadir’s, cleaner than the monstrous arithmetic of sacrificing a mute boy who couldn’t even protest his own abandonment. Kian had the bahr-sense. He had Zulfaqar. He could survive down here. He could keep Al-Rashid company — a scholar and a vizier, two men who loved conversation, who could talk about history and philosophy and the specific loneliness of knowing too much. It made
sense
.

Qadir stared at him. For the first time since Kian had known the captain, something moved behind those flat black eyes — not surprise, exactly, but reassessment. The recalculation of a man who had expected capitulation and received a counter-offer.

“You carry the map,” Qadir said. “You carry the Marid. You are the only person who can read Ibn Majid’s star chart and calculate the spiral. Without you, we gather the teeth blind, and blind gathering kills everyone.”

“Then teach someone else. Give Fatima the map. Transfer Zulfaqar—”

I am not transferable,
Zulfaqar said from inside his chest.
I am bonded to you. The bond is permanent. This is not a library book, scholar.

“Then leave me here with the Marid and come back. After the thirtieth tooth. After the jaw is sealed. Come back and get me.”

“That could be years.”

“Al-Rashid has waited five centuries. I can wait a few years.” Kian met Qadir’s eyes. “The boy is fifteen. He’s mute. He’s been a slave his entire life. He has never once been given a choice about anything. You’re proposing to not give him another one. I am
choosing
. That’s the difference. That’s the only difference that matters.”

Qadir was quiet for a long time. The tooth’s glow pulsed in the chamber — cold, blue-white, the light of a god’s hunger illuminating a human argument about the price of decency.

“The difference,” Qadir said finally, “is that the boy is expendable and you are not. I don’t say this to be cruel. I say it because it is the truth of our situation. You are the key. The map, the Marid, the star chart, the bahr-sense — they are all in you. Losing you is losing the mission. Losing the boy is losing nothing.”

“Losing the boy is losing
the boy
.”

“Yes. And the boy is not the world.”

Kian turned to Fatima. “Tell him. Tell him this is wrong.”

Fatima’s face was still. The surgeon’s composure — the mask she wore when she was cutting into someone, when the blood was flowing and the patient was screaming and the only thing between life and death was her ability to not feel anything until the work was done. She looked at Kian, and her eyes were full of something that was not agreement and not disagreement but something worse: understanding.

“It is wrong,” she said quietly. “And it is necessary. And I hate that those two things can be true at the same time.” She paused. “You can’t stay, Kian. Qadir is right about the map. He’s right about the Marid. He’s wrong about the boy being nothing — the boy is a person, and persons are never nothing — but he’s right that you are irreplaceable, and the boy—” She closed her eyes. “The boy is not.”

“So we’re ranking people now. Ranking their worth. Deciding who matters more.”

“We’ve been ranking people since the slave ship,” Fatima said. “Since Qasim. Since the reef. Every choice we’ve made has been a ranking. Who lives, who dies, who pays the price. The only difference is that now we’re doing it with our eyes open.”

Kian looked at the door. Beyond it, the ghost court repeated its eternal loop. Beyond that, the sea. Beyond that, the surface, the ship, the crew, the remaining teeth. Twenty-six more prices to pay. Twenty-six more rankings. Twenty-six more moments where someone would stand in a room and argue for decency and lose, because decency was expensive and the mission couldn’t afford it.

He could refuse. He could take the boy and swim for the surface and damn the tooth and damn the mission and damn the world. He could choose the small, human, decent thing over the large, necessary, monstrous thing. He could be
good
.

And the jaw would close. And the world would end. And the boy would die anyway, along with everyone else.

“I want to talk to him,” Kian said. “The boy. Before we decide. I want to look him in the eye and — I know he can’t speak. I know he can’t consent. But I want him to see that someone looked at him. That someone saw him as a person and not a price.”

Qadir studied him. Then nodded. “You have ten minutes.”

Kian found the boy in the corridor outside the throne room, sitting against a wall with his knees drawn up, watching the ghost courtiers drift past with the blank patience of someone who had learned that waiting was the only skill that mattered. He was thin. Dark-haired. His eyes, when they met Kian’s, were not blank at all — they were watchful, careful, the eyes of someone who had survived by reading other people’s intentions before those intentions became actions.

Kian sat beside him. The boy tensed, then didn’t move.

“I’m sorry,” Kian said. He didn’t know if the boy understood the words. He said them anyway. “I’m sorry that we’re here. I’m sorry that you’re part of this. I’m sorry that I can’t—” He stopped. Started again. “There’s a man here. Al-Rashid. He’s been alone for a very long time. He wants someone to stay with him. The captain wants that someone to be you.”

The boy looked at him. No expression. No reaction. Just those watchful eyes, reading Kian the way Kian read archaeological sites — looking for the structure beneath the surface, the truth beneath the words.

“I tried to stay instead,” Kian said. “They won’t let me. I’m — apparently I’m important. More important than—” He couldn’t finish the sentence. “I want you to know that I fought. That someone fought for you. That you weren’t just — handed over. Like cargo.”

The boy reached out and touched Kian’s arm. The left arm — the stump, where the hand had been. His fingers were light, careful, the touch of someone who understood damage. He looked at the stump, then at Kian’s face, and something passed between them that was not language but was communication — the recognition of one damaged person by another, the acknowledgment that the world took things from people and didn’t ask permission and didn’t apologize.

The boy nodded. Once. Small. Not consent — he couldn’t consent, not really, not from this position of absolute powerlessness. But acknowledgment.
I see you. I see that you tried. It wasn’t enough. It’s never enough. But you tried.

Kian went back to the chamber. Qadir and Fatima were waiting.

“Do it,” Kian said. The words tasted like ash. “Make the arrangement. But this goes on your ledger, captain. Not mine. I fought this. I lost. But I fought it.”

“Noted,” Qadir said. And went to make the arrangement.

Al-Rashid was delighted. He clapped his hands. He ordered more food. He showed the boy — who stood in the throne room with the blank, accepting expression of someone who had been traded so many times that the concept of ownership had lost its meaning — to a chamber that was clean and furnished and stocked with books that the boy couldn’t read and instruments that the boy couldn’t play and food that the boy couldn’t taste because it was conjured from a god’s memory and had no soul.

The tooth was surrendered. Al-Rashid removed it from his chest — literally, reaching into his own ribcage with fingers that passed through flesh like fingers through water, and extracting a black sphere that pulsed with cold light. He handed it to Kian.

“Four teeth,” Al-Rashid said. “Twenty-six to go. The jaw tightens.” He looked at the boy, who was sitting on the chamber’s bed, staring at nothing. “I will take care of him. I will feed him. I will talk to him, even though he cannot answer. I will be the best companion a mute boy trapped at the bottom of the sea could hope for.” He paused. “This is a very low bar. But I will clear it.”

They left. They swam upward, through the ghost city, past the ghost courtiers and the ghost servants and the ghost woman carrying the ghost child who would drown in four minutes and then laugh again and then drown again, forever.

Kian looked back once. Through the water, through the dark, he saw the palace — lit by the tooth’s glow, beautiful and terrible, a monument to loneliness and preservation and the specific cruelty of a world where the price of survival was always paid by someone who couldn’t afford it.

The boy sat at Al-Rashid’s table. The vizier was serving him food — real food, conjured food, ghost food. The boy ate. He didn’t taste it. He didn’t need to. He was alive, and fed, and captive, and alone, and the sea closed over him like a book closing over a page that no one would read again.

Kian carried the fourth tooth to the surface and said nothing, and the nothing was the heaviest thing he’d ever carried.

On the Dhow, Omar was waiting with food and questions. Kian answered neither. He went to the foredeck, pulled out the star chart from memory, and began plotting the next tooth’s position. The work was mechanical — stellar coordinates, angular adjustments, the cold mathematics of precession — and the mechanics were a mercy. Numbers didn’t judge. Numbers didn’t ask you to justify leaving a boy at the bottom of the sea.

“The fifth tooth,” he told Qadir an hour later, his voice flat, professional, emptied of everything except data. “The spiral points to the Strait of Sorrows. A Peri colony on volcanic spires. The stellar correspondence suggests we need to approach during the conjunction of both moons — that’s nine days from now.”

“Nine days,” Qadir said. “Good. The crew needs rest.”

“The crew needs more than rest. The crew needs to not hate us for what we just did.”

Qadir looked at him. “They won’t hate us. They’ll understand. Understanding is worse than hatred — hatred lets you pretend you’d have done differently.”

Kian went back to his calculations. The spiral tightened. The next tooth waited. And somewhere below them, in a drowned palace lit by a god’s tooth, a mute boy sat at a madman’s table and ate food that had no soul, and the sea closed over the memory of him, and the world moved on, because the world always moves on, and the moving is the cruelest thing about it.
