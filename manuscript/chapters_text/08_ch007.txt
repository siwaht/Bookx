Chapter 5: The Floating City

“A device that contains all knowledge and people use it to look at cats? Your world is stranger than ours.” — Omar

They returned to Iskandar three weeks later, and the city had changed.

Not physically — the barges still floated, the bridges still swayed, the markets still hummed with the commerce of a thousand cultures compressed into a space the size of a small town. But Kian had changed, and a changed man sees a changed world. The Floating City that had overwhelmed him on his first visit now revealed itself in layers, the way an archaeological site reveals itself to a patient excavator — surface first, then structure, then meaning.

He could feel the city now. The Marid called it
bahr-sense
, sea-sense, the ability to feel the currents of magic and memory that flowed through everything in Al-Khaliq. Iskandar, through this new sense, was not a city but a
confluence
— a place where the Sea of Tears’ currents converged, where memories pooled and concentrated, where the boundary between the physical and the remembered was thin enough to step through.

The magic was everywhere. In the Ink Market, the spell-papers glowed with residual Sihr that Kian could now see — faint auras of color that indicated potency, type, and danger. Most were weak, as Amir had said. Parlor tricks. But a few burned bright — genuine spells, powerful and expensive, hidden among the dross like diamonds in gravel. The vendors who sold them knew what they had. Their prices reflected it.

In the Flesh Bazaar, the body parts radiated their former owners’ memories — a thief’s hand twitched with the muscle memory of a thousand picked locks, a seer’s eye wept tears that showed fragments of possible futures. Kian looked away. The bahr-sense made the Bazaar worse, not better — he could feel the pain embedded in the merchandise, the trauma of severance, the ghost-ache of phantom limbs still attached to phantom bodies somewhere in the sea’s memory.

In the Chain District, the slaves’ despair was a physical weight. Kian felt it pressing on his chest, a gravity of suffering that the bahr-sense translated into something almost tangible. He walked through quickly, eyes forward, jaw set. He couldn’t save them. Not yet. Not with two teeth and a Marid and a map. But the
not yet
was new. Before, the impossibility of helping had been absolute. Now it was conditional. A matter of power. A matter of time.

Between the Chain District and the canal markets, there was a stretch of alley that Amir navigated with the practiced ease of someone who knew which shadows were safe and which ones had teeth. The alley smelled of fried dough and sewage and the particular, acrid sweetness of qat being chewed in doorways. Women leaned from upper windows — some selling laundry services, some selling other services, all of them watching the street below with the professional attention of people whose survival depended on reading strangers accurately.

A woman with hennaed hands and a voice like warm oil called down to Amir: “Little prince. Your mother’s been asking after you.”

Amir didn’t look up. “I don’t have a mother.”

“Everyone has a mother, little prince. Yours just happens to live in the sea.” The woman laughed — a rich, knowing laugh that carried no malice but considerable information. She looked at Kian. “New crew? He walks like a man who’s never been robbed. Someone should fix that before the Ink Market does.”

“He’s under my protection, Dalal.”

“Your protection and a copper coin will buy him a cup of bad tea. Tell Omar that Dalal says hello, and that he still owes me for the information about the Strait.”

“Omar owes everyone for everything. It’s his natural state.”

Dalal laughed again and withdrew from the window. Kian looked at Amir. “Who was that?”

“Dalal. She runs a house three barges over. The girls who work for her hear things — shipping routes, navy movements, which merchants are carrying what. Half the intelligence in Iskandar passes through Dalal’s house before it reaches the market. Omar buys from her regularly. He says she’s the most honest businesswoman in the city, which he means as a compliment, though Dalal says honesty is just a pricing strategy.”

A hand brushed Kian’s hip. He felt it through the bahr-sense before he felt it physically — a flicker of intent, quick and practiced, fingers reaching for the pouch at his belt. He turned. A girl, maybe ten, with a face like a sparrow and fingers like a pianist’s, was already three steps away, looking at nothing, walking at the precise speed of someone who was not running because running attracted attention.

She hadn’t gotten the pouch. The bahr-sense had warned him a half-second before the touch. But the attempt was so smooth, so professional, that Kian felt a grudging admiration alongside the alarm.

“Don’t chase her,” Amir said. “She works for Sitt Maryam. The whole crew does — twenty, thirty kids, all under twelve. Maryam takes a cut, but she also feeds them, houses them, keeps the worst of the city off their backs. It’s not a good system. It’s the system that exists.”

“Someone should—”

“Someone should do a lot of things. Someone should end slavery. Someone should feed the hungry. Someone should stop the sea from being made of tears.
Someone
is a word people use when they mean
not me
.” Amir’s voice was sharp, then softened. “Come on. The Mulberry Tree is close. And you need food more than you need outrage.”

This was dangerous thinking. The teeth wanted to be gathered, and the desire to gather them was exactly the kind of motivation they would exploit. But knowing a trap is a trap doesn’t make it less effective when the bait is genuine.

“You’re brooding,” Amir said, materializing at his elbow with two cups of tamarind juice, purchased from a vendor whose cart was built on the back of a sleeping turtle the size of a dining table. “You brood more than the captain, and the captain has been brooding professionally for decades.”

“I’m thinking.”

“Same thing, with scholars. Drink your juice. We’re going to the Mulberry Tree.”

“Why?”

“Because you need to eat a real meal in a real place with real people, and because Fatima is there, and the captain wants you to meet her before we sail.” Amir sipped his juice. “Also because I’m hungry, and Umm Salma’s kabsa is the best in Iskandar, and if I have to eat one more meal of Omar’s dried fish and flatbread, I will throw myself into the sea and become someone else’s memory.”

They walked. The city unfolded around them — bridges and barges and the constant, gentle motion of a world built on water. Kian drank his tamarind juice. It was sweet and sour and cold, and it tasted like nothing except itself, and the simplicity of it was a relief after weeks of the bahr-sense’s overwhelming input.

“Amir.”

“Yes?”

“In Faris’s compound. The Peri on the islands. The Roc, when we passed its territory last week. Every supernatural creature we’ve encountered has reacted to you differently than to the rest of us. They recognize you. They defer to you. Why?”

Amir’s step didn’t falter. His expression didn’t change. But something shifted behind his eyes — a door closing, a curtain drawing, the practiced concealment of someone who had been hiding something for a very long time.

“I’m likeable,” he said. “Even monsters appreciate good manners.”

“That’s not an answer.”

“It’s the answer you’re getting.” He smiled — the old-young smile, knowing and innocent. “Some questions are better left for later, scholar. When you’re ready for the answer. When the answer won’t break you.”

He turned down an alley that smelled of frying onions and cinnamon, and Kian followed, and filed the question away with all the other questions he was accumulating about the cabin boy who wasn’t quite a boy and knew too much and was recognized by monsters.

The Mulberry Tree was at the end of the alley.

It was built on a barge — a wide, flat-bottomed vessel that had been anchored in a quiet canal between two larger platforms, connected to the city by a single rope bridge that swayed with every step. The barge’s deck had been built up with walls of woven reed and a roof of palm fronds, creating a space that was half tavern, half garden. And in the center, growing through a hole cut in the deck, rising through the roof and spreading its branches over the entire structure like a canopy of green and purple, was a mulberry tree.

It was impossible. A tree growing through a barge, roots in the sea, trunk through wood, branches in the air — it should have been dead, drowned, rotted. Instead, it was magnificent. The trunk was thick as a man’s waist, the bark silver-gray and smooth, the leaves broad and dark green, and the mulberries — clusters of deep purple fruit hanging from every branch — filled the air with a scent that was sweet and earthy and alive.

“The tree was here before the city,” Amir said, ducking under a low branch. “Before the barge. Before the sea, some say. It grows from the memory of a garden that existed before Al-Khaliq — a garden where the first humans ate the first fruit and learned the first sorrow. The mulberries taste like whatever you need them to taste like. Try one.”

Kian picked a mulberry. It was warm, sun-ripened, bursting with juice. He put it in his mouth.

It tasted like his grandmother’s kitchen. Not a specific dish — the
kitchen itself
. The smell of saffron and rosewater. The warmth of the stove. The sound of his grandmother humming while she cooked. The feeling of being small and safe and loved in a room where food was being made.

His eyes stung. He swallowed the mulberry and said nothing.

“Told you,” Amir said softly.

The tavern was half-full — sailors, merchants, a few people who defied categorization, all seated on cushions around low tables, eating and drinking and talking in the easy, multilingual murmur of a port city’s social life. Lanterns hung from the mulberry’s branches, casting a warm, shifting light that made everything look slightly golden, slightly dreamlike. An oud player sat in the corner, playing something melancholy and beautiful that Kian’s bahr-sense identified as very old — a melody that had been played in this spot for centuries, absorbed by the tree, replayed by the musician, a loop of beauty that fed on itself.

Fatima was at a corner table, treating a sailor’s infected hand.

Kian saw her before she saw him, and the first thing he noticed was her hands. They were steady. Perfectly, unnaturally steady — the hands of a surgeon, moving with a precision that was almost mechanical, cleaning the wound, applying a poultice, wrapping a bandage, each motion economical and exact. She was talking to the sailor as she worked — quietly, calmly, the way you talk to a frightened animal — and the sailor, who was a large man with a face like a clenched fist, was nodding and looking at her hands with an expression of absolute trust.

The second thing he noticed was her face. She was not beautiful — not in the way that word is usually deployed, not in the symmetrical, conventional, magazine-cover sense. She was
striking
. Dark skin, high cheekbones, a nose that had been broken at least once and set slightly crooked, eyes that were the color of strong tea and moved with the same assessing intelligence as Qadir’s, but warmer. Her hair was black and thick and pulled back in a practical braid. She wore a surgeon’s apron over a simple tunic, and the apron was stained with blood and iodine and something green that might have been herbal.

She finished with the sailor, accepted his thanks with a nod, and looked up. Her eyes found Kian. They assessed him — quickly, thoroughly, the way she’d assessed the wound. He felt catalogued. Measured. Filed.

“You’re the scholar,” she said. Not a question.

“Kian.”

“Fatima.” She wiped her hands on a cloth. “Sit. You look like you haven’t eaten properly since you arrived in this world, and you’re right, because Omar’s idea of nutrition is bread and threats.” She gestured to the cushions across from her. “Amir, tell Umm Salma we need the full table. And tea. Three cups.”

Amir vanished into the kitchen with the enthusiasm of a boy who had been promised food. Kian sat. The cushions were soft, the table was clean, and the mulberry tree’s branches swayed overhead in a breeze that smelled of fruit and sea salt.

“Let me see your arm,” Fatima said.

He extended his left arm. She took it — her grip firm, professional, impersonal — and examined the map tattoo with the same focused attention she’d given the sailor’s wound. Her fingers traced the lines of the Sea of Tears, the coastlines, the pulsing dot that marked his position.

“Sihr,” she said. “Old Sihr. Pre-Caliphate. The script is—” She frowned. “I don’t recognize it. And I recognize most scripts.”

“Neither do I. And I’m an archaeologist.”

“Hmm.” She turned his arm, examining the spiral tattoo that Zulfaqar’s contract had left on his wrist. “This is newer. Marid-work. The binding is clean — whoever did this knew what they were doing.” She looked at him. “You made a deal with Zulfaqar.”

“Yes.”

“What did you pay?”

“My wedding day.”

Something flickered in her eyes — not pity, not judgment, but recognition. The look of someone who understood the weight of what had been lost because they had lost something similar.

“My mother paid her singing voice,” Fatima said quietly. “She was a healer. A kitchen witch, they called her — she used food as medicine, spices as surgery, cooking as Sihr. She bound a minor spirit to cure my brother’s fever. The price was her voice. She never sang again.” She released Kian’s arm. “She said it was worth it. My brother lived. But she never sang again, and the kitchen was quieter after that, and the food tasted different — still good, still healing, but missing something. A seasoning you couldn’t name.”

“I’m sorry.”

“Don’t be. She made her choice. You made yours. The sea runs on choices.” She straightened. “Now. You need to eat. And I need to assess your physical condition, because the captain is planning something that will require you to be stronger than you currently are, and you are currently—” She looked him over with clinical frankness. “—not strong.”

“I’ve been told.”

“Then stop being told and start eating. Ah—” She looked past him. “Here comes the food.”

Omar arrived first, because Omar always arrived where food was. He materialized at the table with the unerring instinct of a man whose internal compass pointed not north but toward the nearest kitchen, and he brought with him Leila, who came reluctantly, and a bottle of something amber that he set on the table with the reverence usually reserved for religious artifacts.

“Arak,” he announced. “From the Isle of Mirrors. Aged in oak. Distilled by monks who have taken a vow of silence, which is the only way to ensure quality — a distiller who talks is a distiller who isn’t paying attention.” He sat, cushions groaning under his weight. “I see we’re at the Mulberry Tree. Excellent. Umm Salma’s kabsa could make a dead man weep with joy. I should know — I’ve seen it happen. Literally. There was a ghul involved, but the point stands.”

Leila sat beside him, cross-legged, back straight, eyes scanning the room with the automatic vigilance of someone who had learned that relaxation was a luxury she couldn’t afford. She nodded at Fatima. Fatima nodded back. There was a warmth between them — not friendship, exactly, but the mutual respect of two women who had survived things that would have broken most people and had emerged not unbroken but
differently shaped
, like metal that had been through a forge.

The food arrived.

Umm Salma — the tavern’s owner, a round woman with hennaed hands and a laugh that could be heard three barges away — brought it herself, plate after plate, bowl after bowl, covering the low table until there was no wood visible, only food.

Kabsa. The dish that Omar had been rhapsodizing about since Kian had met him. A mountain of saffron rice, golden and fragrant, studded with raisins and almonds and topped with pieces of lamb that had been slow-cooked until the meat dissolved at the touch of a fork. The rice was infused with a spice blend that Kian’s bahr-sense identified as containing cardamom, cinnamon, cloves, black lime, and something he couldn’t name — something that tasted like warmth itself, like the concept of comfort rendered as a flavor.

“My mother made it better,” Omar said, already serving himself a portion that could have fed three people. “She used more butter. Everything is better with more butter. Life, food, women—”

A date hit him in the side of the head. Leila had thrown it without looking, her aim unerring, her expression unchanged.

“—I was going to say ‘women’s cooking,’” Omar finished, rubbing his temple. “Which is a compliment. Leila, your aim is as sharp as your personality, which is to say, terrifying.”

Harees came next. A porridge of wheat and chicken, slow-cooked until the grain had broken down into a smooth, creamy mass, topped with a swirl of cinnamon and a pool of golden ghee. It was simple — almost austere — and it was, Kian discovered when he tasted it, one of the most comforting things he had ever eaten. The wheat was soft and warm and slightly sweet, the chicken shredded so fine it was more texture than taste, and the ghee — rich, nutty, fragrant — tied everything together with the authority of a flavor that had been perfecting itself for centuries.

“My mother made this when I was sick,” Fatima said, serving herself a small portion with the precise movements of a woman who ate to fuel her body rather than to enjoy it. “She said the wheat absorbs sorrow. I don’t know if that’s true. But it tastes like it might be.”

Stuffed dates. Medjool dates, fat and glossy, split open and filled with a paste of almonds and cardamom and a touch of rosewater. They were sweet and rich and dense, each one a mouthful of concentrated pleasure. Amir ate six in rapid succession, his cheeks bulging like a squirrel’s. Omar ate twelve, methodically, with the focus of a man conducting serious research.

“Dates,” Omar said, holding one up like a professor with a specimen, “are the perfect food. They contain sugar for energy, fiber for digestion, and potassium for — well, I don’t know what potassium does, but I’m told it’s important. The Prophet, peace be upon him, broke his fast with dates. If dates are good enough for the Prophet, they are good enough for Omar ibn Khalid ibn Omar ibn—”

“We know your lineage,” Leila said. “Eat your dates.”

And then the tea.

Karak chai. Strong black tea, brewed until it was the color of mahogany, sweetened with condensed milk, spiced with cardamom and a thread of saffron that turned the surface gold. Fatima poured. She poured the way she did everything — precisely, attentively, with the focus of someone who believed that small acts deserved the same care as large ones.

“First cup for the guest,” she said, setting a glass before Kian. The glass was small, tulip-shaped, the tea steaming. “Second for friendship.” She poured for Omar, Leila, Amir. “Third to seal the deal.” She poured her own.

They drank. The tea was sweet and strong and spiced, and it warmed Kian from the inside out, and for a moment — just a moment — the weight of the world lifted. Not gone. Just lighter. Bearable. The way a heavy load becomes bearable when someone helps you carry it.

“So,” Omar said, leaning back on his cushions with the satisfied expression of a man who has eaten well and intends to eat more. “The scholar has joined us. He has a Marid in his chest and a map on his arm and the guilt of a man who has discovered that the world is not as kind as his textbooks suggested. He needs to know who we are. Not our names — he knows our names. Who we
are
.”

He looked around the table. Leila looked away. Fatima looked at her tea. Amir ate another date.

“I’ll start,” Omar said. “Because I always start. Because I am generous, and because no one else will.” He cleared his throat. “I am Omar. I was born in a port city that no longer exists — it sank when I was twelve, swallowed by the sea during a storm that was not a storm but a tantrum thrown by a Marid who had been insulted by the harbor master. I survived because I was on a ship. A merchant’s ship. The merchant took me as a cabin boy. He was not kind.” A pause. The jovial mask held, but the eyes behind it were old. “I learned to count on that ship. I learned to trade. I learned that everything has a price and everyone is for sale, including — especially — the people who say they aren’t. I left the merchant when I was sixteen. I left him floating. I joined the Dhow because the captain offered me something no one else had: a share. Not wages — a share. A piece of the profit. A stake in the outcome. I have been quartermaster for eleven years, and in that time, I have made this crew wealthy, kept this ship supplied, and eaten more than any three men combined. I am substantial.” He patted his belly. “I have layers. Like an onion. Like a good biryani.”

Leila spoke next, and the fact that she spoke at all told Kian something about the Mulberry Tree — about what this place meant to the crew, what the food and the tree and the lantern light permitted that the ship’s deck did not.

“I was a slave,” she said. Four words. She let them sit. “I was born a slave. I was raised a slave. I was sold seven times before I was fifteen. I learned to fight because fighting was the only thing they couldn’t take from me — they could take my freedom, my name, my body, but they couldn’t take the fact that I could break a man’s arm in three places before he finished his sentence.” She looked at her hands — scarred, calloused, the hands of a weapon. “The captain freed me. Not out of kindness — out of need. He needed a fighter. I was the best fighter he’d ever seen. He offered me a place on the Dhow. I took it. I’ve been first mate for eight years. I’ve killed—” She stopped. Counted. “—enough. I’ve killed enough.”

She didn’t say more. She didn’t need to.

Fatima’s turn. She held her tea glass in both hands, the steam rising between her fingers, and she spoke with the measured cadence of someone choosing each word the way she chose each surgical instrument — deliberately, for a specific purpose.

“I’m a surgeon. I trained in the Floating Colleges of Iskandar — three years of anatomy, two years of herbalism, one year of Sihr-medicine, which is the practice of healing without spirits, using only the residual magic in plants and minerals and the human body itself. My mother was a healer. A kitchen witch. She taught me that food is medicine and medicine is food and the line between them is drawn by people who don’t understand either.” She sipped her tea. “I joined the Dhow because the Dhow needed a surgeon and I needed a ship. The colleges expelled me for — well. For asking questions that the faculty preferred to leave unasked. About Sihr. About the cost of magic. About whether the cost could be reduced, redirected, shared. They said I was dangerous. They were right. I am dangerous. A surgeon who understands magic is the most dangerous person in any room, because she knows exactly how much damage a body can take and exactly how to inflict it.” She looked at Kian. “I don’t inflict it. I heal. But the knowledge is there. And knowledge, as you’re learning, is never neutral.”

Kian looked at her. She looked back. And something passed between them — not attraction, not yet, but recognition. The recognition of two people who had been shaped by knowledge, who had paid for knowledge, who understood that the pursuit of understanding was both the noblest and the most dangerous thing a person could do.

“Your turn, scholar,” Omar said.

Kian told them about his world. Not everything — not the politics, the history, the wars. The small things. Cars. Phones. The internet. Airplanes. Coffee shops. The concept of a weekend. The idea that you could go to a building full of books and read any of them for free.

They listened with the fascination of people hearing a fairy tale — which, Kian realized, was exactly what his world was to them. A fairy tale. A story about a place where magic didn’t exist and people had replaced it with machines, where no one sold body parts in markets and no one drowned in seas of memory and the worst thing that could happen to you was a divorce and a stalled career.

“A device that contains all knowledge,” Omar said slowly, “and people use it to look at cats?”

“Essentially.”

“Your world is stranger than ours.”

“A library,” Fatima said. “A building full of books. Free. For anyone.” She said it the way a thirsty person says
water
. “In Iskandar, books cost more than slaves. A single medical text — a good one, with accurate anatomy — costs more than I earn in a year. And in your world, they’re free.”

“They’re free.”

She looked at him with an expression he couldn’t read. “I think I would like your world, scholar. Despite the cats.”

Amir said nothing. He ate dates and listened and watched with those too-old eyes, and when the conversation turned to other things — Omar’s jokes, Leila’s dry commentary, the oud player’s increasingly melancholy repertoire — Amir caught Kian’s eye and smiled, and the smile said:
This is what we’re fighting for. Not the teeth. Not the world. This. A table. A meal. People who know each other’s stories.

Kian smiled back. And for one evening, in a tavern built around an impossible tree, eating food that tasted like comfort and drinking tea that tasted like friendship, the horror was far away, and the teeth were quiet, and the sea was just the sea, and the world was just the world, and that was enough.
