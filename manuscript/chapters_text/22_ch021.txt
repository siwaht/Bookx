Chapter 18: The Drunken Night

“Laughter is the only spell that costs nothing.” — Fatima’s mother

The Isle of Mirrors was neutral ground.

Ancient wards — older than the Caliphate, older than the sea, carved into the island’s bedrock by hands that might have been human and might have been divine — prevented violence within its borders. Swords wouldn’t cut. Arrows wouldn’t fly. Fists wouldn’t connect. The wards didn’t distinguish between justified and unjustified violence, between self-defense and aggression, between a punch thrown in anger and a slap delivered in jest. All force was nullified. The island was, in the most literal sense possible, a safe space.

The crew needed a safe space.

Eleven teeth. Qadir dying. Fatima changed. The trap tooth’s damage still healing. The spiral tightening. The crew was fraying — not from external threats but from the accumulated weight of what they’d done and what they’d lost and what they were becoming. They needed a night. One night. A pause in the relentless arithmetic of teeth and sacrifice and the slow, grinding approach of the end.

Omar found the tavern. Of course Omar found the tavern — Omar could find a tavern in a desert, in a blizzard, at the bottom of the sea. This one was called The Honest Liar, which Omar said was “the most accurate name for a drinking establishment I’ve ever encountered, and I’ve encountered many.”

It was built into the island’s cliff face — a series of caves connected by passages, lit by lanterns that burned in colors that shifted with the music. And there was music — drums, oud, a singer with a voice like smoke and honey, performing songs that were old when the Caliphate was young, songs about love and loss and the sea and the specific, universal experience of being human in a world that was not designed for human comfort.

The place was full. Sailors, merchants, smugglers, and the people who lived off all three — the pickpockets and card sharps and women who sold company by the hour and information by the minute. A woman in a green silk dress sat on a raised platform near the back, smoking a water pipe and receiving visitors like a queen holding court. She had kohl-lined eyes and a scar that ran from her left ear to her collarbone, and the men who approached her did so with the deference usually reserved for people who could have you killed.

“Zahra al-Khadra,” Omar said, following Kian’s gaze. “The Green Lady. She runs the pleasure houses on the island. Also the information trade. Also, if the rumors are accurate, a small fleet of smuggling vessels disguised as fishing boats. She knows everything that happens on the Sea of Tears because her girls hear everything, and men who are paying for company tend to forget that the company has ears.”

“You know her?”

“I’ve done business with her. She’s fair. She charges too much, but she delivers what she promises, which puts her ahead of ninety percent of the merchants I’ve dealt with.” Omar waved. Zahra saw him, raised her pipe in acknowledgment, and returned to her visitor — a naval officer who was speaking urgently and quietly and didn’t seem to realize that the two women refilling his drink were memorizing every word.

At a corner table, a thin man with restless hands was playing tawla against himself. Both sides. He’d move a piece on one side, then walk around the table and move a piece on the other, muttering calculations under his breath, his fingers tapping the board between moves with the compulsive rhythm of a man whose body couldn’t be still because his mind never was.

“Hashem the Dice,” Omar said, and his voice carried something Kian hadn’t heard before — not contempt, not pity, but a kind of wary recognition. “The finest gambler on the Sea of Tears. Also the most ruined. He’s won fortunes — three, four times over. Lost them all. Won them back. Lost them again. He plays the way some men drink — not for the winning but for the
playing
, for the moment when the dice are in the air and the world holds its breath and anything is possible. The winning is almost an inconvenience. It means the game is over.”

Hashem looked up. His eyes were bright and sunken, the eyes of a man who slept rarely and ate less, who had traded the ordinary human needs for the single, consuming need to play. He saw Omar and his face split into a grin that was missing two teeth.

“Omar ibn Khalid,” Hashem called. “You owe me forty dinars from Iskandar.”

“You owe me sixty from the Strait of Sorrows.”

“I owe you nothing. That game was rigged.”

“All games are rigged. The question is who rigged them. Sit down, Hashem. Drink with us. You look like you haven’t eaten since the last time you won.”

“I ate yesterday. Or the day before. Time is — time is a distraction from the game, Omar. You know this.” But he came, bringing his tawla board under his arm like a child carrying a beloved toy, and he sat with them, and his fingers never stopped moving — tapping the table, rolling an invisible die, shuffling invisible cards. Even at rest, Hashem was playing.

They drank.

Omar drank arak — the anise liquor that turned milky with water, served in small glasses, consumed in quantities that would have killed a smaller man and merely made Omar more eloquent. He told jokes. Terrible jokes. Jokes that were so bad they circled back around to good, that were so unfunny they became funny through sheer commitment to unfunniness.

“Why did the djinn cross the sea?” Omar asked, his third glass of arak in hand, his face flushed, his eyes bright with the specific joy of a man who has found his audience and intends to hold it hostage.

“Why?” Amir asked, because someone had to.

“Because the sea was already inside him.” Omar paused. Frowned. “Wait. That’s not funny. That’s theology.” He drank. “Why did the Marid refuse to grant the wish?”

“Why?”

“Because the wish was for a smaller Marid, and Marid have professional pride.” He drank again. “What’s the difference between a merchant and a pirate?”

“What?”

“The merchant charges you before he robs you. The pirate is at least honest about the transaction.” He raised his glass. “To honesty. The most expensive luxury in Al-Khaliq.”

Leila arm-wrestled a merchant. The merchant was twice her size — a broad, bearded man who traded in timber and had forearms like ship’s masts. He sat across from her at a table, planted his elbow, and grinned with the confidence of a man who had never lost an arm-wrestling match and didn’t intend to start.

Leila put him down in four seconds. The merchant stared at his defeated arm as if it had betrayed him. Leila’s expression didn’t change.

“Again?” she asked.

The merchant’s bodyguard stepped forward — a larger man, scarred, professional. Leila put him down in six seconds.

“Again?”

The bodyguard’s horse was tied outside. Leila looked at it. The horse looked at Leila. Some form of communication passed between them — warrior to warrior, predator to predator.

“The horse won,” Omar reported later, with the delight of a man who had witnessed something unprecedented. “Leila arm-wrestled a horse and the horse won. She demanded a rematch. The horse declined. I believe the horse was wise.”

Kian tried to explain quantum physics to a spice trader. The trader — a small, patient man with hennaed hands and a turban that contained, Kian suspected, more spice samples than fabric — listened with the polite attention of someone who had no idea what was being said but was too well-mannered to interrupt.

“So the particle exists in all possible states simultaneously,” Kian said, gesturing with his one hand, his prosthetic (a crude device of driftwood and wire that Fatima had built and that worked poorly but was better than nothing) resting on the table. “Until you observe it. The act of observation collapses the wave function and—”

“Fascinating,” the trader said. “Would you like to buy some cardamom? Very good quality. From the highlands.”

Kian bought the cardamom. It was overpriced. He didn’t care.

Samira got into an argument with a fortune-teller. The fortune-teller — a wizened woman with one milky eye and a deck of cards made from fish scales — had set up shop in a corner of the tavern and was offering readings for a copper coin. Samira, four hundred years old and a former member of Tiamat’s coven, sat down across from her and said, “Tell me my future.”

The fortune-teller laid out the cards. She studied them. She looked up at Samira with her one good eye and said, “You will die soon. Violently. Protecting someone you love.”

Samira stared at her. “That’s — that’s actually accurate.”

“Of course it’s accurate. I’m a professional.”

“No, I mean — the sea told me the same thing. Months ago. You’re confirming a prophecy I’ve already received.”

“Then why are you wasting my time? And your copper coin?” The fortune-teller swept up her cards. “Go away. I don’t do confirmations. I do revelations. Come back when you want to be surprised.”

Samira returned to the table looking shaken. Fatima asked what was wrong. Samira said, “Nothing. A charlatan confirmed my death. I’m fine.” She drank Omar’s arak. Omar didn’t object, which was how everyone knew Samira was not fine.

Umm Hani, who had been dragged to the tavern against her will (“Taverns are for people who can’t cook. I can cook. Therefore taverns are beneath me.”), spent the evening critiquing the tavern’s food with the merciless precision of a professional reviewing an amateur’s work.

“The bread is stale,” she announced, tearing a piece and examining its interior with the focus of a surgeon examining a wound. “Not old-stale — lazy-stale. The baker didn’t knead long enough. Kneading is patience. This bread was made by an impatient person. I can taste the impatience. It tastes like regret.”

“Umm Hani, please don’t insult the tavern’s cook,” Omar said. “We’re guests.”

“I am not insulting. I am educating. There is a difference. Insults are personal. Education is a gift.” She bit into an olive. “The olives are acceptable. The hummus is adequate. The lamb is—” She chewed. Considered. Chewed again. “The lamb is an insult. Not to me — to the lamb. This animal died for nothing. It deserved better. We all deserve better.”

The tavern’s cook, a large man with a face like a thundercloud, emerged from the kitchen to investigate the source of the commentary. He and Umm Hani locked eyes across the room. What followed was a twenty-minute argument about the proper temperature for roasting lamb that was conducted entirely in cooking metaphors and ended with the cook admitting that his mother’s recipe was inferior to Umm Hani’s theoretical improvements, and Umm Hani admitting that the cook’s flatbread was “not terrible, which is the highest compliment I give to strangers.”

They exchanged recipes. The cook wept. Umm Hani did not weep, but she patted his hand, which was the Umm Hani equivalent of a standing ovation.

Meanwhile, Leila had gotten into a different kind of trouble. She’d been drinking steadily — not arak but a local spirit made from fermented dates that was, according to Omar, “strong enough to strip the barnacles off a hull and the sense off a sailor.” Leila drank it like water. Leila drank everything like water. Leila’s relationship with alcohol was the same as her relationship with everything else: aggressive, efficient, and conducted without visible emotion.

Until it wasn’t.

“I can’t swim,” Leila said.

The table went quiet. Leila — first mate of the Black Dhow, the most feared fighter on the Sea of Tears, the woman who had arm-wrestled a horse — couldn’t swim.

“I was nearly drowned,” she continued. Her voice was flat. Her eyes were fixed on the table. “As a slave. Punishment for fighting back. They held me under. In a barrel. For — I don’t know how long. Long enough that I stopped fighting. Long enough that the water went into my lungs and the world went dark and I thought — I thought,
this is it, this is how it ends, in a barrel, in the dark, and no one will know my name.
” She drank. “They pulled me out. Not because they were merciful — because I was valuable. Dead slaves don’t sell. But the water — the water stayed. Inside me. I can feel it. Every time I look at the sea, I feel the barrel. Every time the ship rolls, I feel the hands holding me under.”

No one spoke. The silence was not the awkward silence of people who don’t know what to say. It was the respectful silence of people who understood that some confessions are not invitations to respond but acts of release, and the release requires space, and the space requires silence.

“I am the first mate of a ship,” Leila said. “And I cannot swim. And I am terrified of the water. Every day. Every hour. Every moment I stand on that deck and look at the sea, I am terrified.” She looked up. Her eyes were dry. Leila’s eyes were always dry. “And I do it anyway. Because the terror is mine, and the doing-it-anyway is mine, and the mine is the only thing they couldn’t take from me in the barrel.”

Omar reached across the table and put his hand on hers. Leila didn’t pull away. This was unprecedented. Leila didn’t allow touching. Leila’s personal space was defended with the same ferocity she applied to everything else. But tonight, in the safety of the warded island, with the arak in her blood and the confession in the air, she let Omar’s hand rest on hers, and the letting was a kind of bravery that made the arm-wrestling look trivial.

Amir disappeared for an hour. When he returned, he had information about three tooth locations — specific, detailed, actionable intelligence that would have taken Omar’s network weeks to gather. No one asked how. Amir’s sources were Amir’s business, and Amir’s business was conducted in shadows and whispers and the specific currency of secrets that the cabin boy traded in with the fluency of a native speaker.

Fatima drank for the first time.

She’d never been a drinker — her mother’s faith, her surgeon’s discipline, her general preference for clarity over comfort had kept her away from alcohol. But tonight, in the safety of the warded island, in the warmth of the tavern, in the company of people who had bled for her and whom she had bled for, she accepted a glass of arak from Omar and drank.

She was a lightweight. Two glasses and the surgeon’s composure dissolved — not into chaos but into something softer, something younger, something that the inquisitors’ torture had buried but not killed. She laughed. She told Kian his beard was “architecturally unsound — it leans to the left, like a minaret in an earthquake.” She told Omar his jokes were “the verbal equivalent of food poisoning — unpleasant but ultimately cleansing.” She told Leila that her arm-wrestling technique was “surgically perfect, which is the highest compliment I know how to give.”

She danced. Not well — Fatima danced the way she fought, with precision and no grace, her body following a rhythm that was slightly out of sync with the music. But she danced, and the dancing was an act of defiance — a declaration that the inquisitors had broken her fingers but not her feet, had burned her skin but not her spirit, had taken her faith but not her ability to move through a room full of music and feel, however briefly, alive.

Samira danced too. The witch hadn’t danced in decades — not since before Nazia’s coven, before the centuries of servitude, before the dungeon and the torture and the slow erosion of everything that had once made her human. She danced now — slowly, carefully, her scarred body moving with a grace that was not practiced but remembered, the muscle memory of a woman who had once been young and free and had loved to dance.

Fatima watched Samira dance. And the expression on Fatima’s face — visible to Kian, visible to Omar, visible to anyone who was paying attention — was not the surgeon’s composure or the witch’s calculation or the warrior’s assessment. It was something simpler. Something warmer. Something that Omar, elbowing Kian with the subtlety of a man who had never been subtle about anything, identified with a raised eyebrow and a knowing grin.

“Interesting,” Omar murmured.

“Shut up,” Kian said.

“I said nothing. I merely observed. Observation is not commentary. Observation is science. You’re a scientist. You should appreciate—”

“Shut up, Omar.”

Later — after the dancing, after the music slowed, after Omar had gone to argue with the tavern cook about the proper ratio of fat to meat in a lamb kebab — Kian saw them.

Samira and Fatima, outside, on the cliff path above the tavern. The two moons were up, casting double shadows, and the sea below was silver and black and still. Samira was sitting on a rock, her shawl around her shoulders, and Fatima was beside her, close enough that their arms touched. They were not talking. They were just sitting. Together. In the particular silence of two people who have run out of things to say and discovered that the silence is better than the words.

Then Fatima leaned her head against Samira’s shoulder. A small gesture. The kind of thing a tired person does without thinking — the body seeking warmth, seeking contact, seeking the simple animal comfort of another body nearby. But Fatima was not a person who did things without thinking. Every gesture was chosen. Every touch was deliberate. And this touch — her head on Samira’s shoulder, her eyes closed, her breathing slow — was a choice. A surrender. The surgeon putting down her instruments and being, for a moment, just a woman who was tired and afraid and wanted to rest against someone who felt safe.

Samira didn’t move. She barely breathed. Four hundred years old, and she was afraid to move, afraid that any shift would break the spell, would remind Fatima that she was leaning on a witch, on a woman, on a complication that neither of them had the vocabulary to name.

“I can’t feel warmth anymore,” Fatima said. Her voice was quiet. “The volcano took it. I can feel heat — dangerous heat, burning heat. But not warmth. Not the kind that means comfort.” She pressed closer. “I can’t feel you. Not the way I should. I know you’re warm. I know your shoulder is warm. But I can’t feel it. It’s just — pressure. Contact without temperature.”

“I’m sorry,” Samira said.

“Don’t be. I’m telling you because—” Fatima paused. “Because I want you to know that when I lean against you, it’s not because I can feel your warmth. It’s because I remember it. From before. And the remembering is enough.”

Samira’s hand found Fatima’s. Their fingers interlaced. Samira’s were scarred and old and steady. Fatima’s were broken-healed and young and steady. Two steady hands, holding each other in the moonlight, on an island where violence was impossible and tenderness was, for one night, permitted.

“The fortune-teller said I’ll die protecting someone I love,” Samira said. “I used to think that was a curse. Now I think it might be the only good death available.”

“Don’t talk about dying.”

“I’m four hundred years old. Dying is the most interesting thing I haven’t done yet.”

Fatima laughed. A small, broken laugh, the kind that lives next door to crying. “You’re impossible.”

“I’m possible. I’m just unlikely.” Samira squeezed her hand. “Go back inside. Drink more arak. Tell Kian his beard is architecturally unsound again. He liked that.”

“What about you?”

“I’ll sit here a while. The moons are beautiful. I haven’t watched moons in decades. I’ve been too busy being ancient and terrifying.”

Fatima stood. She looked down at Samira — the witch, the teacher, the woman who had given her power and was watching the power consume her and loved her anyway, loved her in the specific, devastating way that people love the things they know they’re going to lose.

She bent down and kissed Samira’s forehead. Gently. The way you kiss something precious. The way you kiss something goodbye.

Then she went back inside, and Samira sat on the cliff in the double moonlight, and the sea was silver and black and still, and the fortune-teller’s words hung in the air like smoke:
You will die soon. Violently. Protecting someone you love.

Samira smiled. It was, she thought, a good fortune. The best she’d had in four hundred years.

Kian turned away from the window. He’d seen enough. The triangle held — the strongest shape in architecture, the most destructive shape in love. But tonight, on the warded island, the destruction looked like tenderness, and the tenderness looked like the most honest thing any of them had felt since the teeth began their hungry work.

Hashem, who had been drinking steadily and losing at tawla against himself — a feat that required a special kind of dedication — materialized at Kian’s elbow. His fingers were doing their restless dance on the table, and his eyes had the feverish brightness of a man who had found something more interesting than dice.

“Your friend,” Hashem said, nodding at Omar. “He carries four purses. I’ve been watching. The left inner pocket, the right boot, the sash, and one sewn into the lining of his robe. The boot purse is the heaviest. Gold, not silver — gold clinks differently, shorter, sweeter. I can hear it when he walks.”

“Why are you telling me this?”

“Because the boy who just bumped into him on the way to the privy lifted the sash purse, and I thought you should know before your friend discovers it and breaks the wards trying to kill someone.”

Kian looked. A boy — not Amir, a different boy, maybe fourteen, with quick hands and a face designed for forgetting — was weaving through the crowd toward the exit. Omar was still talking, still gesturing, still unaware.

“How did you—”

“I watch hands. It’s what I do. Cards, dice, purses — it’s all the same game. The hand moves, the eye follows, the mind calculates.” Hashem’s fingers tapped faster. “Also, I know the boy. Rafi. He works for a woman named Sitt Maryam who runs a crew of child thieves out of the harbor district. Good crew. Professional. Rafi’s the best — he could lift the rings off a sleeping djinn. But he’s sloppy tonight. Nervous. Sitt Maryam must need the money badly to send him into a warded zone where he can’t run if he’s caught.”

Kian caught Rafi at the door. The boy looked up at him with eyes that were too old for his face — the eyes of a child who had learned that the world was a series of transactions and he was always on the losing end. He didn’t try to run. He didn’t try to lie. He just held out the purse and said, “Please don’t tell the fat one. He’ll find Sitt Maryam and she’ll beat me.”

“How old are you?”

“Old enough to steal. Too young to stop.”

Kian took the purse. He looked at the boy — the thin wrists, the patched clothes, the careful blankness of a face that had learned to show nothing because showing something was dangerous. He thought of Amir. He thought of Omar at twelve. He thought of all the children in this world who had been made into tools by adults who should have protected them.

He gave the purse back.

“Tell Sitt Maryam that the fat one is Omar ibn Khalid, and that Omar ibn Khalid remembers what it’s like to be a child who steals to survive. Tell her if she needs work — real work, not theft — to find us at the harbor. The Black Dhow is always short-handed.”

Rafi stared at him. Then he vanished into the night with the purse, quick and silent as a fish in dark water.

“You gave him the money,” Hashem said. He’d followed Kian to the door, his tawla board still under his arm.

“I gave him a chance.”

“Same thing, in this world.” Hashem’s fingers stopped their tapping — the first time Kian had seen them still. “You’re a strange man, scholar. You carry teeth that could buy cities, and you give a thief’s boy a purse of silver.” He looked at Kian with something that might have been respect, or might have been the gambler’s assessment of a man who made irrational bets. “I like you. You play the game wrong. That’s the most interesting way to play.”

He went back to his table and resumed his game against himself. Both sides lost. Both sides won. Hashem didn’t seem to notice the difference.

Late. Drunk. Honest.

They sat in a circle on the tavern floor — cushions and cups and the debris of a night that had been, for a few hours, free of teeth and monsters and the weight of the world. The music had stopped. The other patrons had left. The lanterns burned low, casting the cave in a warm, amber light that made everyone look younger, softer, more like the people they’d been before the sea had claimed them.

Omar spoke first. Because Omar always spoke first.

“I’m afraid,” he said.

The words landed in the silence like a stone in still water. Omar — jovial, calculating, armored in jokes and appetite — was afraid. The admission was so unexpected, so contrary to everything the quartermaster projected, that the crew went still.

“Not of dying,” Omar continued. He was looking at his hands — thick, scarred, the hands of a man who had counted and traded and fought his way from a cabin boy’s hell to a quartermaster’s authority. “I’ve been dying since I was twelve. Every day on that merchant’s ship was a day I might not survive. I’m not afraid of dying. I’m afraid of becoming.”

“Becoming what?” Kian asked.

“What the teeth want. What the power wants. What the world wants.” He looked up. His small, bright eyes were wet. “I was a cabin boy. They did things to me. The merchant. His crew. Things I don’t talk about. Things I’ve never talked about. And I swore — I swore on the sea and the moons and every god I could name — that I would never be powerless again. That I would become strong. That I would become the one who decides, not the one who is decided for.”

He paused. Drank. The arak burned, and the burning was a punctuation mark.

“And I did. I became strong. I became the quartermaster. I became the man who counts and trades and decides. And somewhere along the way, I started doing to others what was done to me. Not the worst of it. Not the — not that. But the intimidation. The casual cruelty. The hazing. The way I make new recruits afraid, the way I use my size and my voice and my authority to remind people that I am in charge and they are not.” He looked at the crew. “I tell myself it toughens them. I know it doesn’t. I tell myself it’s necessary. I know it isn’t. I tell myself I’m not the merchant. I know I’m closer to him than I want to admit.”

No one spoke. The silence was not uncomfortable. It was sacred — the silence of a confession, the silence of a truth that had been carried alone for too long and was finally being set down.

“I am not a good man,” Omar said. “But I am trying to become one. Is that enough?”

Kian looked at the quartermaster — the fat, jovial, terrifying, brilliant, damaged, generous, cruel, kind man who had fed him bread on a storm-tossed deck and taught him the rules of magic and counted the stores with obsessive precision and told terrible jokes to fill the silence that would otherwise be filled with screaming.

“It’s a start,” Kian said.

Omar nodded. “I’ll take a start. Starts are underrated.”

They stumbled back to the Dhow at dawn, leaning on each other — literally, physically, the weight of their bodies shared the way the weight of their secrets had been shared, distributed among shoulders that were stronger together than apart. Umm Hani had breakfast ready — eggs scrambled with tomatoes and onions, flatbread warm from the oven, honey in a clay pot, coffee strong enough to raise the dead and bitter enough to remind them why they’d been buried.

“Fools,” Umm Hani said, watching them collapse onto the deck with the coordinated gracelessness of people who had drunk too much and slept too little. “Beautiful, stupid fools.”

She was smiling. The smile was the warmest thing on the ship.
