Chapter 15: The Karkadann

“Nine pearls, nine pieces of bread. One for each sin we’ll commit to get the rest.” — Umm Hani

Leila went alone.

This was not discussed. It was not debated. It was not submitted to the crew for a vote or presented to the captain for approval. Leila simply announced, over breakfast — Umm Hani’s fasulia and flatbread, the democratic meal, the food for survivors — that she would be going ashore on the island of the Karkadann, alone, to retrieve the two teeth that the bahr-sense had located in the volcanic crater at the island’s center.

“Alone,” Omar repeated. He set down his bread. This was significant — Omar setting down food was the equivalent of another man drawing a weapon. “You want to go alone to an island inhabited by Persian unicorns the size of rhinoceroses with horns made of meteoric iron, alone.”

“I said alone. You don’t need to repeat it.”

“I’m repeating it because I’m hoping that hearing it aloud will make you realize how insane it sounds.”

“It sounds efficient. The Karkadann respond to single challengers. They’re territorial, not aggressive — they fight to defend, not to attack. A group triggers a herd response. A single person triggers a duel. I can win a duel.”

“Against a creature the size of a horse with a horn that can punch through ship’s timber.”

“I’ve fought worse.”

“Name one thing worse.”

“You, when someone eats the last date.”

Omar opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again. “That is — that is not — I am not—” He looked at Kian for support. Kian, wisely, was eating his fasulia and staying out of it. “Fine. Go alone. Die alone. I’ll write your eulogy. It will be short. ‘She was stubborn. She was brave. She was wrong. The Karkadann ate her.’”

“Karkadann are herbivores.”

“Then it will gore you and leave you for the vultures. The point stands.”

Leila looked at him. The look was — complicated. Not angry, not amused, not the dry, cutting expression that was her default. Something softer. Something that acknowledged Omar’s concern without accepting it, that recognized the care behind the bluster without letting it change her mind.

“I need to do this alone,” she said. Quietly. Without the edge. “Not because I’m brave. Because I’m afraid. And the only way I’ve ever beaten fear is by walking into it. Alone. Without anyone to catch me if I fall.”

The table was quiet. Omar picked up his bread. He didn’t argue further.

The island was volcanic — a cone of black rock rising from the sea, its slopes covered in scrub grass and wildflowers that had no business growing on volcanic soil but grew anyway, because life in Al-Khaliq was stubborn and unreasonable and refused to accept the limitations that geology imposed. The crater at the top was wide and shallow, a bowl of green grass and clear water, and in the grass, the Karkadann grazed.

They were magnificent.

Leila saw them from the crater’s rim — a herd of maybe twenty, spread across the grass like boulders that had learned to move. They were the size of large horses, but built like rhinoceroses — thick-bodied, short-legged, armored with hide that was gray and rough and scarred from decades of territorial combat. Their horns were singular — a single spike rising from the forehead, long and straight and gleaming with a metallic sheen that the bahr-sense (Kian had taught Leila to use a rudimentary version, channeled through a charm Samira had made) identified as meteoric iron. Not bone. Not keratin.
Iron
. Fallen from the sky, grown from the skull, harder than any blade.

The alpha was obvious. Larger than the others, darker, with a horn that was longer and more scarred, standing at the herd’s center with the relaxed vigilance of a creature that had never been challenged by anything it couldn’t defeat. It saw Leila. It assessed her. It lowered its horn.

Leila descended into the crater.

She carried her twin short swords. She wore light armor — boiled leather, fitted close, allowing maximum mobility. Her hair was cropped close. Her feet were bare — she fought better barefoot, the ground’s texture feeding information to her body the way the bahr-sense fed information to Kian’s mind.

The alpha watched her approach. The herd parted — not fleeing, but clearing space, the way spectators clear space for a fight they know is coming. The Karkadann understood duels. They lived by duels. Their entire social structure was built on the principle that disputes were settled by combat, and combat was settled by skill, and skill was the only currency that mattered.

Leila stopped ten meters from the alpha. She planted her feet. She raised her swords.

The alpha charged.

It was fast. Impossibly fast for something that size — a blur of gray hide and meteoric iron, covering the ten meters in less than a second, the horn aimed at Leila’s chest with the precision of a lance. The ground shook. The air displaced. The charge was not just physical but
atmospheric
, a wall of force that preceded the horn like a shockwave.

Leila moved.

Not away —
into
. She stepped forward, into the charge, inside the horn’s arc, so close to the Karkadann’s body that she could feel its heat and smell its breath (grass and iron and something sweet, like crushed flowers). Her swords flashed — not cutting, not stabbing, but
redirecting
, the blades catching the horn’s shaft and guiding it past her body the way a matador’s cape guides a bull. The horn missed her by inches. The Karkadann thundered past.

It turned. Charged again. Leila moved again — the same technique, the same impossible precision, stepping into the charge and redirecting the horn with her blades. The Karkadann was learning. Its third charge was different — a feint, the horn dipping left and then sweeping right, a move that would have gutted a less experienced fighter.

Leila read it. Not consciously — her body read it, the way a musician reads a score, the way a dancer reads a rhythm. She had been fighting since she was a child. She had been fighting for her life since she was a slave. Her body knew combat the way Kian’s mind knew archaeology — instinctively, completely, in the bones.

She didn’t kill the alpha. She didn’t try. She fought it to a standstill — parrying charges, redirecting horns, dancing around the massive body with a speed and grace that was not human but was not supernatural either. It was
trained
. Decades of training, compressed into a body that had been forged by slavery and tempered by freedom, a weapon that had made itself.

The duel lasted twenty minutes. At the end, the alpha was breathing hard, its flanks heaving, its horn lowered not in aggression but in exhaustion. Leila was breathing hard too — sweat-soaked, trembling, her arms aching from the repeated impacts of horn on blade. But she was standing. And the alpha was yielding.

It knelt. The massive body folded, the front legs bending, the horn touching the grass. Leila would call it submission. Respect, even. But standing there, breathing hard, her arms shaking, she wasn’t sure. The alpha’s dark eyes held something she couldn’t read — it might have been acknowledgment, or it might have been the calculation of a creature that had chosen not to kill and was reserving the right to change its mind.

Leila sheathed her swords. She walked to the center of the crater, where the two teeth lay in the grass — black spheres, half-buried in the volcanic soil, pulsing with the familiar hunger. She picked them up. The alpha watched. The herd watched. None of them moved.

She walked back to the crater’s rim. At the edge, she turned. The alpha was still kneeling, its massive head raised, its dark eyes fixed on her with an expression that was — she would never admit this, not to Omar, not to anyone — beautiful. The respect of a creature that had never been matched, offered to a woman who had never been respected, in a language that required no words.

She raised a hand. She didn’t know if the gesture meant anything to the creature. She didn’t know if what had happened in the crater was a duel or a conversation or something she had no word for.

The alpha rose. It watched her go. It did not follow.

She returned to the Dhow at sunset. Nine teeth now. Omar was waiting at the rail.

“You’re alive,” he said.

“Disappointed?”

“Relieved. Also hungry. Umm Hani made kubaneh to celebrate your inevitable survival, which she predicted with the confidence of a woman who has never been wrong about anything and intends to maintain her record.”

Leila almost smiled. The almost-smile that was rarer than Omar’s silence and more valuable than any tooth.

“Nine teeth,” Omar said, raising a cup of tea. “We’re almost a third of the way to ending the world. Shall I open the good arak?”

“Save it. We’ll need it later.”

“Later is a concept I distrust. Later implies a future, and futures are unreliable. I prefer now. Now is honest. Now has kubaneh.”

They ate. Umm Hani’s kubaneh, golden and warm, torn by hands and shared by mouths, the bread that was food and communion and prayer. Leila ate more than usual. The fight had left her hungry — not just physically but emotionally, the way intense experience creates a void that only simple pleasures can fill.

Samira watched Fatima across the table. She watched the way Fatima tore the bread — precisely, the surgeon’s habit of making clean breaks — and the way Fatima’s fingers lingered on the honey jar. Kian saw it.

Fatima saw Samira watching. She didn’t look away.

“You’re staring,” Fatima said to Samira. Not accusatory. Not embarrassed.

“You have honey on your chin,” Samira said.

“I do not have honey on my chin.”

“You do. Just there.” Samira reached across the table and wiped the honey from Fatima’s chin with her thumb. The gesture was quick, casual, the kind of thing a mother does for a child or a friend does for a friend. But Samira’s hand lingered — just for a moment, just long enough for the touch to become something other than practical — and Fatima’s breath caught, just slightly, just enough for Kian to notice, and the noticing added another layer to the complicated thing in his chest.

Omar, who noticed everything and commented on most of it, opened his mouth. Kian kicked him under the table. Omar closed his mouth. This was, in the history of the Black Dhow, possibly the most significant kick ever delivered, because it prevented Omar from saying something that would have been accurate and devastating and would have forced a conversation that none of them were ready to have.

The honey was wiped. The moment passed. The kubaneh was eaten. The complications remained, filed away in the spaces between people where the unsaid things lived, growing quietly, like coral, like the reef, like everything in this world that was alive and patient and waiting.

That night, Leila taught Fatima to hold a sword. Not to fight — Fatima was a surgeon, not a warrior, and her hands were too valuable to risk in combat. But to hold. To feel the weight. To understand the balance.

“A sword is a tool,” Leila said, adjusting Fatima’s grip. “Like a scalpel. The difference is intent. A scalpel intends to heal. A sword intends to harm. But the skill is the same — precision, control, the understanding that what you hold in your hand can save or destroy, and the choice is yours.”

Fatima held the sword. She was terrible at it — her grip too tight, her stance too narrow, her weight on the wrong foot. Leila corrected her with the patient, exacting attention of a teacher who cared about the student more than the subject.

They both laughed. It was the first time anyone had heard Leila laugh — a sound that was rusty and unpracticed and surprised, as if the laugh had been stored in a closet for years and had emerged blinking into the light, unsure of its welcome.

Omar, watching from the shadows, smiled. He didn’t say anything. Some moments were too important for jokes.
