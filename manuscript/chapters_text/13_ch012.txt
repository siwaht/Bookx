Chapter 9: Umm Hani’s Galley

“A kitchen is a kingdom if you rule it right.” — Umm Hani

The galley of the Black Dhow was the size of a generous closet.

It occupied a space between the crew quarters and the cargo hold, wedged into the ship’s anatomy like an organ — which, Kian was beginning to understand, was exactly what it was. If the Dhow was alive, the galley was its stomach. The stove was a brick-and-iron construction bolted to the deck, vented through a copper chimney that rose through the ceiling and out the main deck, and it burned a fuel that Omar called “sea-coal” — a black mineral dredged from the shallows that burned hot and slow and smelled faintly of cinnamon, which was either a property of the mineral or a consequence of decades of Umm Hani’s cooking permeating the brickwork.

The galley had rules. They were not posted. They did not need to be. Umm Hani enforced them with the authority of a woman who had survived slavery, piracy, and the daily challenge of feeding thirty people on a budget that Omar described as “theoretical,” and who had emerged from these experiences with a set of convictions that were as immovable as the stove itself.

Rule one: no weapons in the galley. Swords, knives (other than cooking knives), clubs, bows, and “anything that could be used to harm another person, which includes Omar’s singing” were to be left at the door. The galley was neutral ground. A sanctuary. Anyone who objected was welcome to cook their own meals, alone, on the deck, in the rain.

Rule two: everyone eats. No one was permitted to skip a meal. Not the sick (Umm Hani brought food to the infirmary), not the grieving (Umm Hani brought food to the grieving), not the punished (Umm Hani brought food to the brig, on the rare occasions the brig was occupied, and glared at whoever had ordered the imprisonment until they felt appropriately guilty). Food was not a reward. Food was not a privilege. Food was a right, and Umm Hani defended it with the ferocity of a woman who had known what it was to have that right denied.

Rule three: you will compliment the cook. This was not vanity. This was, Umm Hani explained, “the tax you pay for eating. I cook. You eat. You say ‘thank you, Umm Hani, this is delicious.’ This is the social contract. Violate it, and I will put salt in your coffee for a week.”

Kian learned the galley during his recovery. Fatima had confined him to light duty — no climbing, no diving, no heavy lifting, no “anything that might reopen the wound or cause you to fall into the sea, which in your case includes standing near the rail and looking thoughtful.” This left him with little to do except think, which Fatima had also discouraged (“Thinking is how you got into this mess”), and eat, which Umm Hani had enthusiastically encouraged.

So he sat in the galley. On a stool in the corner, out of Umm Hani’s way but within her orbit, watching her cook and listening to her talk and learning, without intending to, the most important lessons of his time in Al-Khaliq.

“I was born in Yemen,” Umm Hani said. She was making jachnun — the rolled dough that she baked overnight in the ship’s oven, emerging in the morning as golden, flaky, buttery spirals that the crew tore apart with their hands and ate with honey and a sharp tomato relish. The rolling was meditative — thin sheets of dough, brushed with clarified butter, rolled tight, coiled into spirals, placed in a heavy pot. Her hands moved with the unconscious precision of someone who had performed this action ten thousand times.

“A village in the mountains. Small. Poor. Beautiful, in the way that poor places are beautiful — because the beauty is not purchased but grown, not designed but inherited. The terraces. The stone houses. The qat trees on the hillside. The smell of bread baking in the tannour.”

She rolled another sheet. Thin as a secret. Tight as a fist.

“I was taken when I was fourteen. Slavers. They came at night, which is when slavers always come, because darkness is the slaver’s ally and the slave’s enemy. They took twelve of us. Girls, mostly. We were worth more.” She said this without bitterness — with the flat factuality of someone who had processed the horror long ago and reduced it to data. “I was sold in Iskandar. To a merchant. Then to a ship’s captain. Then to another merchant. Then to a quarry. Then to a household. Then to another ship.” She counted on her fingers. “Seven owners. Seven years. Seven kitchens.”

“Seven kitchens?”

“Every owner put me in the kitchen. Because I could cook. Because my mother taught me to cook, and my grandmother taught my mother, and her grandmother taught her, back and back and back to the first woman who put a pot on a fire and said, ‘This is mine. This space is mine. What happens in this space, I control.’ The kitchen was the only place I had power. The only place where I decided what happened. The master could own my body. He could not own my bread.”

She placed the rolled jachnun in the pot. Covered it. Set it on the stove’s lowest heat, where it would bake through the night, the butter melting into the dough, the layers separating and crisping, the whole thing transforming from raw flour and fat into something golden and warm and alive.

“Leila freed me,” she said. “During a raid. The Dhow attacked the ship I was on — a merchant vessel, carrying spices and slaves, which is the same thing in this world: cargo. Leila cut my chains. She didn’t ask my name. She didn’t ask if I wanted to be free. She cut the chains and moved on to the next person. That’s Leila. She doesn’t rescue — she
liberates
. The difference is that rescue implies you need saving. Liberation implies you were always free and someone just removed the obstacle.”

“And you stayed on the Dhow.”

“Where would I go? My village is gone — swallowed by the sea, like everything is eventually swallowed by the sea. My family is dead or scattered. I have no home, no country, no people. The Dhow is my home. The crew is my people. The galley is my country.” She wiped her hands on her apron. “And the galley needs a queen. Every kingdom needs a queen. I am the queen of this galley, and my subjects eat well, and my laws are just, and my bread is the best in the Sea of Tears. This is not boasting. This is fact.”

Kian smiled. It hurt — smiling pulled muscles that were connected, through some anatomical conspiracy, to the stump of his left wrist — but it was genuine.

“You’re smiling,” Umm Hani observed. “Good. Smiling means the food is working. Food heals what surgery cannot. The surgeon fixes the body. The cook fixes the soul. Both are necessary. Neither is sufficient alone.” She began preparing the tomato relish — fresh tomatoes, crushed with a mortar and pestle, seasoned with salt and a green chili that she handled with the respect due to a small but potent adversary. “Now. Tell me about your world’s food.”

“My world’s food?”

“You come from another world. Another world has other food. Tell me. I am always learning. A cook who stops learning is a cook who starts poisoning.”

Kian told her about pizza. About sushi. About tacos and pad thai and croissants and the specific, unreproducible perfection of his grandmother’s tahdig — the crispy rice from the bottom of the pot, golden and crunchy and fought over by every member of the family at every meal.

Umm Hani listened with the intensity of a scholar encountering a new text. She asked questions — about ingredients, about techniques, about the cultural contexts of each dish. She was particularly fascinated by the concept of a restaurant.

“A place where strangers cook for you? And you pay them? And you don’t know them? And they don’t know you?” She shook her head. “In my village, food was made by people who loved you. The love was the ingredient. Without it, the food is just — fuel. Calories. Sustenance without substance.” She paused. “But your grandmother’s rice. The crispy part. The part everyone fights over. That has love in it. I can hear it in your voice.”

“It does.”

“Then your world is not so different from mine. The food is different. The love is the same.” She set the relish aside. “Tomorrow, I will try to make your grandmother’s rice. You will tell me if I succeed. If I fail, you will tell me that too, because honest criticism is the cook’s best friend and flattery is the cook’s worst enemy. Agreed?”

“Agreed.”

“Good. Now go. I need to prepare dinner, and you are taking up space that could be occupied by onions. Onions are more useful than scholars. They make people cry for honest reasons.”

Kian left the galley and almost tripped over Omar, who was lying in the corridor outside the galley door, asleep.

Not sleeping —
hiding
. The quartermaster had wedged himself into the narrow passage between the galley and the crew quarters, his bulk filling the space like a cork in a bottle, and he was pretending to sleep with the theatrical commitment of a man who had been caught doing something embarrassing and had decided that unconsciousness was preferable to explanation.

“Omar.”

A snore. Exaggerated. Performative.

“Omar, I can see your eyes moving.”

The quartermaster opened one eye. “I am resting. Resting is essential for the body’s recovery. I read this in a medical text.”

“You can’t read.”

“I had it read to me. By a woman of considerable learning and questionable judgment. The point stands.” He closed the eye. “Go away. I am convalescing.”

“You’re hiding from Leila.”

Both eyes opened. The jovial mask slipped, and underneath was something Kian hadn’t seen before — genuine, childlike embarrassment. Omar was
embarrassed
.

“She caught me,” Omar said quietly.

“Caught you doing what?”

“Eating. From the emergency stores. The dried apricots. The ones I told the crew were infested with weevils and had to be thrown overboard.” He paused. “There were no weevils. I ate them. All of them. Over the course of three weeks. In the middle of the night. Like a rat.”

Kian stared at him. “You — the quartermaster, the man who counts every grain of rice, who lectures the crew about rationing — you’ve been stealing from your own stores?”

“It’s not stealing if you’re the quartermaster. It’s — reallocation. Strategic reallocation of resources to the crew member most in need of caloric supplementation.” He patted his belly. “I am that crew member.”

“Omar.”

“I know. I know. It’s pathetic. A fat man sneaking food in the dark like a child raiding the pantry. Leila saw me last night — she was on watch, and I thought she was asleep, and she wasn’t asleep, and she looked at me with those eyes — you know the eyes, the eyes that say
I have killed men for less than this
— and she said nothing. She just looked. And the looking was worse than anything she could have said.”

He sat up, his bulk rearranging itself in the narrow corridor with the grace of a walrus on a beach. “I eat when I’m afraid, scholar. I eat when I’m sad. I eat when I’m angry. I eat when I’m happy. I eat when I’m bored. I eat when I’m thinking. I eat when I’m not thinking. I eat because the eating fills a space that was emptied when I was twelve years old on a merchant’s ship, and the space has never been filled by anything else — not money, not power, not jokes, not the respect of the crew. Only food. Only the act of putting something in my mouth and chewing and swallowing and feeling, for one moment, full.”

He looked at Kian. The embarrassment was gone. What remained was something rawer — the honesty of a man who had been caught in a small, shameful act and had decided, in the catching, to confess the larger shame behind it.

“Don’t tell Umm Hani,” he said. “She’ll put me on a diet. And a diet would kill me faster than any tooth.”

“Your secret’s safe.”

“It’s not a secret. It’s a weakness. Secrets are interesting. Weaknesses are just — sad.” He hauled himself to his feet. “I’m going to apologize to Leila. Not for the apricots — for lying about the weevils. The lying is worse than the eating. The lying always is.”

He lumbered down the corridor, and Kian watched him go, and thought about the spaces inside people that never get filled, and the things they use to try.

He found Fatima on the foredeck, sitting cross-legged with Samira, their heads bent together over something Kian couldn’t see. As he approached, he realized they were practicing — Samira was teaching Fatima to speak to the wind.

The exercise was simple: Samira held a feather in her open palm and whispered to it. The feather rose, hovered, danced in patterns that followed Samira’s words — spirals, figure-eights, lazy circles. Then Fatima tried. She held the feather, whispered, and the feather — exploded. Not dramatically. It simply came apart, each barb separating from the shaft, the shaft splitting into fibers, the fibers dissolving into the air. Where the feather had been, a small whirlwind spun for three seconds and then dissipated.

“Too much,” Samira said. Her voice was patient but her eyes were worried. “You’re not speaking to the wind. You’re
commanding
it. The wind doesn’t respond to commands. It responds to requests. Politely. With respect.”

“I was polite.”

“You were precise. Precision is not politeness. Precision is a scalpel. Politeness is an open hand.” Samira produced another feather. “Again. Softer. Ask the wind to lift the feather. Don’t tell it.”

Fatima tried again. The feather rose — smoothly this time, gently, riding an invisible current that Fatima’s whisper had created. It hovered at eye level, turning slowly, catching the light.

“Better,” Samira said. And then, so quietly that Kian almost missed it: “You’re a natural. That terrifies me.”

Fatima heard. Her expression didn’t change — the surgeon’s composure held — but something shifted in her eyes. Not pride. Not fear. Something more complex. The recognition that she was becoming something new, something powerful, something that the world she’d been born into had no category for, and that the becoming was both gift and threat.

Kian sat beside them. His stump ached. The bandage was clean — Fatima changed it twice a day, with the meticulous care of a surgeon who understood that infection was a more dangerous enemy than any reef.

“How’s the hand?” Fatima asked. The question was professional — tone neutral, eyes assessing — but her fingers, when she reached for his bandage to check it, were gentle in a way that went beyond medical necessity.

“Gone,” Kian said. “Still gone. Surprisingly, it hasn’t grown back.”

“Give it time. In this world, stranger things have happened.” She unwrapped the bandage, examined the stump, rewrapped it. Her hands were warm. Her touch was careful. “The healing is good. Clean. No infection. Umm Hani’s fenugreek is doing more than my poultices, honestly. Don’t tell her I said that. She’ll be insufferable.”

“She’s already insufferable. In the best way.”

“In the only way that matters.” Fatima tied off the bandage. Her fingers lingered on his wrist — the remaining wrist, the right one, where the Marid’s tattoo spiraled. “Kian.”

“Yes?”

“The reef took your hand. Zulfaqar took your wedding. The ghost took your mother’s voice. You’ve been in this world for — what? Two months? And you’ve already lost—” She stopped. Recalculated. The surgeon assessing damage, tallying costs. “You’ve already lost too much.”

“I’ve gained things too.”

“What?”

He looked at her. At Samira, who was practicing with the feather, her scarred hands gentle, her storm-green eyes focused. At the Dhow, which hummed beneath them, alive and warm. At the sea, which glowed with other people’s memories and held, somewhere in its depths, the remaining teeth of a god who wanted to eat the world.

“This,” he said. “All of this. The crew. The ship. The — the meals. The conversations. The terrible jokes. The—” He stopped. He was not good at this. He was a man who studied dead things, who catalogued and classified and kept the world at arm’s length, and the words for what he was feeling were not in his professional vocabulary. “You,” he said. “I gained you.”

Fatima looked at him. The surgeon’s composure held. But beneath it, something moved. Something warm.

“That’s—” She paused. Chose her words. “That’s a terrible trade. A hand and a wedding and a mother’s voice for a ship’s surgeon with a broken nose and a bad temper.”

“I didn’t say it was a fair trade. I said it was what I gained.”

She almost smiled. The almost-smile that he was learning to read, that meant more than other people’s full smiles, that was rationed and precious and given only when the moment deserved it.

“Eat your dinner,” she said. “Umm Hani is making kubaneh. If you miss it, she’ll be offended, and an offended Umm Hani is a force that even the Marid respects.”

She went below. Kian sat on the foredeck and watched the sea and felt the stump of his left wrist throb in time with the map on his right arm.

That evening, the crew ate together.

Not in the galley — the galley was too small for thirty people. On the main deck, under the stars, seated on coils of rope and upturned buckets and the deck itself, with plates balanced on knees and cups wedged into the scuppers. Umm Hani had made kubaneh — the pull-apart bread, golden with butter, warm from the oven, filling the deck with a scent that was part bakery and part temple.

The bread was served with honey — dark, wild honey that Omar had traded for at the last port — and with Umm Hani’s fasulia, the white bean stew that she called “food for survivors” and that was, Kian had to admit, exactly that: simple, filling, democratic, a dish that made no distinction between captain and cabin boy, between scholar and sailor, between the powerful and the powerless.

They ate. They talked. The conversation was the ordinary, unremarkable conversation of people who lived and worked together — complaints about the weather, arguments about navigation, Omar’s ongoing campaign to convince Umm Hani to use more butter (“More butter is always the answer, regardless of the question”), Leila’s dry commentary on everyone’s combat readiness (“The scholar fights like a man trying to return a library book. The cabin boy fights like a cat in a sack. Omar fights like a man who has eaten too much butter”).

Kian ate with one hand. It was awkward — tearing bread required two hands, and he had one, and the bread was hot, and the honey was sticky, and the fasulia required a spoon that kept sliding off his knee. He managed. Badly. With the graceless determination of a man who refused to ask for help and was clearly, obviously, desperately in need of it.

Fatima sat beside him. Without comment, without fuss, without making it feel like charity, she tore his bread for him. She held his bowl while he spooned the fasulia. She poured his tea. She did these things with the same precision she brought to surgery — efficiently, attentively, without wasted motion — and she did them without looking at him, without acknowledging that she was doing them, as if helping a one-handed man eat dinner were the most natural thing in the world.

It was. In this crew, on this ship, in this world — it was.

Samira watched them. The witch’s storm-green eyes moved between Kian and Fatima with an expression that Kian’s bahr-sense read as
longing
— the ache of someone who had once been cared for and had forgotten what it felt like.

Omar raised his cup. “Nine pearls,” he said. (They had three. Omar was counting ahead, with the optimism of a man who believed that positive thinking was a valid accounting practice.) “Nine pearls, nine pieces of bread. One for each sin we’ll commit to get the rest.”

“We have three pearls,” Leila said.

“I’m investing in futures. The bread market is bullish. Drink your tea.”

They drank. The stars turned overhead — alien constellations that Kian was learning to read, that Ibn Majid’s chart was teaching him to navigate, that would guide them to the next tooth and the next and the next, spiraling inward toward the center of the sea and the end of the story.

Umm Hani collected the plates. She washed them in the galley, alone, humming a Yemeni lullaby that Kian couldn’t hear — not because she was quiet but because the memory of his mother’s voice was gone, and all lullabies now existed in a register he could no longer access.

But he could see her humming. He could see her lips move, her body sway, her hands gentle on the plates. And the seeing was enough.

The ship sailed. The crew slept. The sea remembered.

And in the galley, Umm Hani set the jachnun to bake, and the smell of butter and dough filled the ship like a prayer, and the morning came with the promise that there would be food, and the food would be warm, and the warmth would be enough.

Kian found Qadir on the quarterdeck at dawn, the captain’s hands on the wheel, his eyes on the horizon. The star chart was burning in Kian’s mind — not metaphorically, but with the low, persistent heat of a calculation that demanded to be finished.

“The next tooth,” Kian said. “The spiral points south-southeast. The stellar correspondence is Al-Qalb — the Scorpion’s Heart. The tooth should be at a depth of—” He paused, running the numbers. “Three hundred meters. Maybe more. The chart shows a concentration of old Sihr at those coordinates. Something preserved. Something large.”

“The Sunken Caliphate,” Qadir said. He didn’t look surprised. “Al-Rashid’s domain. I’ve been avoiding it for eleven years.”

“The spiral doesn’t give us a choice. The sequence is the sequence. If we skip it—”

“The jaw snaps shut. I know.” Qadir’s hands tightened on the wheel. “How long to reach it?”

“Two weeks, if the currents hold. But captain—” Kian hesitated. This was the part he’d been turning over in his mind all night, the part that the star chart implied but didn’t state. “The spiral is tightening. The first three teeth were spread across thousands of miles. The next cluster is closer together. After the Caliphate, the chart shows two more teeth within a week’s sail of each other. The pace is accelerating.”

“Or the danger is concentrating.”

“Both. The teeth are closer together because the Sihr is denser near the center. Denser Sihr means stronger guardians, higher costs, less margin for error.” He met Qadir’s eyes. “We need to talk to the crew. Not just orders — a real conversation. About what’s coming. About the costs. They deserve to know what the spiral looks like from here.”

Qadir was quiet for a long time. The sea glowed beneath them. The broken moon hung in the sky like a question.

“Tonight,” the captain said. “After dinner. We’ll talk.”

It was not agreement. It was not refusal. It was Qadir — a man who made decisions the way the sea made waves, slowly and inevitably and with the full weight of everything behind them.

Kian went below to eat Umm Hani’s jachnun, and the honey was sweet, and the bread was warm, and the spiral tightened, and the next tooth waited in the dark.
