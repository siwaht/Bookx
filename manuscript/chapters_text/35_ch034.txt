Epilogue

Years later.

Omar opened a tavern in Iskandar. He called it The Mulberry Tree — the same name as the tavern where they’d eaten kabsa and harees and stuffed dates and drunk karak chai, the tavern where Omar had told terrible jokes and Leila had thrown a date at his head and Kian had laughed for the first time in this world. The new Mulberry Tree was built on a barge, like the original, with a real mulberry tree growing through the deck, and lanterns hanging from the branches, and a musician playing oud in the corner — something melancholy and beautiful, always.

Omar cooked Umm Hani’s recipes. He was not as good as Umm Hani — no one was as good as Umm Hani — but he was good enough, and good enough was, he had learned, underrated. He made kabsa with too much butter, because everything is better with too much butter. He made fasulia and served it to anyone who was hungry, regardless of whether they could pay. He made kubaneh on the anniversary of Leila’s death and tore it and threw pieces into the harbor, and the pieces floated for a moment before the sea took them, and Omar chose to believe that the sea appreciated the gesture, and the sea, which was Kian, did.

He got fat. He got happy. He got old. These three things were not contradictory but complementary — the fatness a consequence of the happiness, the happiness a consequence of the living, and the living a consequence of the loving, and the loving the only thing that mattered.

He died in his sleep at seventy-three, in a bed above the tavern, with the sound of the sea coming through the window and the smell of kabsa coming from the kitchen, where his daughter — named Leila, of course, named Leila — was cooking breakfast. His last thought was of pistachios. This seemed right.

Amir disappeared.

She left Iskandar on a small boat, alone, sailing east toward the edge of the world, toward the place where the sea met the sky and the sky met the nothing and the nothing met the everything. Some said she became a Peri — a bird-woman of the volcanic islands, singing in Old Pahlavi, her song something new, something that healed instead of hurt, something that gave memories back instead of taking them away.

Some said she was still looking for a way to bring Kian back. That she sailed the Sea of Tears searching for a spell, a ritual, a loophole in the contract between a man and an ocean. That she would never stop, because stopping would mean accepting, and the guilt was the only thing that kept her moving.

Some said she had already found the next person to summon. That somewhere, in another world, a woman had found a map in a bottle washed up on a beach, and the map was drawn on skin, and when she touched it, it bled. That the cycle was beginning again, because cycles always begin again, because the sea always calls, because the sea is lonely, because the sea is Kian, and Kian was always lonely, and loneliness is the one thing that becoming a god does not cure.

No one knew which story was true. Perhaps all of them were. The sea is large enough to contain contradictions.

Umm Hani stayed on the Dhow.

The ship, somehow, stopped rotting. Mastema’s curse — the death sentence measured in weeks — lifted the day Kian became the sea, as if the curse had been aimed at a man and not a ship, and the man was gone, and the curse had nothing left to grip. The wood hardened. The ropes tightened. The sails mended themselves in the night, the patchwork fabrics stitching together with the patient industry of a ship that had decided it was not finished yet.

Umm Hani sailed the Dhow alone. Not alone — the ship was alive, had always been alive, and the aliveness was company enough. She sailed from port to port, picking up passengers, delivering cargo, cooking for whoever was aboard. Her galley remained her kingdom. No weapons allowed. No rank. In the galley, the captain ate the same as the cabin boy. She enforced this with a wooden ladle and a glare that could curdle milk.

She talked to the sea.

Every night, after the dishes were washed and the galley was clean and the passengers were sleeping, Umm Hani went to the rail and spoke to the water. She told it about her day — the meals she’d cooked, the people she’d fed, the small dramas and comedies of life on a ship that sailed a sea made of tears. She told it jokes she’d heard in port. She told it recipes she was developing. She told it about the weather, and the stars, and the price of saffron in Iskandar, and the way Omar’s daughter made kubaneh that was almost as good as Umm Hani’s but not quite, because not quite was the space where love lived, the gap between good and perfect that could only be filled by the specific hands of a specific person.

The sea listened. The sea always listened. And sometimes — not always, not often, but sometimes — the current shifted, and the waves calmed, and the ship rocked gently, and Umm Hani knew that the sea was answering, in the only language it had left.

“I told you this ship would hold,” she said to the water, every night, the same words, the same tone, the same unshakeable certainty. “I told you.”

The ship held.

Fatima sailed a small boat to the center of the Sea of Tears.

She did this once a year, on the anniversary of the day she had put a knife through the heart of the man she loved. She sailed alone — a small dhow, single-masted, the kind of boat that a woman could handle by herself if the woman was determined and the sea was kind, and the sea was always kind to Fatima, because the sea was Kian, and Kian had never been anything but kind to her, even at the end, even when kindness was the cruelest thing he could have offered.

She was older now. The years had done what years do — softened the edges, deepened the lines, turned the black hair silver at the temples. But her hands were still steady. They would always be steady. Steadiness was her gift and her curse, the thing that made her a surgeon and a killer and a woman who could pour karak chai with the same hands that had destroyed a god’s jaw.

She anchored the boat at the center of the sea — the place where the water was deepest, where the memories were densest, where the currents converged in a pattern that was not random but
intentional
, the pattern of a consciousness that was vast and diffuse but still, in some fundamental way,
present
.

“I brought you karak chai,” she said to the water. “Two sugars, the way you like it.”

She poured it into the sea. The dark tea spread across the surface — cardamom and saffron and condensed milk, the recipe from the Mulberry Tree, the recipe that Fatima had memorized on the night they’d first eaten together, the night Omar had told terrible jokes and Leila had thrown a date and Kian had laughed and the laughter had been the first real thing in a world of impossible things.

The water rose.

Not a wave. Not a current. A
shape
— a pillar of liquid memory that climbed from the surface like a hand reaching up, like a plant growing toward light, like a man standing up after a long time lying down. The pillar shaped itself. It found a form. It became a face.

Kian’s face.

Blue and translucent and terrible and beautiful — the face of a man who was also an ocean, rendered in water and light and the particular shimmer of a sea that was made of tears. The features were his — the nose, the jaw, the eyes that had been brown and were now the color of deep water, the color of memory, the color of a grief so vast it had become a geography. The face was not alive. It was not dead. It was
present
— the way a mountain is present, the way the sky is present, the way the sea is present: always, everywhere, without effort, without end.

It looked at her.

It could not speak. The sea has no voice — not in the way that humans have voices, not in the way that words are formed and meanings are conveyed through the mechanical apparatus of tongue and teeth and air. But it looked at her, and the looking was a language, and the language said everything that words could not:
I am here. I am still here. I am the water that carries your boat and the current that guides your sail and the tide that brings you to this place every year, and I will be here next year and the year after and the year after that, and the being here is not a sentence but a choice, and the choice is love, and the love is the only thing that survived the transformation, the only thing that the sea could not dissolve, the only thing that salt preserves forever.

Fatima reached out and touched the water-face.

It was cold. It was alive.

The touch lasted a moment — a single moment, the duration of a heartbeat, the time it takes for a wave to crest and fall. And in that moment, the current shifted, and the boat rocked gently, and Fatima felt something on her hand — not water, not cold, but
warmth
. A ghost of warmth. A memory of warmth. The warmth of a man’s hand holding hers on the deck of a dying ship, the warmth of a man’s body beside hers in the dark, the warmth of a man who had been human and was now something else but was still, in the ways that mattered, still
him
.

She smiled. The smile was not happy. The smile was not sad. The smile was the smile of a woman who has learned that love does not end when the beloved changes form — that love is not a contract between two bodies but a current between two souls, and currents do not stop flowing just because the river becomes the sea.

“Same time next year,” she said.

The water-face held for a moment longer. Then it dissolved — gently, slowly, the features softening and spreading and returning to the sea, the face becoming waves, the waves becoming water, the water becoming everything.

Fatima sailed home. The sea carried her. The sea always carried her.

And somewhere — in another world, on another shore, under a different sky with a single, unbroken moon — a woman found a map.

It was in a bottle, washed up on a beach of white sand, tangled in seaweed and crusted with salt. The bottle was old — green glass, hand-blown, sealed with wax that had darkened with age. The woman picked it up because she was curious, and curiosity is just hunger wearing a scholar’s robe, and she was hungry, and the hunger had a name, and the name was
more
— more than this beach, more than this life, more than the ordinary world with its ordinary rules and its ordinary grief.

She broke the seal. She unrolled the map.

It was drawn on skin. The ink was brown-red, the color of old blood. It showed a sea that didn’t exist — a sea called
Bahr al-Dumou
, the Sea of Tears — and the sea was vast and strange and full of islands and monsters and cities that floated and ships that flew and a darkness in the deep that was also a man who was also a god who was also a cage.

She touched the map.

The map bled.

The sea was calling. The sea always calls. The sea is made of tears, and the tears are made of memory, and the memory is made of salt, and salt preserves nothing forever.

But it tries.

God help it, it tries.

End of The Black Dhow
