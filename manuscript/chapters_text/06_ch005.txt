Chapter 3: The Village of Qasim

“Before the first move, the game is already won by those who wrote the rules.”

They docked at Iskandar in the gray hour before dawn, when the city was at its quietest and the sea was the color of old silver. The Dhow slid between the outer barges with the silence of a knife entering water — no splash, no wake, as if the ship and the sea had an understanding about discretion.

Kian stood on deck and watched the city materialize around him.

Up close, Iskandar was less beautiful and more astonishing. The barges that formed its foundation were enormous — some the size of football fields, lashed together with chains as thick as Kian’s thigh, their surfaces built up with structures of wood and stone and coral that had been growing and accreting for centuries. Streets ran between buildings that were also boats, alleys threaded through hulls that had been gutted and repurposed, and everywhere — on every surface, every wall, every door — there were carvings. Geometric patterns, calligraphic inscriptions, images of sea creatures and ships and storms, layered over each other in a palimpsest of decoration that made the city look less built than
written
, a text in architecture that no single lifetime could read.

The smell hit him next. Fish — fresh, dried, smoked, rotting. Spices — cardamom, cumin, something sharp and citric he couldn’t identify. Incense — frankincense and myrrh and oud. Sewage. Salt. Bread baking. Coffee roasting.

“Welcome to Iskandar,” Omar said, appearing beside him in a fresh tunic — clean, embroidered, almost respectable. The quartermaster had transformed from a pirate into a merchant with the ease of a man changing hats. “The jewel of the Sea of Tears. The crossroads of the world. The only city where you can buy a man’s soul before breakfast and sell it back to him at a profit by lunch.” He adjusted his belt, which now held a purse instead of a weapon. “Stay close. Don’t touch anything. Don’t eat anything I haven’t approved. And if anyone offers you a deal that sounds too good to be true, it is, and the penalty for default is usually anatomical.”

The crew dispersed into the city with the practiced efficiency of people who had done this many times. Sailors went to resupply — water, food, sailcloth, the mundane necessities of maritime life. Others went to sell cargo from the Dhow’s hold — Kian didn’t ask what cargo, and no one volunteered the information. Qadir disappeared into the city alone, moving with a purpose that suggested a specific destination and a desire not to be followed.

Kian was assigned to Amir.

“Think of me as your guide,” the cabin boy said, leading Kian down a gangplank onto the nearest barge. “Your translator. Your guardian. Your—”

“My babysitter.”

“I was going to say ‘cultural attaché,’ but yes. Essentially.” Amir moved through the waking city with the confidence of a native, dodging early-morning vendors setting up stalls, stepping over sleeping drunks, navigating a maze of alleys that seemed to shift and reconfigure as they walked. “The captain wants you to see the city. To understand what we’re fighting for. Also, he wants you out of the way while he conducts business that you’re better off not knowing about.”

“What kind of business?”

“The kind that involves people who would find a scholar from another world very interesting and very valuable, and not in ways you’d enjoy. The flesh-scribes, for instance. They’d pay a fortune for a man who can read dead languages. They’d tattoo spells on your skin and sell you by the word.” Amir glanced back. “Don’t worry. The captain won’t sell you. Probably.”

“Probably?”

“I said don’t worry.”

They walked. Kian catalogued.

The Ink Market occupied a cluster of barges near the city’s center, connected by bridges hung with paper lanterns that burned in colors that didn’t exist in nature — a blue that was also a sound, a red that was also a temperature, a green that tasted of mint when you looked at it too long. The stalls sold spells.

Not books of spells, not scrolls of incantation, not the paraphernalia of stage magic. Actual spells, written on rice paper in inks made from substances Kian didn’t want to identify, folded into packets the size of postage stamps. You bought one, you ate it, and the spell took effect. The side effects, according to a cheerful vendor who demonstrated by eating a paper that made his left hand glow blue for thirty seconds, included “mild nausea, prophetic dreams, and occasional conversations with furniture.”

“The spells are weak,” Amir explained, steering Kian past a stall selling papers that allegedly cured impotence. “Parlor tricks. Real Sihr requires a spirit. These are just… echoes. Residue. Like the heat left in a pan after the fire goes out. Useful for small things. Useless for anything that matters.”

“People buy them?”

“People buy hope. The papers are just the packaging.”

They moved on. The Flesh Bazaar was worse.

It occupied the lower levels of a massive barge that had once been a warship, its gun ports converted into display windows. The merchandise was body parts. Not butchered meat — preserved, functional, magical body parts, displayed on velvet cushions like jewelry. A hand that had belonged to a thief, its fingers still nimble, still twitching, guaranteed to open any lock. An eye from a seer, floating in a jar of amber fluid, that would show you tomorrow if you pressed it to your own empty socket. A tongue from a poet that would make anyone who heard you speak fall in love. A heart from a warrior that would make you fearless, though the vendor noted with professional honesty that fearlessness and stupidity were often indistinguishable.

Kian stared. His archaeological mind tried to categorize what he was seeing — cultural practice, magical economy, the commodification of the human body in a society where magic made such commodification literal rather than metaphorical — and failed. This was not an artifact to be studied. This was a market where people sold pieces of themselves, or pieces of others, and the buyers browsed with the casual expertise of shoppers at a farmer’s market, squeezing a bicep here, testing the flexibility of a finger there.

“Who sells these?” he asked.

“The desperate. The dying. The enslaved.” Amir’s voice was flat. “A slave’s body is not their own. Their owner can sell it piece by piece — a hand today, an eye tomorrow, a heart when the rest is used up. It’s legal. It’s common. It’s—” He stopped. His jaw tightened. For a moment, the mask of the cheerful cabin boy slipped, and Kian saw something underneath — not anger, exactly, but a cold, focused fury that was far too old for a twelve-year-old’s face. “It’s how things are.”

“It’s monstrous.”

“Yes. And it’s how things are. Both statements are true. Welcome to Al-Khaliq.”

They left the Flesh Bazaar. Kian didn’t look back. But he filed it away — the hands, the eyes, the hearts on velvet — in the growing catalogue of horrors that this world presented as normalcy. He was beginning to understand something about Al-Khaliq that no amount of academic study could have taught him: that the line between civilization and atrocity was not a line at all but a gradient, and that people could live comfortably at any point along it, provided they’d been there long enough.

The Chain District was the last stop before Kian’s composure broke.

It was exactly what it sounded like: the slave market. Open-air, sprawling, occupying an entire barge that rocked gently with the sea’s motion, so that the chained men and women and children swayed in unison, a choreography of captivity. The auctioneers called out prices in the singsong cadence of men selling fish or fruit, and the buyers examined the merchandise with the same professional detachment — checking teeth, testing muscles, asking about provenance and health history and magical aptitude.

Kian stopped walking. He stood at the edge of the Chain District and watched a girl — maybe eight, maybe nine, with enormous dark eyes and a bruise on her cheek that was the exact shape of a man’s hand — being led to the auction block by a handler who held her chain the way you’d hold a dog’s leash: loosely, confidently, without thought.

“We need to go,” Amir said quietly.

“We need to—”

“We need to
go
, scholar.” Amir’s hand was on his arm, small and surprisingly strong. “You can’t help her. You can’t help any of them. You have no money, no power, no standing in this city. If you make a scene, the slavers will chain you next to her and sell you both. And the captain will not come for you. Not here. Not for this.”

Kian looked at the girl. The girl looked back. Her eyes were not pleading — they were assessing, the way Leila’s eyes assessed, the way Qadir’s eyes assessed. She was evaluating him. Deciding if he was a threat, an opportunity, or irrelevant. She decided he was irrelevant. She looked away.

He let Amir lead him from the Chain District. He did not speak for a long time.

Leila found them near the spice market. She was carrying supplies — rope, sailcloth, a crate of dried fish — and she was not alone. Behind her, moving with the careful precision of someone navigating unfamiliar territory, was a woman Kian hadn’t seen before. She was tall, thin, dark-skinned, with the bearing of someone who had once been important and the wariness of someone who had learned that importance was no protection.

“New recruit,” Leila said to Amir, jerking her head toward the woman. “Navigator. She passed Omar’s tests. Barely.” She looked at Kian. “You look like you’ve seen the Chain District.”

“I have.”

“Good. Now you know what we’re all running from.” She shifted the crate on her hip. “The captain wants everyone back on the ship by midday. We’re not staying long. Nazia has eyes in Iskandar.”

She walked on. The navigator followed. Kian watched them go and thought about the girl in the Chain District and the boy on the slave ship and the village he hadn’t yet betrayed, and he felt the weight of this world settling on his shoulders like a yoke.

The village of Qasim was not on any map.

It sat on a rocky island three days’ sail from Iskandar — a fishing village of maybe two hundred souls, built into the cliffs above a natural harbor. Stone houses with flat roofs, terraced gardens where vegetables grew in soil carried up from the beach in baskets, a small mosque with a minaret that leaned slightly to the east, as if listening to the sea. Goats. Chickens. Children playing in the dust. The smell of woodsmoke and baking bread and the particular, unmistakable scent of a community that had existed in the same place for generations: sweat and stone and continuity.

The Dhow anchored in the harbor at dusk. Qadir sent a shore party for supplies — fresh water, vegetables, goat meat if available. Kian was included, not because he was useful but because, as Omar explained, “the captain wants you to see how the world works outside the city. Cities are performances. Villages are truth.”

The villagers were wary but not hostile. They’d seen ships before — traders, fishermen, the occasional pirate — and they had a protocol: the headman met the shore party at the beach, terms were discussed, goods were exchanged, and the ship left. It was commerce stripped to its essentials, conducted with the formal courtesy of people who understood that politeness was cheaper than violence.

Kian wandered. He couldn’t help it — the archaeologist in him was cataloguing everything. The construction techniques (dry-stone walls, lime mortar, coral aggregate). The pottery (hand-thrown, unglazed, functional). The textiles (hand-loomed cotton, dyed with indigo and turmeric). The food (flatbread baked on an inverted clay pot, a stew of fish and lentils and dried limes that smelled extraordinary). He was, for a few hours, almost happy.

Except these weren’t remains. These were living people. And Kian, absorbed in his cataloguing, forgot that living people have living enemies.

The bandits arrived at sunset.

There were twenty of them, mounted on animals that Kian’s mind refused to fully process — something between a horse and a lizard, with scales and hooves and eyes that reflected light like a cat’s. They came from the interior of the island, from the rocky highlands where the village’s goats grazed, and they moved with the unhurried confidence of predators who knew their prey couldn’t run.

Their leader was a woman. She was small, wiry, sun-darkened, with a scar that bisected her face from forehead to chin, crossing her nose at an angle that suggested the blade had been aimed at her eye and she’d moved just enough. She wore armor of boiled leather and carried a lance decorated with what Kian hoped were animal teeth and suspected were not.

She rode directly to the Dhow’s shore party. Qadir met her. They spoke — quietly, in a dialect Kian could follow but only barely, the words clipped and coded with the shorthand of people who had negotiated before.

The bandit wanted the village. Not to destroy it — to tax it. She claimed the island as her territory and the village as her property, and she wanted the Dhow to confirm this claim by leaving without trading. If the Dhow left, the village would pay the bandit’s tax — half their catch, half their harvest, half their children’s futures — and survive. If the Dhow stayed, the bandit would burn the village and blame the pirates.

Qadir’s response was immediate and pragmatic: he didn’t care about the village. He cared about supplies. If the bandit let him trade, he’d leave. The village’s fate was the village’s problem.

The bandit countered: the Dhow’s presence undermined her authority. If the villagers saw that ships could trade freely, they’d resist the tax. She needed the Dhow gone, and she needed the villagers to see it leave on her terms.

Qadir considered. He looked at the village — the stone houses, the terraced gardens, the children who had stopped playing and were watching from doorways with the wide eyes of children who understood that adults were deciding their future. He looked at his crew. He looked at Kian.

“Scholar,” he said. “You walked the village. What did you see?”

Kian blinked. “What?”

“The layout. The defenses. The approaches. You’re an archaeologist — you read places. Read this one.”

And Kian, without thinking — without understanding what he was doing, what he was enabling, what the information would be used for — read the village aloud.

“The walls are dry-stone, no mortar on the eastern side — that section was rebuilt recently, probably after a storm. The gate faces south, toward the harbor, but there’s a goat path on the north cliff that bypasses it entirely. The mosque is the strongest structure — thick walls, small windows, could serve as a refuge. The water cistern is on the western terrace, exposed. If you cut the water supply, the village couldn’t hold out for more than two days. The granary is—”

He stopped. Qadir was looking at him. The bandit was looking at him. Everyone was looking at him, and in the silence, Kian heard what he’d just done.

He’d given a tactical assessment of the village’s vulnerabilities. To a pirate captain. In front of a bandit who wanted to subjugate it.

“The granary,” the bandit said, smiling with the scar-split face. “Go on.”

“I—” Kian’s mouth was dry. “I didn’t mean—”

“The granary is next to the mosque,” Qadir said, finishing for him. His voice was neutral. His eyes were not. “Thank you, scholar. That’s useful.”

The negotiation concluded quickly after that. Qadir traded the village’s tactical information for safe passage and a supply of fresh water. The bandit got what she needed — a detailed map of the village’s weaknesses, provided by an outsider with an archaeologist’s eye for structural vulnerability. The Dhow left with the tide.

The village of Qasim burned that night.

Kian heard the screams.

The Dhow was a mile offshore, running before a fair wind, and the sound carried across the water with the clarity that the Sea of Tears gave to all things — not just sound but
memory
, so that Kian didn’t just hear the screams but
experienced
them.

He stood at the stern rail and watched the glow on the horizon — orange and red and the sickly yellow of burning thatch — and he understood what he had done.

Not directly. He hadn’t lit the fires. He hadn’t swung a sword. He hadn’t even intended to help the bandit — he’d been answering a question, reading a place the way he’d read a thousand places, the way his training had taught him, automatically, without thought. But the information he’d provided — the weak wall, the goat path, the exposed cistern — was a weapon, and he’d handed it to someone who used it.

Intent was irrelevant.

Omar found him at the rail.

The quartermaster didn’t speak for a long time. He stood beside Kian and watched the distant fire and ate dates — Ajwa dates, small and dark, the kind the child in Kian’s sea-crossing vision had been eating — and said nothing. The silence was not comfortable. It was not meant to be.

Finally: “You’re thinking about the village.”

“I’m thinking about what I did.”

“You answered a question. The captain asked, and you answered. This is what scholars do.”

“I gave them the information they needed to—”

“Yes. You did.” Omar ate another date. “And the captain used it. And the bandit used it. And the village burned. And you are standing here, alive, on a ship with a full water supply, feeling terrible about it. This is the correct response. Feeling terrible means you are still human. The day you stop feeling terrible is the day you become the captain.”

“That’s not comforting.”

“It’s not meant to be. Comfort is for people who haven’t done anything wrong. You have done something wrong. You will do more wrong things. This is the sea. Every wave is a choice someone made. Yours just happened to drown a village.” He paused. “The captain didn’t need your information, you know. He already knew the village’s layout. He’s been here before. He asked you because he wanted you to understand.”

“Understand what?”

“That knowledge is never neutral. That information is a weapon. That the scholar who reads a place and the soldier who burns it are separated by nothing more than a decision, and decisions in this world are made by people with power, and you, scholar, have no power.” Omar turned to face him. The jovial mask was gone. Underneath was something harder, older, sadder. “The captain is teaching you. Every lesson costs something. This one cost a village. The next one will cost more. By the end, you’ll have paid for an education that no university in your world or mine could provide, and you’ll wish you’d stayed ignorant.”

He left. Kian stayed at the rail.

The fire on the horizon dimmed. The screams faded. The Sea of Tears absorbed them — the screams, the fire, the village, the lives — and added them to its vast, indifferent archive of human experience. Somewhere in the water beneath the Dhow, the memory of Qasim was settling into the current, joining the soldier on the wall and the woman braiding hair and the child eating dates, becoming part of the sea’s endless, terrible, beautiful collection.

Kian gripped the rail and stared at the dark water and felt the guilt settle into him like ballast — heavy, permanent, load-bearing.

In the bilge, Zulfaqar stirred.

I could have prevented it, you know. A word from me to the wind, and the bandit’s mounts would have bolted. A whisper to the water, and the fires would have drowned before they spread. The price would have been small. A memory. Something you wouldn’t miss. The taste of your morning coffee. The feeling of sunlight on your face in a park you used to visit. Something that matters less than two hundred lives.

Kian’s hands were shaking. The Marid was right. One memory against two hundred lives. Any rational person would make that trade. The fact that Kian hadn’t — that he’d stood on the deck of a pirate ship and watched a village burn rather than pay the price of a single memory — said something about him that he didn’t want to examine.

You’re not a bad man, scholar. You’re a frightened one. And frightened men make the worst decisions — not because they choose wrong, but because they choose nothing. Inaction is a choice. Silence is a choice. You chose silence on the slave ship, and the boy died. You chose silence at Qasim, and the village burned. How many more silences before you understand that the price of saying nothing is always higher than the price of saying yes?

The Marid’s presence receded. The ship sailed on.

Kian stood at the rail until dawn, carrying his new guilt alongside the old, and did not break. Not yet. It would stay. He knew this with the certainty of a man who has discovered something true about himself and wishes he hadn’t. The guilt would stay, and it would grow, and it would become part of him the way the map had become part of his arm — not chosen, not wanted, but indelible.

Amir brought him lentil soup.

It was the middle of the night — or what passed for night under the broken sky of Al-Khaliq, when the silver moon set and the amber one hung alone, casting everything in a warm, wounded light. Kian was still at the rail. He hadn’t moved. His legs were numb. His eyes were dry from staring at the sea.

The cabin boy appeared with a wooden bowl and a spoon and sat down on the deck beside him, cross-legged, as if this were the most natural thing in the world — sitting on the deck of a pirate ship at three in the morning with a man who had helped burn a village.

“Eat,” Amir said.

“I’m not hungry.”

“I didn’t ask if you were hungry. I said eat.” He held up the bowl. The soup was thick, brown, fragrant with cumin and garlic and dried limes. Steam rose from it in lazy spirals. “Umm Hani made it. She’s the cook. You haven’t met her yet — she stays in the galley mostly. She says the deck is for fools and the galley is for people with sense. She’s not wrong.”

Kian looked at the soup. His stomach, which had been a clenched fist for hours, unclenched slightly. The smell was — it was
real
. In a world of liquid memory and bleeding maps and burning villages, the smell of lentil soup was the most real thing he’d encountered. It smelled like food. Just food. No magic, no metaphor, no hidden cost. Just lentils and water and spices and the labor of someone who had stood over a pot and stirred.

He couldn’t eat. The guilt was a stone in his throat. He thought of the village — the bread baking, the fish stew, the children in the dust — and the thought of eating while they burned was obscene.

“You’re thinking that you don’t deserve to eat,” Amir said. He said it without judgment, without sympathy, with the matter-of-fact clarity of someone stating an observable fact. “You’re thinking that the village is burning and you helped and eating soup while people die is wrong.”

“Yes.”

“It is wrong. And you should eat anyway. Because starving yourself doesn’t unburn the village. It doesn’t bring back the dead. It doesn’t change what happened. It just makes you weaker, and weak people can’t fix anything.” He pushed the bowl closer. “Eat. Be guilty and eat. The two are not mutually exclusive.”

Kian took the bowl. The wood was warm. The soup was warm. He raised it to his lips and drank, and the taste — earthy, savory, the slight sourness of dried lime cutting through the richness of the lentils — was so simple and so good that his eyes stung.

He drank the soup. He didn’t taste redemption. He didn’t taste forgiveness. He tasted lentils and cumin and garlic and salt, and that was enough, and it wasn’t enough, and both of those things were true.

Amir sat beside him in silence. The cabin boy didn’t speak. Didn’t offer wisdom or comfort or platitudes. Just sat, small and quiet and present, on the deck of a ship that sailed a sea of tears, and kept a guilty man company while he ate.

It was the kindest thing anyone had done for Kian in this world.

He finished the soup. He set the bowl down. He looked at Amir and said, “Thank you.”

Amir nodded. He picked up the bowl and stood. “The captain says we sail for the open sea tomorrow. There’s a pearl — a tooth — somewhere to the south. He can feel it.” He paused at the hatch. “Scholar?”

“Yes?”

“The village wasn’t your fault. Not entirely. The captain used you. The bandit used you. You were a tool, and tools don’t choose how they’re used.” He looked at Kian with a steadiness that didn’t belong to a child. “But you’re not a tool anymore. You’re crew. And crew choose. Remember that.”

He disappeared below. Kian sat on the deck with an empty bowl and a full conscience and watched the amber moon trail its broken debris across the sky, and thought about choices, and tools, and the difference between them, and whether a man who had been used as a weapon could ever be anything else.

The sea glowed beneath him. The ship hummed. In the bilge, something ancient and hungry waited with the patience of water wearing stone.

And in the captain’s chest, locked in silk and shadow, a single black tooth sang its quiet, terrible song — a lullaby for the end of the world, patient and sweet and inevitable as the tide.
