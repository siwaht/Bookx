Chapter 2: The Voice in the Bilge

“The Djinn do not grant wishes. They grant contracts. Read the fine print, or become the fine print.”

The storm lasted three days.

Kian spent most of it vomiting. The Sea of Tears had gotten inside him during the crossing and was working its way out in bursts of borrowed memory. He’d be gripping the rail, retching bile and saltwater, and suddenly he’d be a seventy-year-old woman dying in a bed of silk, surrounded by grandchildren whose names he knew and faces he’d forget within the hour. He’d be on his hands and knees in the scuppers, and he’d be a young man kissing another young man in a garden of jasmine, terrified and elated, the taste of someone else’s first love on his tongue alongside the taste of his own stomach acid.

The crew ignored him. They had work to do — the Dhow demanded constant attention in heavy weather, and the storm was not natural. It moved with intention, circling back when the ship tried to outrun it, pressing from the west with the patience of a siege. The crew fought it with a competence that Kian, even in his misery, recognized as extraordinary. They moved through the rigging like spiders, barefoot on wet wood, adjusting sails that seemed to have minds of their own. They bailed water that fought back, that tried to climb the buckets and return to the sea. They sang — work songs in the same not-quite-Arabic that Kian now understood, songs about a woman who loved a fish and a fish who loved the moon and the moon who loved no one because the moon was wise.

On the second day, the fat man brought him bread.

“Eat,” Omar said, squatting beside Kian’s huddled form in the lee of the mainmast. He was enormous — built like a barrel that had been taught to walk and given a personality. His eyes were small and bright and missed nothing. “You look like something the sea rejected, and the sea accepts everything.”

“I can’t eat.” Kian’s stomach convulsed at the sight of the flatbread. “I’ll—”

“You’ll vomit. Yes. And then you’ll eat again. And vomit again. And eat again. This is the process. The sea is inside you. It needs to come out. But if you don’t eat, you’ll have nothing to vomit, and dry heaving is worse. Trust me. I am an expert in all matters of the stomach.” He patted his belly, which was considerable. “I have studied extensively.”

Kian took the bread. It was dense, slightly sour, baked with a grain he didn’t recognize. He bit into it and tasted — just bread. No memories. No visions. Just flour and water and salt, the most ordinary thing in the world, and the ordinariness of it made his eyes sting.

“Good,” Omar said. “Now. You are the scholar. The one who talks to Zulfaqar.”

“Word travels fast.”

“Word travels at the speed of gossip, which is faster than light and less reliable. I am Omar ibn Khalid ibn Omar ibn — well, the lineage goes on, but the short version is Omar. Quartermaster. I manage the ship’s supplies, finances, and morale. The first two are difficult. The third is impossible.” He smiled. It was a good smile — warm, genuine, the kind of smile that made you want to smile back. It was also, Kian sensed, a weapon. “The captain says you refused the Marid’s offer.”

“I did.”

“Interesting. Most people who hear Zulfaqar for the first time either scream, go mad, or say yes. You said no. This makes you either very intelligent or very new. Since you are clearly very new, I will assume intelligence until proven otherwise.” He leaned closer. The dried fish in his beard trembled. “A word of advice, scholar. Free of charge, which is unusual for me — I charge for everything, including advice, especially advice. The Marid will ask again. And again. And again. It is patient in the way that water is patient. It does not need you to say yes today. It needs you to say yes eventually. And eventually always comes.”

“What if I keep saying no?”

Omar’s smile didn’t waver, but something behind it shifted — a shadow, there and gone. “Then you die. Probably soon. Probably badly. This world is not kind to the powerless, and without Sihr, you are powerless. The captain keeps you alive because you interest him. Interest fades. Usefulness does not.” He stood, knees cracking. “Eat your bread. Vomit. Eat again. And think about what you’re willing to pay to stay alive. Everything has a price, scholar. The only question is whether you choose the price or the price chooses you.”

He walked away, steady on the pitching deck, humming one of the work songs. Kian ate the bread. He vomited. He ate again.

Omar was right. It helped.

On the third day, the storm broke, and Kian got his first clear look at the world he’d fallen into.

The Sea of Tears stretched to every horizon — not blue, not green, but a color that had no name in any language Kian spoke, a shifting iridescence that was all colors and none, like oil on water or the surface of a soap bubble. It moved wrong. Ocean waves follow patterns — wind-driven surface waves, deep swells from distant storms, tidal movements governed by lunar gravity. The Sea of Tears followed none of these rules. Its waves moved in multiple directions simultaneously, crossing and recrossing without interference, as if driven by forces that had nothing to do with wind or moon. Some waves moved
upward
, rising in columns that held their shape for minutes before collapsing. Others spiraled, creating whirlpools that appeared and vanished without cause.

And the sea
glowed
. Not bioluminescence — Kian knew what that looked like. This was different. The light came from within the water itself, pulsing in patterns that suggested meaning, like a language written in luminescence. When Kian stared at it long enough, he began to see shapes in the glow — faces, hands, buildings, landscapes — the memories that composed the sea, flickering beneath the surface like fish beneath ice.

The sky was vast and wrong. The two moons — the whole one silver, the broken one amber — hung in positions that shifted throughout the day in ways that defied orbital mechanics. The sun was too large and too orange, and it set in the north. The stars, when they appeared, were dense enough to cast shadows.

The Black Dhow itself was a revelation.

In daylight, Kian could see what the storm had hidden. The ship was old — not decades old, but
centuries
old, its timbers dark with age and saturated with salt and something else, something that made the wood warm to the touch and faintly vibrant, as if the ship were not built but grown. The hull was black, but not uniformly — in places, the wood was streaked with veins of deep red, like blood vessels in flesh. The figurehead was a woman with her mouth open in a scream or a song, carved from a wood so dark it absorbed light, and her eyes were set with stones that might have been rubies and might have been something worse.

The sails were patchwork — dozens of fabrics stitched together over what must have been generations. Silk and canvas and something that looked like skin and something that looked like shadow given material form. They moved in winds that didn’t exist, adjusting themselves without human hands, and when the ship changed course, the sails anticipated the turn.

The ship was alive. Kian was certain of this by the end of the first day. Not alive the way a tree is alive — alive the way a dog is alive, with awareness and preference and something that might have been personality. The Dhow
liked
certain crew members and
disliked
others. It creaked contentedly when Omar sang. It groaned when anyone leaned too hard on the port rail near the stern. It purred — actually purred, a vibration Kian could feel through his bare feet — when the wind was right and the sails were full and the sea was calm.

“She’s old,” Amir said, appearing at Kian’s elbow with the silent suddenness that Kian was learning was the cabin boy’s defining characteristic. “Older than anyone knows. The captain says she was built before the Caliphate. Before the Sunken Kings. Before the sea was tears.” The boy — small, quick, dark-eyed, with a face that was all angles and attention — ran a hand along the rail with unmistakable affection. “She chooses her crew. Not the other way around.”

“Ships don’t choose.”

Amir looked at him with an expression that was too old for his face — patient, amused, slightly pitying. “In your world, maybe. Here, everything chooses. The sea chooses who drowns. The wind chooses who sails. The ship chooses who lives on her deck.” He paused. “She chose you. I don’t know why. You can’t sail, you can’t fight, and you vomit constantly. But she wants you here. I can feel it.”

“How?”

“She told me.” Amir said this with complete sincerity, as if ships speaking to cabin boys were as unremarkable as the tide. “She tells me things. Not in words. In… feelings. Warmth when she’s happy. Cold when she’s angry. A kind of humming when she’s curious.” He looked at Kian. “She’s curious about you. That’s good. Curious is better than hungry.”

“The ship gets hungry?”

“Everything gets hungry, scholar. Even ships. Even seas. Even gods.” He handed Kian a cup of water — fresh water, not the bilge-tainted slurry from the slave ship. “Drink. The captain wants to see you.”

Captain Qadir’s cabin was at the stern, behind a door of iron-banded oak that opened only for him and, apparently, for Amir. It was smaller than Kian expected — a bunk, a desk, a chest, a lantern. The walls were covered in maps. Not the bleeding, skin-drawn impossibility on Kian’s arm, but proper nautical charts — hand-drawn on parchment, annotated in multiple scripts, showing coastlines and currents and depths and dangers. Some were old enough to be crumbling. Some were fresh, the ink still sharp. All of them showed the same sea: the Sea of Tears, in all its impossible, iridescent, memory-laden vastness.

Qadir sat behind the desk. In the lamplight, he looked older than he had in the storm — the lines deeper, the scars more prominent, the missing ear more conspicuous. He was drinking tea from a small glass — dark tea, fragrant with cardamom, the steam curling in the lamplight like a question mark.

“Sit,” he said. There was one other chair. Kian sat.

Qadir studied him. The flat black eyes moved over Kian’s face, his hands, his arms — lingering on the map tattoo — with the thoroughness of a man reading a document. Whatever he read, he didn’t share.

“You are from the other side,” Qadir said. Not a question.

“Yes.”

“You are a scholar of dead things.”

“I’m an archaeologist. I study—”

“Dead things. Ships that sank. Cities that fell. People who lived and died and left their garbage for you to catalogue. Yes. I know what you are.” He sipped his tea. “The sea brings your kind sometimes. Scholars, scientists, men of reason. It finds you amusing. You arrive with your logic and your categories and your desperate need to
understand
, and the sea watches you try to fit this world into your little boxes, and it laughs.”

“I’m not trying to fit anything into boxes. I’m trying to survive.”

“Good. Survival is the only box that matters.” Qadir set down his tea. “I will tell you a story, scholar. Not because I enjoy stories — I don’t; stories are lies with better lighting — but because you need to understand what you’ve fallen into, and I don’t have time to let you figure it out yourself.”

He reached into the chest beside his desk and withdrew something wrapped in black silk. He unwrapped it with the care of a man handling a live coal. Inside was a sphere — small, no larger than a marble, perfectly round, perfectly black. It absorbed the lamplight. It seemed to absorb the air around it, creating a tiny zone of cold and silence.

“Touch it,” Qadir said.

Kian reached out. His fingers brushed the sphere’s surface.

The world vanished.

He was standing in a mouth. A mouth the size of a cathedral, with teeth like black pillars rising from gums of raw red flesh, and a tongue that was a landscape of taste buds the size of boulders, and a throat that descended into a darkness so absolute it made the slave ship’s hold look like noon. The mouth was alive. It was breathing. Each breath was a hurricane of hot, wet air that smelled of everything that had ever rotted — meat and fruit and hope and civilizations. And the mouth was laughing.

The laugh was the worst part. It was not a human laugh. It was not even an animal laugh. It was the laugh of something that existed before the concepts of human and animal, something that had watched the universe assemble itself from nothing and found the whole process hilarious because it knew — it
knew
— that everything the universe built, it would eventually eat.

The vision lasted less than a second. Kian snatched his hand back and nearly fell out of his chair. His heart was hammering. His mouth was dry. The taste of rot lingered on his tongue.

“That,” Qadir said calmly, “is Ahriman.”

Kian stared at the black sphere. It sat on the silk, inert, innocent, a marble. A toy. A thing you might give a child.

“Ahriman,” Qadir continued, “is a god. Not a god in the way your world uses the word — not a metaphor, not a cultural construct, not a psychological projection. A god. A being of such power that its existence warps reality the way a heavy stone warps a sheet of silk. Ahriman is the god of consumption. Of hunger. Of the void that exists at the center of all things, the emptiness that matter and energy and life are merely temporary interruptions of.”

He wrapped the sphere in silk again, handling it with the same careful distaste.

“Ahriman tried to eat the world. Not this world —
the
world. All worlds. All realities. Everything that exists, existed, or might exist. It opened its mouth and began to swallow, and the only thing that stopped it was Tiamat.”

“The sea goddess.”

Qadir’s eyebrow rose. “You know the name.”

“Babylonian mythology. Tiamat was the primordial goddess of the salt sea. Marduk killed her and made the world from her body.”

“Your world’s version. A children’s story. The truth is worse.” Qadir leaned back. “Tiamat did not die. She
became
. She became the sea — this sea, the Sea of Tears — and in becoming, she imprisoned Ahriman within herself. Her body is his cage. Her death is his chains. The sea exists because a goddess chose to become a prison.”

“And the sphere?”

“A tooth. Ahriman’s tooth. When Tiamat imprisoned him, his teeth were scattered — thirty of them, flung across the world like seeds. Each one contains a fragment of his consciousness. His hunger. His will.” Qadir’s voice was flat, recitative, the voice of a man who had told this story many times and no longer felt it. “Gather all thirty, and the jaw reassembles. The mouth opens. Ahriman wakes. And everything — every world, every reality, every memory in this sea — is consumed.”

The cabin was very quiet. The ship creaked. The lantern flickered.

“So why,” Kian said slowly, “are you collecting them?”

Qadir smiled. It was not Omar’s warm, weaponized smile. It was the smile of a man who has been asked the right question and is deciding how much of the answer to share.

“Because if I don’t, someone worse will.”

He told Kian about Nazia.

Not everything — Qadir was not a man who shared everything, or even most things. But enough. Nazia the Sea Witch, four hundred years old, three stolen hearts, seven shed skins. A daughter of Tiamat’s coven who had decided that the goddess’s sacrifice was a waste. Tiamat had imprisoned Ahriman to save the world. Nazia wanted to
negotiate
with him — to offer him a partial awakening, a controlled consumption, in exchange for power. She believed she could bargain with a god of hunger. She believed she could feed it enough to satisfy it without feeding it everything.

“She’s wrong,” Qadir said. “You cannot negotiate with hunger. You cannot offer a fire ‘just a little’ fuel. Ahriman will consume everything or nothing. There is no middle ground. Nazia is brilliant and ancient and she is wrong, and her wrongness will end the world.”

“So you’re collecting the teeth to keep them from her.”

“I am collecting the teeth to keep them from everyone. Including myself.” He looked at the silk-wrapped sphere. “One tooth, and I hear it singing. I’ve had it for eleven years. Eleven years of whispers. Eleven years of dreams where I am the mouth and the world is the meal. One tooth.” He met Kian’s eyes. “Imagine thirty.”

“Then destroy them.”

“They cannot be destroyed. They are fragments of a god. You might as well try to destroy gravity, or time, or the concept of hunger itself. They can only be gathered and guarded. Or gathered and used.” He paused. “There is a third option. But it requires a sacrifice I am not willing to make. Not yet.”

He didn’t elaborate. Kian didn’t press. He was learning — slowly, painfully — that in this world, the questions you didn’t ask were as important as the ones you did.

“Why me?” Kian asked instead. “Why did the sea bring me here?”

“I don’t know. The sea has its reasons. It brought me a scholar who can read dead languages and hear Marid. Perhaps it thinks I need a translator.” The ghost of a smile. “Perhaps it thinks I need a conscience. Mine wore out years ago.”

“And if I refuse to help? If I just want to go home?”

Qadir’s expression didn’t change, but the temperature in the cabin seemed to drop. “There is no home for you, scholar. The crossing is one way. The map on your arm is not a return ticket — it is a leash. The sea brought you here because it wants you here, and the sea does not release what it claims.” He stood. “You can join my crew and hunt the teeth. You can be useful, and alive, and part of something that matters. Or I can sell you at Iskandar, and you can spend whatever remains of your life as a flesh-scribe’s property, writing spells on rice paper until your mind breaks.”

“That’s not much of a choice.”

“No. It isn’t. Welcome to Al-Khaliq.”

He opened the cabin door. The night air rushed in — salt and cardamom and the faint, sweet rot of the sea. On the deck, the crew moved in the lantern light, coiling ropes, mending sails, doing the thousand small tasks that kept a ship alive. Omar’s laugh boomed from somewhere near the bow. Amir’s small shadow darted between the masts.

Kian stood in the doorway and looked at the sea. It glowed beneath the broken moon, iridescent and vast and full of other people’s lives, and somewhere in its depths, thirty black teeth waited to become a jaw.

“I’ll need to know everything,” Kian said. “The teeth. Their locations. The magic — the Sihr. How it works. What it costs. Everything.”

“You’ll learn. Some of it I’ll teach you. Some of it the sea will teach you. Some of it Zulfaqar will teach you, and those lessons will cost the most.” Qadir paused in the doorway. “One more thing, scholar. The Marid in the bilge — it will offer you power. It will offer you knowledge. It will offer you everything you’ve ever wanted, and the price will seem small, and the price will grow, and by the time you realize what you’ve paid, you will have nothing left to buy it back.” He looked at Kian with something that might have been compassion, or might have been the memory of compassion, worn smooth by years. “Say yes carefully. Say yes slowly. And never, ever say yes when you’re afraid. Fear is the Marid’s currency. It will spend you like a coin.”

He left. Kian stood alone in the captain’s cabin, surrounded by maps of a world he didn’t understand, and listened to the ship breathe, and felt the map on his arm pulse in time with the sea, and knew — with the certainty of a man who has run out of denial — that his old life was over.

Not ended. Over. Kian Farahani, PhD, maritime archaeologist, divorced, childless, career-stalled — that man was over. What came next was unknown.

He was terrified. He was also, and this surprised him, curious.

Curiosity. The thing that had made him touch the map. The thing that Sara had called his “replacement for human connection.” The thing that had brought him to this world and would, if he wasn’t careful, kill him in it.

But also: the thing that might save him. In a world where knowledge was power and power was survival, curiosity was the closest thing Kian had to a weapon.

He went to find Omar. He needed to learn how to sail.

Omar taught him nothing about sailing.

“Sailing is for sailors,” the quartermaster said, waving a hand dismissively. They were on the foredeck, Omar perched on a coil of rope like a sultan on a throne, eating pistachios and flicking the shells overboard with practiced accuracy. “You are not a sailor. You are a scholar. Scholars do not sail. Scholars
think
, and then they tell sailors where to sail, and then the sailors ignore them and sail wherever they want. This is the natural order.”

“Then what am I supposed to do on this ship?”

“Survive. Think. Translate. The captain has maps he can’t read, texts he can’t decipher, artifacts he can’t identify. You are his library. His reference book. His—” Omar searched for the word. “His
tool
. A useful tool is kept sharp. A useless tool is thrown overboard. Be useful, scholar.”

“My name is Kian.”

“I know your name. I choose not to use it until you’ve earned it. On this ship, names are earned, not given. Until then, you are ‘scholar.’ Be grateful — the last new arrival was called ‘vomit’ for three months.” He cracked a pistachio with his teeth. “Now. You want to know about Sihr.”

“Yes.”

“Then listen, because I will explain this once, and I will explain it simply, because I am a simple man—” He paused, waiting for Kian to contradict him. Kian didn’t. “Good. You’re learning. Sihr is magic. But not the magic of your world — your world has no magic, only machines, which are magic for people too frightened to pay the price. Sihr is real. Sihr has rules. And Sihr has costs.”

He held up three thick fingers.

“Rule one: magic requires a vessel. You cannot cast a spell. You are not magical. No human is magical. Humans are meat and memory and stubbornness, and none of these things can bend reality. To perform Sihr, you must convince a spirit — a Marid, an Ifrit, a Ghul, a Peri, any of the thousand beings that exist between the mortal and the divine — to enter your body and act through you. You are the vessel. The spirit is the wine. Without the vessel, the wine spills. Without the wine, the vessel is empty.”

Second finger. “Rule two: the vessel always takes payment. The spirit does not work for free. It cannot — this is not greed, it is physics. The spirit exists in a state of hunger, and to act in the mortal world costs it energy, and that energy must come from somewhere. It comes from you. From your memories, your body, your identity. Every spell costs a piece of what makes you
you
. Small spells, small prices — the color of a sunset you once loved, the sound of a song you used to sing. Large spells, large prices — a hand, an eye, the face of your mother, the name of your child.”

Third finger. “Rule three: magic corrupts the goal. This is the cruelest rule, and the one that kills the most people. The spirit does what you ask, but it does it in the spirit’s way, not yours. You ask to be healed — the spirit heals you by consuming the life force of everyone nearby. You ask for strength — the spirit gives you the strength of a beast and the mind of one too. You ask for knowledge — the spirit shows you everything, and the everything breaks your mind like a wave breaks a sandcastle. The spirit is not malicious. It simply does not think like you. It is a force of nature wearing a mask of intelligence, and when you invite it inside you, you are inviting the storm into your house and asking it to light a candle.”

Omar ate another pistachio. “Questions?”

Kian had a hundred. He asked the most important one. “What happens when you run out of memories to pay with?”

Omar’s chewing slowed. He looked at Kian with an expression that was, for the first time, entirely without performance — no smile, no warmth, no calculated charm. Just a fat man on a coil of rope, looking at a thin man on a strange ship, and telling the truth.

“You become the spirit’s memory instead. It wears you like a coat. Walks in your body. Speaks with your mouth. You are still in there — somewhere, screaming, aware — but you are no longer the one driving. You are the passenger. And the passenger never gets off.”

He smiled again. The warmth returned. The performance resumed. But Kian had seen what was underneath, and he would not forget it.

“More pistachios?” Omar offered.

That night, Zulfaqar came again.

Kian was in the crew’s quarters — a long, low space below the main deck, lined with hammocks that swung with the ship’s motion. He’d been given a hammock near the stern, away from the experienced sailors, in the section Omar called “the nursery” with a grin that suggested the name was not affectionate. The other hammocks in the nursery were occupied by the survivors pulled from the slave ship — those the crew had deemed useful enough to keep. A blacksmith. A carpenter. A woman who claimed to be a navigator and who Omar was testing with increasingly obscure questions about star positions.

Kian couldn’t sleep. The hammock was uncomfortable, the ship’s noises were alien, and every time he closed his eyes, he saw the boy from the slave ship — the unnamed boy, the dead boy, the boy who had asked for a story and received silence.

The voice came not from the bilge this time but from everywhere — from the wood of the hull, from the water outside, from the air itself. As if Zulfaqar had expanded to fill the ship, or as if the ship and the Marid were, in some fundamental way, the same thing.

You spoke with the captain.

“Yes.”

He told you about the teeth. About Ahriman. About the witch.

“Yes.”

He told you the truth. Most of it. He is an honest man, for a pirate. He left out the part where he is dying.

Kian sat up in his hammock. “What?”

The tooth he carries — the one he showed you — is killing him. Slowly. The way a river kills a stone. Not by force but by persistence. He has carried it for eleven years. He has perhaps two more. The tooth consumes the carrier. This is its nature. It is a fragment of a god of hunger. It is always eating.

“Why are you telling me this?”

Because you are a scholar, and scholars need information the way fish need water. Because the captain will not tell you himself — he is proud, and pride is the last luxury of the dying. And because—
A pause. The hunger pulsed, closer now, more intimate, like a hand on the back of his neck.
Because when he dies, someone will need to carry the tooth. And the teeth that come after. And I want that someone to be you.

“Why?”

Because you are interesting. Because you refused me, which no one does. Because you carry guilt like a stone in your chest — the boy, the village, the silence — and guilt makes the best fuel. The guilty work harder. They sacrifice more. They are willing to pay prices that the innocent would never accept, because they believe they deserve to pay.

The words landed like blows. Not because they were cruel — because they were accurate. Kian thought of the boy. Of Qasim. Of the silence that was becoming the loudest thing about him.

“I’m not making a deal with you.”

Not tonight. But the captain is dying, scholar. The witch is hunting. The teeth are scattered across a world that wants to kill you. And you are a man with no magic, no weapons, no skills, and no hands that can hold a sword.
A beat.
Well. You have hands now. Enjoy them while they last.

“What does that mean?”

But the presence was already receding, sinking back into the bilge, into the water, into whatever space between spaces a Marid occupied when it wasn’t whispering to scholars in the dark. The last thing Kian felt was amusement — that same cat-watching-mouse amusement — and then silence.

He lay in his hammock and stared at the ceiling and thought about what Omar had said.
Everything has a price. The only question is whether you choose the price or the price chooses you.

He thought about what Qadir had said.
Say yes carefully. Say yes slowly. And never say yes when you’re afraid.

He thought about the boy. The unnamed, dead, forgotten boy who had asked for a story and gotten nothing.

And he thought:
If I had said yes to Zulfaqar on the slave ship — if I had paid the price, whatever it was — could I have saved him?

The answer, he knew, was yes. The Marid could have broken the chains, killed the slavers, healed the boy. The price would have been a memory — something small, something he wouldn’t miss. The color of his mother’s kitchen. The sound of rain on a window.

He had said no. The boy had died. The moral calculus was simple and devastating: Kian had valued his memories more than a child’s life.

This was not true. This was not fair. He hadn’t known the boy would die. He hadn’t understood the stakes. He had been afraid, and confused, and rational, and all of these were reasonable responses to an unreasonable situation.

But the boy was still dead. And Kian’s memories were still intact. And the equation, however unfair, balanced.

He lay in the dark and listened to the ship breathe and the sea whisper and the Marid wait, and he understood — not intellectually, but in his bones, in the place where guilt lives and reason cannot reach — that he would eventually say yes.

Not tonight. Not tomorrow. But eventually.

Omar was right. Eventually always comes.

The next morning, Leila broke his nose.

It was not personal. It was, she explained afterward — or rather, did not explain, because Leila did not explain things; Omar explained things while Leila stood nearby looking satisfied — a test. The first mate tested all new crew members. The test was simple: she hit you, and she observed how you responded. Your response told her everything she needed to know about your character.

Kian’s response was to fall down, bleed profusely, and say a word in English that needed no translation.

“Interesting,” Leila said. She was tall — taller than Kian, taller than most of the crew — and built like a weapon that had been given legs. Her skin was dark brown, her hair cropped close to her skull, her face a collection of angles that might have been beautiful if beauty had been something she valued, which it clearly wasn’t. She wore a sleeveless tunic that showed arms roped with muscle and mapped with scars — not the decorative scars of ritual, but the working scars of someone who had been cut many times and had cut back more. Her eyes were the color of teak, and they assessed Kian the way a butcher assesses a carcass: professionally, without sentiment.

“He didn’t fight back,” she said to Omar.

“He’s a scholar,” Omar said, handing Kian a rag for his nose. “Scholars don’t fight back. They write strongly worded letters.”

“He didn’t run, either.”

“Where would he run? We’re on a ship.”

“Some of them jump overboard.” Leila looked at Kian, who was sitting on the deck with blood running through his fingers and stars in his vision. “You didn’t jump.”

“I considered it,” Kian said through the rag.

Something that might have been amusement flickered across Leila’s face. It was gone so quickly Kian wasn’t sure he’d seen it. “He’ll do,” she said to Omar. “He won’t fight, but he won’t break. That’s enough.”

She walked away. Her gait was precise, economical, the walk of someone who had learned to move through spaces where any wasted motion could get you killed.

“Don’t take it personally,” Omar said, helping him up. “She broke my nose when I came aboard too. And the captain’s. And Amir’s, though Amir’s nose is so small I’m not sure it counts.” He examined Kian’s face with the critical eye of a man who had seen many broken noses. “It’s not bad. Fatima will set it.”

“Who’s Fatima?”

“The ship’s surgeon. She’s ashore at the moment — we’re approaching Iskandar, and she went ahead to buy supplies. You’ll meet her soon.” Omar grinned. “She’s gentler than Leila. Most earthquakes are gentler than Leila.”

Kian spent the rest of the day learning the ship.

Not sailing — Omar was right that sailing was for sailors, and Kian’s attempts to help with the rigging were met with polite horror by the crew. Instead, he learned the ship the way he learned archaeological sites: by mapping, cataloguing, and asking questions that annoyed everyone.

The Black Dhow was larger than it appeared from outside — a trick of its construction, or its magic, or both. Below the main deck were three levels: the crew quarters, the cargo hold, and the bilge. The cargo hold was divided into sections — weapons (swords, bows, and sealed clay jars that Omar said contained Ifrit and should not, under any circumstances, be opened indoors), supplies (food, water, rope, sailcloth, medicine), and a locked section at the stern that Omar called “the treasury” and refused to discuss.

The bilge was where Zulfaqar lived. Kian went down once, drawn by curiosity. The bilge was knee-deep in water that was not entirely water — it was Sea of Tears, liquid memory, and it glowed faintly in the dark. The glow was stronger here than anywhere else on the ship, pulsing in rhythms that matched Kian’s heartbeat, and the water was warm, and it whispered, and when Kian stood in it, he felt the Marid’s presence like a hand pressed against his chest from the inside.

You came to visit. How flattering.

“I came to understand.”

Understanding is the most expensive thing in the world. More expensive than gold, more expensive than magic, more expensive than love. Are you sure you can afford it?

“I’m not paying you anything. I’m observing.”

Observe, then. But know this: every moment you spend in my water, I learn you. Your fears, your desires, your guilt. The boy on the slave ship. The village you betrayed. The wife who left you because you loved dead things more than living ones. I know all of it, scholar. The water knows. And knowledge, in this world, is power.

Kian left the bilge. He did not go back.

But he thought about what Zulfaqar had said —
the wife who left you because you loved dead things more than living ones
— and he thought about Sara, whose face he could still see, whose voice he could still hear, whose judgment he could still feel like a bruise that wouldn’t heal. And he thought:
If I make the deal, and the price is a memory of her — would that be payment, or relief?

The thought frightened him more than the Marid.

Iskandar appeared on the horizon at sunset.

Kian was on deck, watching the sea darken. The two moons were rising, the whole one silver, the broken one trailing its amber debris across the sky like a wound that wouldn’t close.

And there, where the sea met the sky, lights.

Not a coastline. Not a harbor. A
city
— floating on the water, spread across dozens, maybe hundreds, of barges and platforms and anchored vessels, connected by bridges of rope and wood and bone. The lights were lanterns — thousands of them, oil and candle and something that burned blue-white and might have been magical — and they reflected off the sea’s surface, creating a mirror-city below, so that Iskandar appeared to exist in two worlds simultaneously, one above the water and one below.

“Beautiful, isn’t it?” Amir appeared at his elbow. The cabin boy had a talent for materialization that bordered on the supernatural. “The Floating City. Iskandar the Eternal. Built on the spot where Alexander — the one your world calls the Great — threw his crown into the sea and said, ‘If I cannot conquer the water, I will build upon it.’ That was two thousand years ago. The city has been floating ever since.”

“Alexander the Great was here?”

“Alexander the Fool, we call him. He came through the sea, like you. He tried to conquer Al-Khaliq with an army of ten thousand. The sea ate them in a week. Alexander survived because the sea found him amusing. It finds conquerors amusing. It finds scholars more so.” Amir glanced at Kian. “You’ll like Iskandar. It has libraries.”

“I don’t need libraries. I need answers.”

“In Iskandar, they’re the same thing. Also, you need a meal. A real meal. Not Omar’s bread and pistachios.” Amir’s face softened — the first time Kian had seen the boy look his age. “There’s a tavern I know. The Mulberry Tree. The food is good. The tea is better. And the woman who runs it owes me a favor.”

“A cabin boy with tavern connections.”

“I am a person of many talents and few explanations.” Amir smiled. It was a strange smile — old and young at the same time, knowing and innocent, the smile of someone who had secrets and enjoyed having them. “We dock at dawn. Get some sleep, scholar. Tomorrow, you eat.”

He vanished below deck. Kian stood at the rail and watched Iskandar grow on the horizon, a city of light floating on a sea of tears, and felt — beneath the fear, beneath the grief, beneath the guilt that was becoming his constant companion — something that might have been hope.

Or might have been hunger. In this world, he was learning, the two were difficult to distinguish.

In the bilge, Zulfaqar stirred. The water glowed. The ship hummed.

And the single black tooth in the captain’s chest whispered to no one and everyone, singing a song about a mouth that was waiting to open, and a world that was waiting to be eaten, and a scholar who was waiting to say yes.
