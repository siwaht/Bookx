Chapter 24: Mastema’s Cathedral

“Mercy is the cruelest argument. It asks you to agree that suffering is worse than nothing, and nothing is worse than everything, and therefore everything must end. The logic is perfect. The conclusion is monstrous. This is why I distrust logic and worship pain.” — Kian, in debate with Mastema

The cathedral floated on the bones of whales.

Kian saw it first through the morning fog — a structure that should not have existed, that defied every principle of architecture and buoyancy and the basic agreement between gravity and matter that kept things either on the water or under it but not, as this thing was,
above
it. The cathedral hovered three meters above the sea’s surface, supported by a lattice of whale ribs and coral columns that grew downward into the water like roots, drawing sustenance from the sea the way a tree draws sustenance from soil. The structure itself was bone — not whale bone but
human
bone, thousands of skeletons fused and shaped and polished into walls, arches, buttresses, and a spire that rose fifty meters into the fog and terminated in a point that was, unmistakably, a finger. A human finger, scaled up to monstrous proportions, pointing at the sky with the accusatory certainty of a prophet identifying the source of all suffering.

“That,” Omar said, “is the most disturbing thing I have ever seen. And I have seen a man eat his own foot to win a bet.”

“Mastema’s cathedral,” Samira said. Her voice was flat — the flatness of a woman who had lived four hundred years and had seen things that made bone cathedrals look like garden sheds. “He built it from the congregants who died in his service. He considers it an honor — their bones become the house of worship, their skulls become the windows, their teeth become the floor tiles. He calls it ‘the architecture of devotion.’”

“He’s insane,” Fatima said.

“He’s a priest,” Samira replied. “The distinction is often academic.”

The cathedral’s congregation was visible on the lower platforms — figures moving with the slow, deliberate gait of people who had been fundamentally altered by proximity to something they worshipped. Even from a distance, Kian could see the changes: skin that had taken on the texture of barnacles, eyes that had been replaced by small, dark spheres that might have been pearls or might have been something worse, gills that opened and closed on necks that had forgotten how to breathe air. The congregants had been human once. They were becoming something else — something aquatic, something devoted, something that had surrendered its humanity to a god that was sleeping and a priest who was not.

Mastema himself stood at the cathedral’s entrance — a doorway formed by two whale jawbones meeting in an arch. He was tall, thin, dressed in robes that appeared to be woven from seaweed and human hair. His skin was pale — not the pale of illness but the pale of something that had replaced its blood with seawater, which was, according to Samira, exactly what he had done. His veins were visible beneath his translucent skin, and they ran blue-green, the color of deep ocean, the color of things that lived where sunlight couldn’t reach.

He was smiling. The smile was the worst part. It was genuine — warm, welcoming, the smile of a host greeting expected guests. Mastema had been waiting for them the way everything in this world waited for Kian — with the patient certainty of a story that knew its own plot.

“Welcome,” Mastema called across the water. His voice carried unnaturally — not loud but
present
, filling the fog the way incense fills a room, permeating everything, leaving a residue of sound that lingered in the ears long after the words had ended. “Welcome to the Cathedral of the Drowned God. I have been expecting you. I have prepared a sermon.”

“We’re not here for a sermon,” Kian said. He stood at the Dhow’s prow, his prosthetic hands gripping the rail, his voice carrying the flat, efficient authority that the pearls had given him. “We’re here for the teeth.”

“Of course you are. Everyone who comes here is here for the teeth. The teeth are the only thing of value in this cathedral — everything else is bone and devotion, and devotion, I have learned, has no market value. But the teeth—” Mastema’s smile widened. “The teeth are
currency
. The teeth are
power
. The teeth are fragments of a god who will wake and end all suffering, and ending suffering is the only moral act, and moral acts are the only things worth doing, and therefore—” He spread his arms, the seaweed robes billowing. “Therefore, I invite you to come inside. To hear my argument. To debate, if you wish. I am a priest, but I am also a scholar, and scholars, I believe, prefer debate to violence.”

He was looking at Kian. Specifically at Kian. At the prosthetic hands, the tattoos, the scars, the eyes that had seen the Simurgh’s vision and carried it like a stone. Mastema saw something in Kian that he recognized — the scholar’s hunger, the need to understand, the compulsion to engage with ideas even when the ideas were monstrous. Especially when the ideas were monstrous.

“It’s a trap,” Omar said quietly.

“Everything is a trap,” Kian said. “The question is whether the trap contains what we need.”

He boarded the cathedral.

The interior was worse than the exterior.

The bone walls were translucent in places, and through them, the sea was visible — a shifting, blue-green presence that pressed against the cathedral like a living thing, like a congregant trying to get closer to the altar. The floor was, as Samira had said, made of teeth — human teeth, thousands of them, set in mortar like mosaic tiles, creating patterns that Kian’s stripped-down mind recognized as mathematical: Fibonacci spirals, fractal geometries, the golden ratio rendered in enamel and dentin. The ceiling was skulls — inverted, their empty eye sockets serving as windows through which a dim, aquatic light filtered, giving the interior the quality of an underwater cave.

The congregants lined the walls. They stood in alcoves carved from ribcages, their barnacle-skin glistening, their pearl-eyes tracking Kian’s movement with the synchronized precision of a school of fish. They didn’t speak. They breathed — a collective, rhythmic inhalation and exhalation that sounded like waves, that
was
waves, the congregants’ lungs having synchronized with the sea’s tides until the distinction between breathing and drowning had become, for them, irrelevant.

Mastema led Kian to the altar. It was a whale’s skull — massive, ancient, the bone yellowed and carved with the same impossible script that decorated Kian’s arm-tattoo. Inside the skull, on a shelf of bone, sat two pearls. They were larger than the others Kian had collected — the size of plums, their surfaces not smooth but
textured
, covered in tiny ridges that formed, when Kian looked closely, faces. Screaming faces. Laughing faces. Faces caught in the moment between one expression and another, frozen in the transition between joy and horror, which was, Kian realized, the same transition, the same moment, the same face.

“Beautiful, aren’t they?” Mastema said. He stood beside the altar with his hands clasped, his seawater veins pulsing with the tide, his smile unchanged. “I’ve kept them for forty years. They speak to me. Not in words — in
feelings
. Hunger. Anticipation. The particular excitement of a sleeper who knows the alarm is about to ring. Ahriman is dreaming, and his dreams are becoming more vivid, and the vividness is a countdown, and the countdown is—” He paused. “But you know this. You carry seventeen of his teeth. You can hear the song.”

“I can hear it,” Kian said.

“And? Is it not beautiful?”

“It’s beautiful. Beauty is not an argument for truth.”

Mastema’s smile shifted — not disappearing but
deepening
, the way a river deepens when it narrows, the same volume of water compressed into a smaller channel, the current faster, more dangerous. “No. Beauty is not an argument for truth. But it is an argument for
value
. The song is beautiful because the thing it promises is valuable. And the thing it promises is the end of suffering. All suffering. Every broken bone, every broken heart, every child who dies of dirty water in a slave ship’s hold—” He looked at Kian, and the look was precise, surgical, the look of a man who knew exactly which wound to press. “Every silence that haunts a scholar who couldn’t tell a dying boy a story. All of it. Ended. Not through healing — healing is temporary, healing is a bandage on a wound that will reopen. Ended through
completion
. The way a story ends. The way a song ends. The way everything ends, if you let it.”

“You’re arguing for annihilation,” Kian said.

“I’m arguing for mercy. Is a surgeon cruel for cutting? Is a mercy-killer evil? The world is in pain, Kian. Not some of it — all of it. Every living thing suffers. Every conscious mind carries a burden of grief and fear and loneliness that it cannot put down, that it was born carrying, that it will carry until it dies. And death — death is not the end of suffering. Death is the
transformation
of suffering, the conversion of a living pain into a dead memory, and the memory is carried by the living, and the living suffer, and the cycle continues, and the cycle is the world, and the world is pain, and pain is—”

“A false binary,” Kian said.

Mastema stopped. The smile flickered.

“Your argument assumes the only options are suffering or oblivion,” Kian continued. His voice was calm — the calm of the pearls, the calm of a mind that had been refined by loss until only the essential remained, and the essential was
logic
, and logic was the weapon he’d been trained to wield, the weapon that worked even without hands. “That’s a false binary. A Nash equilibrium exists between total destruction and total suffering — a state in which the costs of change are balanced against the benefits of stability, in which suffering is minimized without being eliminated, in which the system persists not because it’s perfect but because it’s
better than the alternatives
.”

“Game theory,” Mastema said. The word was spoken with the particular contempt of a priest encountering mathematics. “You’re applying game theory to theology.”

“I’m applying game theory to your argument, which is theological in form but logical in structure. You claim that suffering is universal and therefore the world should end. But universality is not an argument for elimination — it’s an argument for
management
. Pain is universal. So is joy. So is love. So is the taste of mansaf eaten with people you care about on a ship that’s falling apart. Your argument accounts for the pain but not the mansaf. Your calculus is incomplete.”

“The mansaf is temporary.”

“Everything is temporary. Temporariness is not a deficiency — it’s a
feature
. The mansaf is good
because
it ends. The love is precious
because
it’s finite. The story matters
because
it has a last page. If you remove the ending, you remove the meaning. Ahriman’s promise of oblivion is not mercy — it’s
erasure
. It doesn’t end suffering. It ends the
sufferer
. And ending the sufferer is not the same as ending the suffering, any more than burning a library is the same as answering the questions in the books.”

Mastema stared at Kian. The smile was gone now — replaced by something more honest, more dangerous. Interest. The interest of a mind that had been arguing with itself for forty years and had finally found an opponent worth the effort.

“You are clever,” Mastema said. “Cleverer than the others who came for the teeth. They came with swords. You come with syllogisms. I appreciate the upgrade.” He paused. “But your argument has a flaw. You speak of Nash equilibria as if the players are rational. They are not. The world is not a game played by rational actors — it is a story told by a mad author, and the author is the sea, and the sea is made of tears, and tears are not rational. Your game theory assumes that suffering can be
managed
. I have lived for two hundred years, and I have seen no evidence of management. I have seen only accumulation. Suffering accumulates. Grief accumulates. The tears that make the sea accumulate. And the accumulation is the argument — not for oblivion, but for the
possibility
of oblivion. The possibility that there is an exit. That the story has a last page. That the author can put down the pen.”

“The author putting down the pen doesn’t end the story,” Kian said. “It ends the
writing
. The story continues in the minds of everyone who read it. Ahriman’s awakening wouldn’t end the world — it would end the
experience
of the world. And the experience is the point. The experience is the mansaf and the karak chai and the boy who wanted to be a sailor and the woman who pours tea into the sea. The experience is everything. Remove it, and you don’t have mercy. You have nothing. And nothing is not better than something. Nothing is just — nothing.”

The debate continued for hours. The fog thickened and thinned. The congregants breathed their tidal breath. And Kian and Mastema argued — not with swords but with the particular, exhausting, exhilarating combat of two minds that took ideas seriously enough to fight over them.

Kian did not win. Mastema did not win. The debate was not the kind that produces winners — it was the kind that produces
clarity
, the kind that forces both participants to see their own positions more clearly by seeing them reflected in the opposition, and the clarity was its own reward, and the reward was its own cost, because clarity, in a world of pearls and songs and dying ships, was a luxury that Kian could not afford and could not refuse.

While Kian debated, Fatima and Samira stole the pearls.

This was the plan — Kian’s plan, made alone, without consultation, with the efficient ruthlessness that the pearls had given him. Distract the priest with debate. Send the witches for the teeth. The plan was cold. The plan was effective. The plan was the kind of plan that the old Kian — the Kian with hands and books and mercy — would have found distasteful, because it used Mastema’s genuine intellectual engagement as a weapon, because it exploited the priest’s loneliness and hunger for conversation, because it was, in its essence, a con.

Samira shapeshifted. This was a skill she had not used in decades — the old magic of Tiamat’s coven, the ability to wear another’s form the way Nazia wore her shed skins. The cost was high — Samira’s own identity blurred at the edges, her sense of self becoming porous, permeable, the boundary between
Samira
and
not-Samira
thinning to gossamer. She became a congregant — barnacle-skin, pearl-eyes, tidal breathing — and moved through the cathedral’s alcoves with the synchronized gait of the devoted, invisible in her conformity.

Fatima provided cover. Not physical cover — magical cover. She wove a spell of
inattention
, a subtle enchantment that didn’t make the pearls invisible but made them
uninteresting
, that redirected the congregants’ pearl-eyes away from the altar the way a magnet redirects a compass needle, gently, imperceptibly, without force.

It was the first time Fatima used offensive magic. Not combat magic — she had used that against the navy, the cold, precise killing that the inquisitors had created in her. This was different. This was
manipulation
, the magical equivalent of a lie, and lies were not something the old Fatima would have used, because the old Fatima believed in truth the way she believed in surgery — as a discipline, a practice, a moral commitment to seeing things as they were rather than as you wanted them to be.

The new Fatima used the lie without hesitation. The new Fatima was good at it. Too good. The spell flowed from her hands with the ease of a natural talent finally unleashed, and the ease was terrifying, because ease in magic meant
affinity
, and affinity meant the magic was not a tool she was using but a part of her she was expressing, and the part was growing, and the growth was not under her control.

Samira reached the altar. Samira took the pearls. Samira returned to the Dhow. The congregants didn’t notice. Mastema didn’t notice. Kian, still debating, still arguing about suffering and mansaf and the value of temporary things, kept the distraction running.

It worked until it didn’t.

Mastema felt the pearls leave. Not saw —
felt
. The pearls had been part of his cathedral for forty years, embedded in its spiritual architecture the way the bones were embedded in its physical architecture, and their removal was not a theft but an
amputation
, a severing of something that had been connected, and the severing hurt, and the hurt broke through the debate like a stone through glass.

Mastema’s face changed. The interest drained. The intellectual engagement evaporated. What remained was something older, something that had been hiding behind the priest’s courtesy and the scholar’s curiosity — rage. Not hot rage, not the explosive fury of a man betrayed. Cold rage. The rage of a priest whose relics have been desecrated, whose cathedral has been violated, whose god has been robbed. The rage of a man who has replaced his blood with seawater and his heart with devotion and has nothing left to lose except the things he worships.

“You stole from me,” Mastema said. His voice was different now — not the warm, carrying voice of the host but something deeper, something that resonated with the whale-bone walls and the tooth-tile floor and the skull-windows and the tidal breathing of the congregants. “You sat in my cathedral and debated my theology and stole my god’s teeth while I was distracted by your
syllogisms
.”

“Yes,” Kian said. There was no point in lying. The pearls were gone. The plan had worked. The only question now was the cost.

“I respected you,” Mastema said. “I respected your mind. I respected your argument about the mansaf and the temporary and the Nash equilibrium. I thought — for the first time in forty years, I thought I had found someone worth talking to. Someone who understood that ideas matter. Someone who would engage honestly.”

“I did engage honestly. The debate was real. The theft was also real. Both things can be true.”

“Both things can be true,” Mastema repeated. “Yes. Both things can be true. And both things can have consequences.”

He raised his hands. The seawater in his veins glowed — blue-green, bioluminescent, the light of deep ocean, the light of things that lived where sunlight couldn’t reach. The cathedral responded. The bone walls groaned. The tooth-tiles shifted. The skull-windows opened their empty eyes wider, and through them, the sea pressed closer, and the sea was not patient now, the sea was
angry
, and the anger was Mastema’s, channeled through the cathedral’s architecture, focused through forty years of devotion into a single, devastating act.

He cursed the Dhow.

Not with fire. Not with storm. Not with the dramatic, visible destruction that Kian had prepared for. Mastema cursed the ship with
rot
. A quiet curse. A patient curse. A curse that would work the way Mastema’s theology worked — slowly, inevitably, with the gentle insistence of a truth that didn’t need to shout because it knew it was right. The wood would soften. The ropes would fray. The sails would thin. The ship would die — not in a blaze of glory but in a whisper of decay, the way all things died, the way Mastema believed all things
should
die, quietly, mercifully, returning to the nothing from which they came.

“Your ship will rot,” Mastema said. His voice was calm again — the calm of a man who has made a decision and is at peace with it. “From the inside. The way the world rots. The way everything rots. You have weeks. Perhaps less. The curse is not dramatic — I am not a dramatic man. The curse is
true
. And truth, as you said, is not beautiful. But it is valuable.”

Kian left the cathedral. He walked across the whale-bone platform and down to the Dhow and he did not look back, because looking back would mean seeing Mastema’s face, and Mastema’s face would show the thing that Kian didn’t want to see — not rage, not triumph, but
sadness
. The sadness of a lonely man who had found a conversation partner and lost him in the same hour. The sadness of a priest whose god was sleeping and whose cathedral was made of bones and whose only companions were congregants who had forgotten how to speak.

On the Dhow, the crew was waiting. Omar had the pearls — Samira had handed them off the moment she’d returned, her shapeshifted form dissolving back into her own with a shudder that left her gasping on the deck, her storm-green eyes unfocused, her sense of self reassembling like a shattered mirror being glued back together, the pieces fitting but the cracks visible.

“That was cruel,” Omar said to Kian. Not accusatory — observational. The quartermaster’s voice carried the flat neutrality of a man stating a market price. “Using his loneliness against him. Using the debate as a distraction. That was—”

“Effective.”

“I was going to say
cruel
. I said cruel. I meant cruel.” Omar looked at Kian with an expression that Kian had not seen from the quartermaster before — not the jovial mask, not the calculating assessment, but something rawer, something that looked like the expression Omar had worn in Tariq’s dining hall, the expression of a man recognizing a pattern he’d seen before and didn’t like. “I have cheated merchants. I have lied to kings. I have stolen from men who deserved it and men who didn’t. But I have never used a lonely man’s need for conversation as a weapon. You crossed that line without hesitating.”

“We needed the pearls.”

“We needed the pearls,” Omar repeated. “Yes. We always need the pearls. The pearls are always the reason. The pearls justify everything. The pearls are the most convenient excuse for cruelty that I have ever encountered, and I have encountered many excuses for cruelty, because cruelty is the one industry that never lacks for justification.”

Kian said nothing. The nothing was not the strategic silence he’d learned from Qadir or the cowardly silence of the slave ship. It was the silence of a man who knew Omar was right and did not care, and the not-caring was new, and the newness was the pearls, and the pearls were singing, and the song was beautiful.

Umm Hani emerged from the galley. She looked at Kian. She looked at Omar. She looked at the space between them, which was wider than the physical distance, which was the width of a moral line that had been crossed and could not be uncrossed.

“Eat,” she said. To both of them. “Whatever this is, it can wait until after you eat. Nothing good has ever been decided on an empty stomach. Nothing good has ever been decided on a full stomach either, but at least you won’t be hungry while you make your mistakes.”

They ate. The food was simple — rice and fish, the democratic meal, the food for survivors. They ate in silence, and the silence was not companionable, and the food was not communion, and the meal was not a prayer. It was fuel. It was sustenance. It was the minimum required to keep bodies functioning in service of a mission that was consuming the people who served it.

Nineteen pearls. The ship was dying. Kian felt the pearls’ song growing louder — nineteen voices now, harmonizing, building, the jaw three-fifths formed, the bite approaching.

He caught himself humming along. The melody was beautiful. The melody was hungry. The melody was becoming, with each new pearl, less distinguishable from the sound of his own heartbeat.

He didn’t stop.
