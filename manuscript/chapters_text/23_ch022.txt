Chapter 19: Omar’s Monster

“Survival requires becoming the monster. The question is whether you can become the monster and then become something else.” — Omar

The slaver king’s name was Tariq al-Mansur, and he was the first man who had ever owned Omar.

Omar recognized him the moment they entered the merchant city of Dar al-Salaam — a sprawling port on the eastern coast, built on trade and slavery and the comfortable fiction that the two were different things. Tariq controlled the city’s slave markets, its shipping lanes, and its treasury, which contained — according to Amir’s intelligence — two of Ahriman’s teeth, acquired decades ago from a collector who had died under circumstances that Tariq described as “unfortunate” and everyone else described as “murder.”

But before Omar saw Tariq, the crew nearly fell apart over a goat.

The goat was Umm Hani’s idea. She’d spotted it at the harbor market — a fat, healthy animal with a beard more dignified than Omar’s — and declared that the ship needed fresh meat. “Dried fish,” she said, with the contempt of a woman who had been cooking dried fish for three weeks and had reached the limits of her considerable creativity. “Dried fish for breakfast. Dried fish for lunch. Dried fish for dinner. I am a cook, not a mortician. I refuse to serve another dead thing that has been dead for so long it has forgotten what alive tasted like.”

The goat cost twelve silver dirhams. Omar refused to pay.

“Twelve dirhams for a goat,” he said, his voice carrying the particular outrage he reserved for financial injustice, which was to say, any transaction in which he was not the one profiting. “I could buy a small house for twelve dirhams. I could buy a boat. I could buy a wife — not a good wife, but a wife nonetheless.”

“You could not buy a wife for twelve dirhams,” Leila said. “You could not buy a wife for twelve thousand dirhams. No woman alive would accept that transaction.”

“This is hurtful and also probably accurate. But the point stands. The goat is overpriced.”

“The goat is necessary,” Umm Hani said. She had the goat by a rope and was already leading it toward the ship. “I am buying the goat. You are paying for the goat. This is not a negotiation. This is an announcement.”

“Everything is a negotiation. I once negotiated the sunrise. I told the sun it was too early and it waited an hour. This is a true story.”

“This is a lie. Pay for the goat.”

Omar paid for the goat. He paid with the expression of a man being slowly murdered by a farm animal, and he spent the next three hours calculating the goat’s cost-per-meal ratio and announcing the results to anyone who would listen, which was no one, because the crew had learned that Omar’s financial monologues were best survived through strategic deafness.

The goat, meanwhile, ate Omar’s spare turban. Omar discovered this an hour later and stood on the deck holding the chewed remains of his finest silk headwear, staring at the goat with an expression of such profound betrayal that Kian had to look away to keep from laughing.

“The goat,” Omar said, “has declared war.”

“The goat ate your turban,” Leila said. “This is not war. This is lunch.”

“It was
silk
. Embroidered silk. From the looms of Isfahan. That turban cost more than the goat.”

“Then you should have worn it instead of leaving it where a goat could reach it. This is a lesson in asset management. You’re welcome.”

The goat bleated. It sounded, to everyone except Omar, like laughter.

Kian was still smiling about the goat when they entered the market square, and then Omar stopped walking, and the smile died.

The market square of Dar al-Salaam was a different animal from Iskandar’s floating bazaars. Iskandar was a city that pretended to be civilized. Dar al-Salaam didn’t bother pretending. The market was a sprawl of competing ambitions — legitimate merchants alongside fences selling stolen cargo, money-changers whose exchange rates shifted depending on how desperate you looked, and in the alleys behind the main square, the quieter trades: a woman selling forged documents so convincing that the Caliph’s own scribes couldn’t tell the difference, a man offering passage on ships that didn’t officially exist, and in one shadowed doorway, a boy no older than Amir running a three-card monte game with cards made from sharkskin, fleecing a sailor who was too drunk to realize the game was unwinnable.

Omar navigated it all with the ease of a man returning to his native language. He nodded to a fence. He waved off a pickpocket with a look that said
I see you and I’m not worth the trouble
. He bought information from a tea seller — not tea, information, the tea was just the cover — about Tariq’s compound, his guards, his schedule, his debts. The information cost three silver dirhams and a promise that Omar would remember the tea seller’s name if things went well. Omar would remember. Omar remembered everything that had a price.

A woman in a doorway caught Omar’s eye and raised a hand — not a wave but a signal, two fingers, the gesture of someone in the trade. Omar excused himself for ten minutes and returned with a folded paper.

“Dalal’s network reaches this far?” Kian asked.

“Dalal’s network reaches everywhere there are men with secrets and women with ears. She has a girl in Tariq’s household. Has had for years.” Omar unfolded the paper. “Tariq’s debts. All of them. Amounts, creditors, interest rates, dates of default. Dalal’s girl copied them from his ledger while he slept.” He studied the numbers with the reverence of a man reading scripture. “This is beautiful. This is — Kian, this man is drowning in debt. He owes more than his entire estate is worth. He’s been borrowing against his slave inventory to cover the interest, which means—”

“Which means you can buy his debts.”

“Which means I can buy
him
.” Omar’s smile was not the jovial smile. It was the other one — the one that lived beneath the jokes, the one that had been sharpened on a slave ship’s deck when he was twelve. “Dalal charges too much. She always charges too much. But she delivers.”

Omar saw Tariq across the market square — an old man now, shrunken by age, leaning on a cane, surrounded by bodyguards who were younger and stronger and more afraid of their employer than of anything the world could throw at them. Tariq was small. He had always been small — small in stature, small in spirit, small in the specific way that men who need to own other people are always small, compensating for an interior emptiness with an exterior accumulation of property.

Omar stopped walking. His face — the jovial, calculating, perpetually performing face that the crew knew — went blank. Not angry. Not afraid. Blank. The face of a man who has encountered something he thought he’d left behind and is discovering that the past is not a place you leave but a weight you carry, and the weight never gets lighter, and the carrying never gets easier.

“Omar?” Kian said.

“I know him,” Omar said. His voice was flat. “He owned me. When I was twelve. He was the merchant. The one I told you about. The one who—” He stopped. Swallowed. “The one.”

Kian looked at Tariq al-Mansur — the small, old, cane-leaning man in the market square — and tried to reconcile what he saw with what Omar had described on the drunken night at the Isle of Mirrors. The things that had been done. The things Omar didn’t talk about. The cabin boy’s hell.

“We can find another way to get the teeth,” Kian said. “We don’t have to—”

“Yes we do.” Omar’s voice was still flat, but something was moving beneath the flatness — something large and dark and old, rising from the depths the way the Crawling Reef rose from the sea floor. “The teeth are in his treasury. His treasury is in his compound. His compound is in his city. There is no way to get the teeth without going through him. And I—” He paused. The something beneath the flatness broke the surface. It was not rage. It was not grief. It was
purpose
. “I need to go through him. I’ve been carrying him for twenty years. It’s time to set him down.”

Omar negotiated. This was what Omar did — he negotiated the way Leila fought, the way Fatima healed, the way Kian catalogued. It was his art, his weapon, his identity. And in Dar al-Salaam, negotiating with Tariq al-Mansur, Omar was at his most brilliant and his most dangerous.

He didn’t reveal himself. He approached Tariq as a merchant — a wealthy, connected, influential merchant representing a consortium of northern traders (the same fictional consortium he’d used on Faris, because Omar believed in recycling good lies). He negotiated access to Tariq’s compound, to his treasury, to the teeth — not through force or deception but through commerce, through the language that Tariq understood better than any other: profit.

The negotiation took three days. Three days of meetings in Tariq’s compound — a sprawling estate of white stone and blue tile, staffed by slaves who moved with the silent efficiency of people who had learned that visibility was dangerous. Three days of Omar sitting across from the man who had owned him, who had hurt him, who had shaped him into the person he was — the jovial mask, the calculating mind, the armor of appetite and humor that protected the wounded boy underneath.

On the first day, Omar nearly ruined everything.

It was a stupid mistake — the kind of mistake that only a brilliant person could make, because it required intelligence to construct and intelligence to fail to see. Omar, in his fictional merchant persona, mentioned a trade route through the Strait of Sorrows. The detail was specific, convincing, and completely wrong. The Strait of Sorrows had been closed for six years — a blockade by the Caliph’s navy, common knowledge to anyone who actually traded in the region. Omar knew this. Omar had known this for years. But Omar was sitting across from the man who had broken him as a child, and his mind — his magnificent, calculating, usually infallible mind — was not entirely his own. The twelve-year-old boy was in the room, and the twelve-year-old boy was terrified, and terror makes even brilliant men stupid.

Tariq’s eyes narrowed. “The Strait of Sorrows,” he said. “An interesting route. Particularly since it’s been blockaded since the Year of the Red Tide.”

The room went cold. Omar felt it — the slip, the crack in the performance, the moment where the mask shifted and the face beneath was visible. He had approximately two seconds to recover before Tariq’s suspicion hardened into certainty.

“The blockade,” Omar said, and his voice was steady, and the steadiness was the most impressive thing Kian had ever witnessed, because Kian could feel through the bahr-sense the tsunami of panic beneath it, “is precisely why the route is valuable. Blockades end. The navy’s funding is tied to the Caliph’s treasury, and the Caliph’s treasury is tied to the spice trade, and the spice trade is declining. Within two years — eighteen months, if my projections are correct — the blockade will be unsustainable. The consortium I represent is positioning for the reopening. We are not trading in the present, Tariq al-Mansur. We are trading in the future. And the future, unlike the present, is always for sale.”

Tariq studied him. The old man’s eyes — small, sharp, the eyes of a predator that had grown old by being smarter than its prey — searched Omar’s face for the lie. They didn’t find it. Not because the lie wasn’t there, but because Omar had buried it beneath a truth — the blockade
was
unsustainable, the spice trade
was
declining, and the future
was
for sale. The best lies, Omar had taught Kian, are truths wearing someone else’s clothes.

“Interesting,” Tariq said. And the negotiation continued.

That night, on the Dhow, Omar vomited over the rail. Not from drink — from fear. The delayed reaction of a body that had held itself together through sheer will and was now, in the safety of the ship, allowing itself to fall apart.

Kian found him there. “The Strait of Sorrows,” Kian said.

“Don’t.”

“You knew it was blockaded.”

“I said don’t.”

“You made a mistake. A stupid mistake. The smartest man I know, making the dumbest—”

“I am not the smartest man you know. I am a fat merchant who counts things and tells jokes and eats too much, and I am sitting across from the man who—” Omar stopped. Gripped the rail. His knuckles were white. “He doesn’t recognize me. Twenty years. A hundred pounds. A beard. He looks at me and sees a merchant. He doesn’t see the boy. And I don’t know if that’s a relief or the worst thing that’s ever happened to me.”

“It can be both.”

“It can’t be both. Things can’t be two things at once. That’s not how—”

“Omar. You’re a man who is simultaneously a pirate and a merchant and a comedian and a quartermaster and a survivor and a friend. You are at least six things at once on any given day. You can handle two feelings.”

Omar was quiet for a long time. The sea lapped against the hull. The goat — still aboard, still eating things that didn’t belong to it — bleated from somewhere below deck.

“The goat ate my second-best turban,” Omar said.

“I know.”

“I am going to eat the goat. This is not revenge. This is justice.”

“Umm Hani already has plans for the goat.”

“Umm Hani’s plans and my plans are aligned. The goat’s plans are irrelevant. The goat has forfeited its right to plans by eating my turban.” He straightened. Wiped his mouth. The mask was going back on — Kian could see it happening, the jovial armor reassembling piece by piece, the wounded boy retreating behind the fat merchant’s smile. “Tomorrow I go back. Tomorrow I sit across from him again. And I will not make another mistake.”

“And if you do?”

“Then I will make a better mistake. A more creative mistake. A mistake so elaborate that it becomes indistinguishable from genius.” He clapped Kian on the shoulder. “This is the secret of success, scholar. Not avoiding mistakes. Surviving them with enough style that people think you meant to do it.”

Tariq didn’t recognize him. Twenty years, a hundred pounds, and a beard had transformed the skinny cabin boy into a man that his former owner couldn’t identify. Omar sat across from Tariq and smiled and negotiated and ate the food that Tariq’s slaves prepared and drank the tea that Tariq’s slaves poured, and he felt — beneath the performance, beneath the mask, beneath the twenty years of distance — the twelve-year-old boy who had been powerless and afraid and had sworn to never be either again.

On the third day, Omar made his move. Not for the teeth — for Tariq himself.

He bought him.

The transaction was legal, technically. Tariq had debts — old debts, accumulated over decades of extravagant living and poor investments, debts that his slave trade’s profits could no longer cover. Omar, through a network of intermediaries and shell companies that he’d established in the three days of negotiation, acquired those debts. All of them. And then he called them in.

Tariq couldn’t pay. Omar offered an alternative: Tariq’s compound, his treasury, his slaves, his ships, his name — everything. In exchange for the debts being forgiven. It was a hostile takeover conducted with the precision of a military campaign and the courtesy of a tea ceremony.

Tariq signed. He had no choice. The law of Dar al-Salaam was clear: debts must be paid, and the debtor who cannot pay becomes the property of the creditor. Tariq al-Mansur, slaver king, owner of men, the man who had broken a twelve-year-old boy in ways that the boy would carry for the rest of his life, was now Omar’s property.

Omar owned the man who had owned him.

He made Tariq serve dinner. In the compound’s great hall, at the table where Tariq had entertained merchants and princes and the powerful men of Dar al-Salaam, Omar sat in Tariq’s chair and ate Tariq’s food and watched Tariq — small, old, trembling — carry plates and pour wine and perform the tasks that his slaves had performed for decades.

The crew watched. Kian watched. Fatima watched with her arms crossed and her expression unreadable. Leila watched with an expression that was very readable — she understood this. She understood the need to make the master serve. She understood it in her bones, in the scars on her wrists where the chains had been, in the part of her that would never fully leave the barrel where they’d held her under.

“More wine,” Omar said. His voice was flat. Not cruel — flat. The voice of a man performing a ritual he’d rehearsed in his mind for twenty years and finding that the performance was nothing like the rehearsal.

Tariq poured. His hands shook. The wine spilled. Omar watched the spill and said nothing, and the nothing was louder than any words.

“Omar,” Kian said quietly. “This isn’t—”

“Don’t.” Omar’s eyes didn’t leave Tariq. “Don’t tell me what this isn’t. You don’t know what this is. You don’t know what he did. You don’t know what it cost me to sit in this chair and eat this food and watch this man pour wine with the same hands that—” He stopped. Breathed. “You don’t know.”

“I know enough.”

“You know nothing. You know the outline. You know the shape of it. You don’t know the details. The details are where the devil lives, scholar, and the devil in this story has small hands and a neat beard and he’s standing right there, pouring wine, and he doesn’t even know who I am.” Omar’s voice cracked. Just slightly. Just enough. “He doesn’t know who I am. He broke me and he doesn’t even remember doing it. I am the most important thing that ever happened to me, and to him, I was inventory. I was a line item. I was a number in a ledger that he closed twenty years ago and never opened again.”

The table was silent. Umm Hani stood in the doorway with a plate of food she’d prepared herself — she refused to eat anything Tariq’s cooks had made — and her face was a landscape of grief and fury and the devastating compassion of a woman who had been a slave herself.

“Eat,” Umm Hani said. She set the plate in front of Omar. Mansaf — lamb cooked in dried yogurt, served over rice, the dish she made for celebrations and for grief, because in Umm Hani’s kitchen, the two were often the same thing. “Eat, Omar. Not his food. Mine. Eat mine.”

Omar looked at the mansaf. He looked at Umm Hani. And something in his face — the flat, ritual mask of the avenger — cracked, and behind it was not the quartermaster or the merchant or the comedian but the boy. The twelve-year-old boy who had been hungry and afraid and had learned to hide both behind a wall of jokes and appetite, and who was now sitting in his tormentor’s chair and discovering that revenge tasted like nothing. Like ash. Like the absence of the thing you thought would fill you.

He ate Umm Hani’s mansaf. He ate it slowly, deliberately, tasting each bite, and the tasting was a choice — a choice to be fed by love instead of revenge, to take nourishment from the woman who cooked for him freely instead of the man who had taken everything by force.

It was justice, in a sense — the symmetrical justice of a victim becoming the master. It was also ugly. The ugliness of a cycle completing itself, of a man becoming the thing he hated in order to defeat it.

Omar watched Tariq scrub the floor of the great hall — on his knees, with a brush, the way Omar had scrubbed the deck of the merchant’s ship when he was twelve — and something happened. Something shifted. The purpose that had driven Omar through three days of negotiation and twenty years of carrying — it broke. Not dramatically. Quietly. The way a fever breaks. The way a wave breaks on a shore and becomes, in the breaking, something else.

He saw himself. Not in Tariq — in the
situation
. The master and the slave. The powerful and the powerless. The cycle that had shaped him, that had made him, that had driven him to become the quartermaster, the negotiator, the man who counted and controlled and never, ever let himself be powerless again. He saw the cycle, and he saw that he was inside it, and he saw that staying inside it — owning Tariq, humiliating Tariq, becoming the merchant who had broken the boy — would not end the cycle but perpetuate it.

He freed Tariq.

Not with ceremony. Not with a speech. He simply said, “Go,” and Tariq went — out the door, into the street, into a city that no longer belonged to him, a small old man with nothing, which was exactly what he deserved and exactly what Omar no longer needed him to have.

The teeth were in the treasury. Omar opened it with Tariq’s keys and took them — two black spheres, whispering their duet of hunger. Thirteen teeth now.

That night, on the Dhow, Omar found Kian on deck.

“I am not a good man,” Omar said. The same words as the drunken night. But different now. Lighter. Not because the truth had changed but because the weight of it had shifted — from a burden carried alone to a fact acknowledged and accepted and, perhaps, in the acknowledging, diminished.

“But I am trying to become one,” Omar said. “Is that enough?”

“It’s a start,” Kian said. The same answer. But different now too.

“Starts are underrated,” Omar said. And smiled. The real smile. The one beneath the mask.

They stood at the rail for a long time, watching the lights of Dar al-Salaam recede. The city that had been Tariq’s kingdom was now Omar’s property, and Omar’s first act as its owner had been to free every slave in it — all of them, hundreds, a mass emancipation that had taken three hours and had left the city’s economy in ruins and its former slaves standing in the streets, blinking, unsure whether freedom was a gift or a punishment.

“They’ll hate you for it,” Kian said. “The merchants. The traders. The people who depended on slave labor.”

“They’ll adapt. Humans adapt. It’s the only thing we’re genuinely good at — adapting to circumstances we created and then pretending the circumstances were inevitable.” Omar leaned on the rail. “Do you know what Tariq said to me? When I freed him? He said, ‘You’ll regret this.’ Not a threat. A prediction. He said that power, once tasted, cannot be untasted. That I would spend the rest of my life wanting to own someone the way he owned me, because ownership is a hunger, and hunger doesn’t care about morality.”

“Do you believe him?”

“I believe he believes it. I believe it’s true for him. I believe it might be true for me.” Omar was quiet. The sea lapped. “Kian. Do you think people can change? Really change? Not the surface — not the habits, the manners, the mask. The underneath. The architecture. Can a man who was built by cruelty become something that cruelty didn’t design?”

Kian thought about it. He thought about himself — the scholar who had become a sailor, the rational man who had accepted magic, the coward who had found something that looked like courage if you squinted. He thought about Fatima — the healer who was becoming a weapon. About Leila — the slave who had become a warrior. About Samira — the witch who had become something like a mother.

“I think people can’t stop changing,” Kian said. “I think the question isn’t whether we change but whether we choose the direction. Tariq chose to stay what cruelty made him. You’re choosing to become something else. The choosing is the change.”

“That’s very philosophical.”

“I’ve been spending too much time with a mountain that asks questions.”

“The mountain should try spending time with a goat that eats turbans. That would cure it of philosophy very quickly.”

They laughed. The laughter was small and tired and real, and it carried across the water to where the city lights were fading, and somewhere in those fading lights, a small old man with nothing was walking into a future he hadn’t chosen, and a fat merchant with everything was sailing away from a past he couldn’t forget, and the distance between them was twenty years and a hundred pounds and a beard and the entire, unbridgeable gulf between the person you were and the person you’re trying to become.

The goat ate Omar’s third turban that night. Omar discovered it in the morning and stood on the deck holding the remains and said, to no one in particular: “The goat is testing me. The goat is a philosophical instrument sent by the universe to determine whether I have truly renounced the desire for revenge.” He looked at the goat. The goat looked at him. “I have not. The goat will be dinner. This is not revenge. This is
cuisine
.”

Umm Hani made the goat into a magnificent biryani two days later. Omar ate three helpings and declared it the finest meal he’d ever tasted. He did not mention the turbans. The turbans were between him and the goat, and the goat was now biryani, and biryani does not hold grudges.

End of Chapter 19
