

Chapter Twelve: The Weaver and the Soldier


Naram could not visit the civilian victims. They were outside the Archive — in Damascus, in Baghdad, in Mosul, in cities he had visited exactly never, in a world he had seen exactly four times from the surface entrance, a world of sun and sand and open sky that made him feel like he might fall upward into the blue.


But the Archive could visit them. The Blank Books could show him their lives. And the Blank Books, unlike the official biographies, did not lie.


He went to the Catalog of the Living in the early hours of the second day, before the morning shift, before the scriptorium filled with scribes and the corridors filled with apprentices and the refectory filled with the resigned sighs of people confronting lentils. The Catalog was empty. The golden light of the crystal shafts was just beginning — the first rays of desert sun finding the copper mirrors and bouncing downward through sixty feet of stone, arriving in the Catalog as a warm, diffuse glow that made the blood-red books on the shelves look like they were lit from within.


He found Layla’s book first. The weaver from Damascus. The woman whose biography was being completed, whose life was being written to its end, whose death by fever was scheduled for — he checked the most recent entry — nine days from now.


He opened the book and read.


The Archive’s writing was, as always, small and precise and inhuman in its regularity. But the content was not inhuman. The content was a life. A real life. A life lived in a house with a courtyard and a loom and three children under the age of ten and a husband who worked in the tannery and came home smelling of chemicals and leather and the particular exhaustion of a man who spent his days turning animal skins into something useful.


Layla wove. That was the center of her life — the loom, the threads, the patterns. She wove carpets, mostly. Not the fine silk carpets of the wealthy — the sturdy wool carpets of the working class, the carpets that covered dirt floors and kept children warm in winter and wore out after five years and were replaced by new carpets woven on the same loom by the same hands. She was good at it. Not brilliant — not the kind of weaver whose work ended up in palaces or museums. Good. Competent. Reliable. The kind of weaver whose carpets were bought by neighbors who knew that Layla’s work would hold up, that the colors wouldn’t fade, that the edges wouldn’t fray.


The Blank Book documented everything. The specific tension she applied to the warp threads — firm but not tight, the tension of a woman who had learned through years of practice exactly how much pressure the wool could bear. The way she chose her colors — not by theory or design but by instinct, by the feeling of rightness that came when she held two skeins of dyed wool side by side and knew, without being able to explain how she knew, that these two colors belonged together. The songs she sang while she wove — folk songs, mostly, songs her mother had taught her, songs about rivers and harvests and the particular sadness of women whose husbands work too much and come home too tired to talk.


She sang to her children. The book recorded this with the same dispassionate precision it applied to everything else — the specific songs, the specific children, the specific moments. She sang to her youngest, a boy of three, when he couldn’t sleep. She sang to her middle child, a girl of six, when the girl was frightened by thunderstorms. She sang to her eldest, a boy of nine, when the boy came home from school with bruises and wouldn’t say who had given them.


She sang, and the songs were ordinary, and the children were ordinary, and the life was ordinary, and the ordinariness of it was the thing that made Naram’s chest tight and his eyes hot and his hands — his ink-stained, scribe’s hands — clench into fists on the pages of a book that was documenting the last days of a woman who had done nothing wrong except exist in a world where a dead man needed her life to fuel his immortality.


Nine days. Layla had nine days. Nine days of weaving and singing and cooking lentils (everyone ate lentils, it seemed, not just the Archive) and putting children to bed and lying beside a husband who smelled of leather and chemicals and who loved her in the inarticulate, exhausted way of working men everywhere. Nine days, and then a fever would come — a fever written by Gilgamesh, a fever that was not a disease but a narrative, a story imposed on a body that had no defense against stories because the body didn’t know it was in a story.


Naram closed the book. He put it back on the shelf. He stood in the golden light of the Catalog and breathed and did not cry, because crying would not help Layla, and the only thing that would help Layla was stopping Gilgamesh, and stopping Gilgamesh required clarity, not tears.


He found Rashid’s book next. The soldier from Baghdad. Twelve years of service, twelve years of marching and fighting. The Blank Book documented the violence with dispassionate precision — but also the quiet moments. The mornings when Rashid watched the sun rise over the Tigris and felt something that might have been peace. He had a friend named Dagan. They shared a tent, shared meals, shared the wordless intimacy of men who have faced death together. Rashid was scheduled to die of fever in twelve days. And Dagan would wake up in a tent that was half-empty and would not understand why.


He found the child’s book. A seven-year-old girl in Mosul named Tiamat — a name that meant “sea,” too large for a child so small. Seven years of learning to walk, to talk, of discovering that the world was filled with wonders. Butterflies. Rain. The taste of honey.


The book documented a moment from three days ago. Tiamat had found a beetle in her courtyard — iridescent, green and gold. She had held it in her cupped hands with the total, undivided attention that only children can give to small things. She had named it Shamash, after the sun god, because the beetle was golden and the sun was golden and the connection was obvious to a seven-year-old mind that had not yet learned to distinguish between metaphor and reality.


Tiamat was scheduled to die of fever in eleven days.


Naram put the book back on the shelf. He stood in the Catalog and breathed. The golden light fell across the shelves, illuminating thousands of books, thousands of lives, thousands of people who were living and breathing and none of them knew that their lives were being documented by a sentient library and consumed by a dead man.


He thought about the list in his pocket. Twenty-three names. Twenty-three lives. Twenty-three people who would die in the next two weeks unless he found a way to stop a three-hundred-year-old consciousness that had merged with a living library and was trying to become a god.


The scale of it was — impossible. The weight of it was — crushing. The responsibility of it was — absurd. He was nineteen. He was a junior scribe. He wrote biographies of potters and merchants. He ate lentils. He had lost a song and a color and the feeling of his childhood home, and he was supposed to save twenty-three lives and stop a god and reform a system that had been running for three thousand years.


He couldn’t do it. The thought was clear and cold and honest. He couldn’t do it. He was too small. Too young. Too ordinary. Too — human.


But Layla was human too. And Rashid. And Tiamat. And the twenty others on his list. They were human, and their humanity was the thing that Gilgamesh was consuming, and if Naram couldn’t save them — if no one could save them — then their humanity would be converted into text and the text would be converted into power and the power would be converted into a god who had never been human enough to understand what he was taking.


He couldn’t do it. But he would try. Because trying was the only response to impossibility that didn’t involve surrender, and surrender meant Tiamat’s beetle and Layla’s songs and Rashid’s sunrises, all of it consumed, all of it converted, all of it lost.


He left the Catalog. He went to find Utu. He went to plan.


The clock was running. The books were writing. And somewhere in Damascus, a woman was singing to her children, and the song was beautiful, and the song was ordinary, and the song had eleven days left.




End of Chapter Twelve
