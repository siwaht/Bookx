

Chapter Fifteen: The Forgery


The morning of the second day — the last full day — began with Naram writing his first real forgery.


Not a practice lie. Not a test. A forgery with consequences, written on Archive clay with Archive ink, intended to deceive the Archive itself for long enough to buy him time.


He sat at his desk in the scriptorium, surrounded by the ordinary sounds of the morning shift — styluses scratching, scribes murmuring, an apprentice dropping a stack of tablets and being scolded by the duty master with the weary patience of a man who had scolded apprentices for dropping tablets every morning for twenty years and had long since accepted it as a law of nature. Naram picked up his stylus. He took a fresh tablet. He loosened his certainty — the technique Sarai had taught him, the mental act of holding his beliefs at arm’s length, of making himself genuinely unsure of what was true — and he wrote:


Naram is ill. He has been confined to his quarters with a fever. He will not report for duty today.


The clay held.


The scriptorium didn’t notice. That was the thing about institutions — they were so busy being institutional that they missed the revolutions happening at their desks. The scribe to Naram’s left was writing about a tanner in Basra, his stylus moving with the mechanical rhythm of a man who had been documenting tanners for six years and had long since stopped wondering why. The scribe to his right was asleep, his head resting on his forearm, his stylus still in his hand, a thin line of drool connecting his lip to the tablet in front of him. The duty master was at the front of the room, reviewing a stack of completed biographies with the expression of a man who had read ten thousand biographies and had stopped being surprised by any of them.


Nobody looked at Naram. Nobody noticed the forgery. The clay held, and the Archive accepted the lie, and the world continued to turn, and Naram sat at his desk with a fresh tablet in front of him and a forgery behind him and the strange, vertiginous feeling of having just lied to a god and gotten away with it.


He felt the cost immediately — a memory, departing. Not a small one this time. The forgery was bigger, more consequential, and the Archive demanded a proportional payment. He lost — what did he lose? He reached for his memories, checking them the way you check your pockets after walking through a crowd.


His mother’s face. Still there. Her voice. Still there. Her hands at the loom. Still there. Utu. Sarai. The Archive. The plan. All there.


But his childhood home was gone. Not the knowledge that he’d had one — he knew he’d grown up somewhere in the Archive, in the apprentice quarters, in a room with a cot and a shelf and a window that looked out onto a corridor. He knew this. But the 
feeling
 of it — the specific, sensory memory of what it was like to be seven years old in that room, to lie on that cot, to look out that window — was gone. Replaced by a blank. A space where a memory used to live, now empty, now quiet, now just a fact without a feeling.


The forgery held. The Archive believed he was sick. For the next six hours — maybe longer, if his practice had improved — the Archive would not look for him. The duty master would not expect him. Tabni would not patrol his desk. He was, as far as the institution was concerned, in his quarters with a fever.


The irony of using a fever — the same method Gilgamesh used to kill his victims — was not lost on Naram. But irony was a luxury he couldn’t afford. He had six hours. He needed to use them.


He went to the Restricted Section.




Not for the First Blood this time. For the third room. The one Sarai had told him not to enter. The one where the Archive paid the most attention. The one that contained the founding documents, the original manuscripts, the texts that had woken the Archive up.


The one that contained the biographies of the gods.


The corridor leading to the third room was different from the rest of the Restricted Section. The other corridors were stone — rough, functional, the architecture of a building that had been designed for storage rather than beauty. But this corridor was lined with something else. Clay. Fired clay, glazed in a deep blue-black that caught the lamplight and threw it back in fragments, so that walking down the corridor felt like walking through a shattered sky. The glaze was not decorative. It was text. Every inch of the clay surface was covered in writing — cuneiform, the oldest script the Archive knew, the angular, wedge-shaped characters that had been pressed into wet clay four thousand years ago by the first scribes, the ones who had invented the idea that a sound could be a shape and a shape could be a meaning and a meaning could outlast the person who made it.


Naram couldn’t read cuneiform. Not fluently. He’d studied it in his training — every scribe studied the old scripts, the way every musician studies scales — but cuneiform was a dead language in a dead script, and his reading was slow, halting, the literary equivalent of walking with a limp. He caught fragments as he passed. Names. Dates. The word for “god” — 
dingir
 — repeated so often that it became a rhythm, a drumbeat, a pulse that matched the Archive’s breathing and added to it a layer of meaning that the breathing alone did not possess.


The air in the corridor was warm. Not the warmth of the service passages, which was geological, the heat of stone that had been underground for millennia. This warmth was different. It was the warmth of a body. Of breath. Of something alive and close and paying attention. The Archive was watching him. Not the passive, ambient awareness that pervaded the main halls — the awareness of a building that knew its contents the way a body knows its cells. This was active. Focused. The awareness of a guard dog that has heard a noise and is deciding whether to bark.


Naram kept walking. His footsteps echoed on the glazed clay floor. The cuneiform text on the walls seemed to shift as he passed — not moving, not rearranging, but somehow becoming more legible, as if the corridor was adjusting itself to his reading level, dumbing down its ancient script for the benefit of a junior scribe who had no business being here.


He reached the door. It was not locked. It was not even closed. It stood open, the way a mouth stands open when the thing behind it is confident enough not to need teeth.


He needed to understand Gilgamesh’s plan. Not just the mechanics — the completions, the sacrifices, the continuous writing — but the purpose. The endgame. What was Gilgamesh building toward? What did three hundred years of ceaseless writing produce? What was the point of consuming twenty-three lives — or two hundred, or two thousand — if the consumer was already immortal, already powerful, already woven into the fabric of the Archive itself?


The answer, Naram suspected, was in the third room. In the biographies of the gods.


Because the gods of this world were not born. They were written.


This was the deepest secret of the Archive — deeper than Gilgamesh’s survival, deeper than the completions, deeper than the Unwritten. The gods that people prayed to, the gods that shaped the weather and the harvests and the fates of nations, were not supernatural beings. They were characters. Stories written with such power, such conviction, such absolute truth that they had achieved independent existence. They had stepped off the page and into reality, the way a dream sometimes steps into waking — not quite real, not quite unreal, but present. Affecting the world. Being affected by it.


He stood in the doorway of the third room and looked at them. The biographies of the gods. They were not books, not in any conventional sense. They were tablets — massive clay tablets, each one the size of a dining table, propped upright on stone pedestals arranged in a circle around the room. There were seven of them. Seven gods. Seven stories so powerful that they had become people, or people so powerful that they had become stories — the distinction, in the Archive’s system, was meaningless.


The nearest tablet was Enlil’s. The god of wind, of breath, of the space between words. His biography was written in a script that Naram had never seen — not cuneiform, not Aramaic, not any of the dozens of writing systems the Archive employed. Something older. Something that predated writing itself, that looked less like language and more like weather — swirling, turbulent, the visual equivalent of a storm captured in clay. And the tablet was warm. Not the ambient warmth of the corridor but a specific, localized heat, the warmth of a body, of a living thing, of a text that had achieved the critical mass of truth required to generate its own reality.


Naram touched it. He shouldn’t have. He knew he shouldn’t have. But the warmth was magnetic, and his hand moved before his judgment could stop it, and his fingers pressed against the clay surface, and he felt —


Everything. For one second, he felt everything. The wind on every hilltop in the world. The breath in every lung. The space between every word ever spoken. He felt the god — not as a character, not as a story, but as a presence, a vast and indifferent awareness that existed in the gap between the written and the real, that was both text and truth, both page and person, both the word and the thing the word described.


He pulled his hand back. His fingers were tingling. His heart was hammering. The room was spinning — or he was spinning, or the tablets were spinning, or the distinction between the room and himself and the tablets had temporarily dissolved and was only now reassembling itself into the familiar categories of subject and object, self and other, reader and text.


The other tablets were similar. Ishtar’s biography radiated a warmth that was less like heat and more like desire — a pull, a gravity, the narrative equivalent of beauty. Shamash’s tablet glowed, faintly, with an amber light that had no visible source. Nergal’s was cold — the only cold tablet in the room, cold the way a grave is cold, the way the absence of life is cold. Ea’s tablet was wet, somehow, its surface beaded with moisture that appeared from nowhere and evaporated before it could drip. Marduk’s was the largest, the most densely written, the most complex — a biography so vast and so detailed that it seemed to contain not just a god but a civilization, a cosmology, an entire system of meaning compressed into clay.


And the seventh tablet, in the center of the circle, was empty.


Not blank. Empty. The clay was there, the pedestal was there, the space was there. But the surface of the tablet was unmarked. No text. No script. No biography. Just raw, unfired clay, waiting. The way a page waits. The way a story waits for its author.


Naram understood. The seventh pedestal was Gilgamesh’s. The space he was building toward. The biography he was writing with the consumed lives of thousands, the narrative he was constructing word by word, death by death, until it achieved the same critical mass as the other six. Until it stepped off the page and into reality. Until the character became a god.


The biographies of the gods were the most powerful texts in the Archive. They were the proof that Onomancy’s ultimate potential was not documentation but creation. That writing could not just capture reality but 
make
 it.


And Gilgamesh wanted to join them.


Naram knew this the way he knew the Archive was alive — not through evidence but through the logic of the system. Gilgamesh had transcended his body. He had become text. He was powerful, immortal, woven into the Archive. But he was not a god. He was a scribe — the greatest scribe who had ever lived, but still a scribe. Still human, in origin if not in form. Still limited by the boundaries of what a human consciousness could achieve, even when that consciousness was distributed across hundreds of books and sustained by centuries of continuous writing.


To become a god, he needed more. More power. More truth. More lives consumed, their essences absorbed, their biographies feeding his own until his biography was so vast, so comprehensive, so absolutely true that it achieved the same critical mass that the gods’ biographies had achieved. The mass that turned a story into a being. The mass that turned words into flesh.


Twenty-three lives were not enough. Twenty-three lives were a start. A down payment. The beginning of a process that would require thousands — tens of thousands — of completed biographies, each one feeding Gilgamesh’s own, each one adding a layer of truth and power and reality to the narrative that was Gilgamesh, until the narrative was so real that it became realer than reality itself.


Gilgamesh was trying to write himself into godhood.


And the executions — the five scribes, including Naram — were not just fuel. They were witnesses. People who had seen the truth and who might, if they lived, tell others. People who might organize resistance. People who might find a way to stop the process before it reached critical mass.


Gilgamesh was cleaning house. Eliminating threats. Tying up loose ends before the final push.


Naram stood in the corridor outside the third room and felt the weight of this understanding settle on him like a physical thing — heavy, cold, absolute. He was not just fighting for his life. He was fighting for the world. Because a god written by Gilgamesh — a god whose biography was built on the consumed lives of thousands of innocent people — would not be a benevolent god. It would be a god of control. Of documentation. Of the absolute, totalitarian certainty that every life could be written, every truth could be captured, every person could be reduced to text and owned by the author.


A god of the Archive. A god of the written word. A god that would turn the world into a library and the people in it into books.


He did not enter the third room. He didn’t need to. He had what he needed — not information but understanding. The shape of the enemy. The scale of the threat. The reason why a junior scribe’s accidental discovery of a merchant’s dishonesty had triggered a death sentence.


He stood in the corridor for a long moment, the blue-black glaze reflecting his face back at him in fragments — an eye here, a jaw there, the pieces of himself scattered across the ancient text like a portrait that had been shattered and reassembled wrong. He thought about the empty tablet. The seventh pedestal. The space waiting for a god that was being built from the ground up, one death at a time, one biography at a time, one consumed life at a time.


He thought about Kidu. The seven-year-old in Mosul who liked butterflies. Whose death would add a sentence to a dozen biographies, a paragraph to a handful, a chapter to one or two. Whose fever would ripple through the narrative network like a stone dropped in a pond, generating the truth-power that Gilgamesh needed to fill that empty tablet. One child’s death. One small stone. One tiny ripple in a pond that was three thousand years wide.


The mathematics of it was staggering. If each death generated narrative energy proportional to its connections — to the number of biographies it touched, the number of stories it changed — then the twenty-three current victims were not twenty-three deaths. They were hundreds of narrative events, thousands of biographical modifications, a cascade of truth-power flowing through the Archive’s systems and converging on that empty tablet like rivers converging on a sea.


And twenty-three was just the beginning. The down payment. The first harvest of a crop that would be planted and replanted and harvested again and again until the tablet was full, until the biography was complete, until the story was so real that it became realer than reality.


How many deaths would it take? Naram didn’t know. Couldn’t calculate. The variables were too many, the system too complex, the relationship between narrative energy and divine manifestation too poorly understood. But he could estimate. If the other gods’ biographies had taken centuries to achieve critical mass — centuries of natural deaths, natural narrative events, the organic accumulation of truth-power through the normal course of human history — then Gilgamesh, who was accelerating the process through deliberate, targeted killings, might achieve the same result in decades. Maybe less. Maybe much less, if the killings were chosen carefully enough, if the victims were selected for maximum narrative impact, if each death was not just a death but a strategic investment in the architecture of godhood.


He turned and walked back through the Restricted Section, past the catalog room with its ash-filled registry, past the vault with its empty vial, through the service passage and into the corridors of the deep Archive. He walked fast. He had six hours of forgery left, maybe less. He needed to find allies. He needed to build a plan. He needed to do, in one day, what should have taken weeks.


But he was a scribe. And scribes, whatever else they were, were fast writers.




End of Chapter Fifteen
