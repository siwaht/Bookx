Chapter Sixteen

The Dockworker's Testimony

The woman who sold fish at the harbour had been dead for three hundred years and did not know it.

Archimedes discovered this on a Tuesday — or what passed for Tuesday on an island where the calendar was a suggestion and the days blurred into each other like watercolours left in the rain. He had gone to the harbour because he needed to think, and thinking, for Archimedes, required movement, and movement required a destination, and the harbour was the only place on the island that felt like it connected to somewhere else.

The harbour was small — a crescent of volcanic rock that formed a natural breakwater, sheltering a basin of dark water where the Roman galleys sat at anchor, their hulls crusted with barnacles and the specific, reddish residue of water that was not entirely water. The docks were wooden — planks laid across stone pilings, weathered to the colour of old bone, creaking under the weight of the few people who used them.

The fish woman was one of these people. She sat at the end of the longest dock, on an overturned crate, with a basket of fish beside her and a knife in her hand, gutting the catch with the mechanical efficiency of a person who had been doing this for a very long time. The fish were — Archimedes looked closely — wrong. Not rotten. Not diseased. Wrong in the way that everything in the Doubled City was wrong: slightly out of phase, slightly transparent, the scales catching light that was not quite the light that fell on the dock.

The woman herself was — he looked more closely — doubled. Two versions of her, occupying the same space, the same crate, the same posture. One version was solid, present, real — a woman of perhaps fifty, weathered by sun and salt, her hands scarred from a lifetime of knives and fish and the specific, repetitive labour of a trade that was older than civilisation. The other version was — thinner. Fainter. A sketch of the same woman, slightly offset, slightly transparent, visible only if you looked from the corner of your eye.

“Good morning,” Archimedes said.

The woman looked up. Both versions of her looked up — the solid one with the direct, assessing gaze of a person who was accustomed to being looked at and had decided, long ago, that the looking was the looker’s problem. The faint one with a gaze that was — Archimedes felt it in his blood — confused. Lost. The gaze of a person who was not sure where they were or when they were or which version of themselves was doing the looking.

“You’re the new one,” the solid woman said. Her voice was — Archimedes listened carefully — singular. Only one voice. The faint version’s mouth moved, but no sound came out. “The one who talks to the dead.”

“I’m a coroner.”

“Same thing, on this island.” She returned to her gutting. The knife moved with practised speed — a single cut from vent to gill, a twist of the wrist, the entrails sliding out in a wet, glistening mass that she flicked into the water with the casual accuracy of long practice. “You want fish?”

“I want to talk.”

“Talk is free. Fish costs money. I prefer fish.”

Archimedes sat on the dock beside her. The planks were warm — the blood beneath the harbour, like the blood beneath the island, generating heat that rose through the stone and the wood and the water, a constant, low-grade fever that the island could not break.

“How long have you been here?” he asked.

“Here on the dock? Since dawn. Here on the island?” She paused. The knife stopped. The solid version of her face went still, and the faint version — Archimedes watched — flickered. A moment of instability. A moment where the two versions of the woman were not quite aligned, not quite synchronised, the doubling visible as a tremor, a stutter, the visual equivalent of a skipped heartbeat.

“I don’t remember,” she said. “I’ve been here — a long time. I remember the city before it was doubled. I remember the fountain running. I remember the market — the real market, not the — the echo of it. I remember pomegranates. I remember the smell of jasmine in the evening. I remember —” She stopped again. The knife resumed. “I remember things that don’t match. Things that happened in two different ways. I remember the eruption — the mountain opening, the ash falling. And I remember the eruption not happening — the mountain staying quiet, the city continuing, the market opening the next day as if nothing had changed. Both memories are — real. Both memories are mine. And I don’t know which one is — the right one.”

“They’re both right,” Archimedes said. “You’re a duplicate. A version of yourself from a collapsed branch. The branch where the eruption didn’t happen — that was your branch. Your original timeline. It was collapsed four hundred years ago, when Danaë performed the working that saved the city. Your consciousness — your self, your memories — manifested as blood, and the blood reformed, and you’ve been here since. Living in the Doubled City. Selling fish. Carrying two sets of memories because you’re two versions of the same person, occupying the same space, unable to fully merge and unable to fully separate.”

The woman was quiet for a long time. The knife moved. The fish were gutted, one by one, with the steady rhythm of a task that did not require thought and therefore left room for thought, and the thought that was happening behind the woman’s weathered face was — Archimedes could see it — enormous. The thought of a person who has just been told the shape of their existence and is trying to fit the shape into the space where their understanding used to be.

“Three hundred years,” she said.

“Approximately.”

“I’ve been dead for three hundred years.”

“You’ve been — suspended. Not dead. Not alive. Existing in a state between the two, held in place by the mathematics of the Doubled City, anchored by the doubling itself. You’re — part of the architecture. Part of the system that keeps the city stable. Without you — without the duplicates, the people like you who hold the doubling in place — the city would collapse.”

“So I’m a — a brick. A piece of the wall.”

“You’re a person who is also a brick. Both things are true. Both things are — you.”

The woman set down her knife. She looked at her hands — the solid hands, scarred and strong, the hands of a woman who had gutted fish for three hundred years without knowing she was dead. She looked at the faint hands — the transparent, flickering hands of the version of herself that had lived in the branch that was collapsed, the version that remembered the eruption not happening, the version that was — Archimedes could see it — fading. Losing coherence. The faint version was thinner than it had been when he sat down, the outline less distinct, the features less defined.

“Am I going to disappear?” she asked.

“I don’t know.”

“Is there anything you can do?”

“I don’t know that either. I’m — working on it. The mathematics. The Proof. The calculation that might — resolve things. End the doubling. Let the city be one thing instead of two.”

“And if the city becomes one thing — which thing does it become? The version where I’m alive, or the version where I’m dead?”

The question was — Archimedes felt it in his chest, in the specific, physical location where empathy lived, the place that tightened when he was confronted with a suffering he could not fix — the question. The question that the Deathless Proof would answer. The question that Clotho and Lachesis and Atropos were all asking, in their different ways, with their different hopes, their different fears.

Which version survives?

“I don’t know,” he said. “I’m sorry. I don’t know.”

The woman picked up her knife. She resumed gutting. The fish were — Archimedes noticed — becoming more transparent. The scales were losing their shimmer. The flesh was losing its colour. The fish, like the woman, were fading — the doubled version of a catch that had been pulled from a doubled sea by a doubled woman on a doubled dock, all of it slowly, imperceptibly, losing the coherence that had sustained it for three centuries.

“My name is Myrto,” the woman said. “In case it matters. In case someone is keeping a record. My name is Myrto, and I sold fish in the harbour of Atalánta, and I had a husband named Philon and a daughter named Eirene and a cat that slept on the dock and caught more fish than I did. And I’ve been here for three hundred years, and I didn’t know I was dead, and now I know, and the knowing doesn’t change anything, but I wanted someone to know my name.”

“I’ll remember it,” Archimedes said.

“Will you? You’re a busy man. You have mathematics to solve and dead men to examine and — whatever else it is you do. Will you remember the name of a fish seller who’s been dead for three hundred years?”

“I remember every name,” Archimedes said. “Every body I’ve examined. Every person I’ve spoken for. Four thousand, six hundred and thirteen names. Yours will be four thousand, six hundred and fourteen.”

Myrto looked at him. The look was — he could not read it. Not because the expression was ambiguous but because it was too many things at once — gratitude and grief and the quiet dignity of a person who has been told they are dead and has decided to continue gutting fish because the fish need gutting and the dead, apparently, still need to eat.

“Four thousand, six hundred and fourteen,” she said. “That’s a lot of dead people.”

“Yes.”

“Do they ever talk back?”

“One of them does. A Roman soldier. He’s in the ground beneath the camp. He cheats at dice.”

Myrto almost smiled. The expression was brief — a flicker, a ghost, the memory of a smile on a face that had been smiling and frowning and expressing the full range of human emotion for three hundred years without knowing that the face was made of blood and the emotions were the echoes of a life that had ended before Rome was an empire.

“Tell him Myrto says hello,” she said. “Tell him the fish are biting.”

“I will.”

Archimedes stood. He walked back along the dock, and the planks creaked under his feet, and the water beneath the dock was dark and warm and whispering, and the fish woman sat on her crate and gutted her fading fish and did not look up, and the not-looking-up was its own kind of courage — the courage of a person who has learned something terrible about themselves and has decided to continue, because continuing was the only option, and the only option was, by definition, the best option.

He carried her name. Myrto. Four thousand, six hundred and fourteen.

He carried it the way he carried all the names — in the specific, durable, professional part of his mind that was reserved for the dead, the part that did not forget, the part that held the names and the faces and the specific, individual details of every person he had ever spoken for, because speaking for the dead was his job, and his job was the only thing he had that was entirely his, and the having was enough, and the enough was everything.

The harbour was quiet. The Roman galleys sat at anchor. The water was warm.

Somewhere beneath the dock, the blood whispered names that no one remembered.

Archimedes remembered them all.

He walked back to the camp. The water was warm behind him. The dock creaked in the tide.
