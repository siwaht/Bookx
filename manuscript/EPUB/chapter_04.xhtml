Chapter Four

The Catacomb of Selves

The entrance was a mouth.

Not metaphorically — or not only metaphorically. The stone had been carved, centuries ago, into the shape of a woman’s face, and the doorway was her open mouth, and the teeth were columns, and the tongue was a ramp of polished basalt that descended into darkness, and the expression on the face was not the serene blankness of monumental sculpture but something else, something specific: the expression of a woman who has opened her mouth to scream and forgotten how to close it.

Archimedes recognised the face. It was Danaë’s.

Or rather, it was a face that had been Danaë’s four centuries ago, before the duplications, before the rot, before the centuries of splitting and collapsing and reforming had turned one woman into three women into a system into a cage. The face in the stone was young — mid-twenties, perhaps, with the high cheekbones and strong jaw of the Atalántean aristocracy and the wide-set eyes that Archimedes had seen, in various states of decay, on the three women who now called themselves Clotho, Lachesis, and Atropos.

“She carved it herself,” Artemisia said. “After the first duplication. She wanted to remember what she looked like before.”

“Before what?”

“Before she became plural.”

They descended.

The ramp was steep, the basalt slick with moisture that was not water. Archimedes placed his feet carefully, one hand on the wall for balance, and the wall was warm — warmer than the ground above, warmer than the air, warm the way skin is warm, the way blood is warm, the way a living thing is warm when the living is still happening even though the thing has stopped.

The darkness was not complete. As they descended, a light appeared — not torchlight, not sunlight filtering through cracks, but a glow that came from the walls themselves. A faint, reddish luminescence, the colour of blood held up to a candle, the colour of a sunset seen through closed eyelids. The light pulsed. Slowly. Rhythmically. The rhythm of a heartbeat, if the heart were the size of a building and the blood it pumped were the walls and floor and ceiling of the space Archimedes was walking through.

The Catacomb was alive.

Not alive the way a person is alive — with intention, with consciousness, with the capacity for choice. Alive the way a coral reef is alive, or a forest, or a city: a system of interconnected processes, each one simple, the aggregate complex, the whole greater than the sum of its parts and also more terrible, because the parts were people.

The first bodies appeared twenty paces down.

They were set into the walls — not buried, not entombed, but embedded, the way fossils are embedded in sedimentary rock. Women. All of them women. All of them Danaë.

The nearest was young — younger than the face above the entrance, perhaps eighteen, her features still soft with the last traces of adolescence. She was naked, her body pressed into the stone as if the stone had grown around her, and her skin had the same reddish luminescence as the walls, and her eyes were open, and her mouth was open, and she was whispering.

Archimedes leaned close. The whisper was not words — not in any language he recognised. It was a sound, a vibration, a frequency that lived below speech and above silence, the sound of a consciousness that had been compressed into a body that had been compressed into a wall that had been compressed into the earth, and the compression had not killed the consciousness but had reduced it, distilled it, concentrated it into a single, continuous emission of — what? Grief? Memory? The residual charge of a life that had been lived and ended and refused to discharge?

He pulled back. The whisper followed him — not physically, not as sound, but as a sensation in his blood, a tug, a pull, the feeling of being recognised by something that shared his fundamental architecture. His blood knew this woman’s blood. His blood had been, in some branch, in some iteration, derived from the same source.

“How many?” he asked.

“In the Catacomb? Thousands.” Artemisia’s voice was steady, but her hands were not. She held them at her sides, fingers slightly curled, the posture of a person resisting the urge to touch something. Or the posture of a person resisting the urge to dissolve. “Every iteration of Danaë that died and reformed and died again. Every duplicate that was created by a working and collapsed and manifested and was brought here and — stored.”

“Stored.”

“They can’t be destroyed. The blood won’t let them. Even when the body loses coherence, the consciousness persists — in the blood, in the stone, in the earth. The Catacomb is the only place where the persistence is — contained. Managed. The walls absorb the whispers. The stone holds the shape. Without the Catacomb, the iterations would spread — into the groundwater, into the soil, into the roots of every tree and the foundation of every building. The whole island would become a single, distributed consciousness. A Danaë the size of a landscape.”

Archimedes looked down the corridor. It stretched beyond the reach of the reddish light, descending further into the earth, and on both sides, the walls were full of women. Hundreds of them, visible in the glow, their bodies pressed into the stone at regular intervals like specimens in a collection, each one a different age, a different iteration, a different version of the same woman who had cut her palm all those years ago and asked the blood to remember a better world.

Some were old. Some were young. Some were barely formed — shapes that suggested a body without committing to one, the outline of a woman rendered in blood and stone, the features blurred, the limbs indistinct, as if the iteration had been created and collapsed so quickly that it never had time to fully become a person.

Some were screaming. Silently, perpetually, their mouths open, their faces contorted, frozen in the moment of manifestation — the moment when a consciousness that had been alive in a parallel branch found itself suddenly, violently, impossibly here, in a body made of blood, in a wall made of stone, in a darkness that would last forever.

Archimedes counted as he walked. Ten. Twenty. Fifty. A hundred. The corridor turned, descended, turned again. More walls. More women. More whispers. The air was thick with them — not sound but presence, the accumulated weight of thousands of consciousnesses pressing against the boundaries of their containment, each one a complete person, each one aware, each one trapped in the private, individual hell of knowing exactly what they were and being unable to change it.

He stopped counting at three hundred. The corridor kept going.

“Four hundred years,” he said. “Four hundred years of this.”

“Yes.”

“And the Triarchs — the three who remain — they come here?”

“Clotho comes every day. She sits with them. She talks to them. She apologises.” Artemisia paused. “Lachesis hasn’t been down here in a century. She says the Catacomb is a monument to failure, and she doesn’t believe in monuments.”

“And Atropos?”

“Atropos lives here.”

They found her in the deepest chamber.

The corridor ended in a room — circular, domed, the ceiling lost in darkness above. The walls were not stone here but something else: a substance that was part mineral, part organic, part liquid, a material that had no name because it had no precedent. It was the colour of old blood — not red but brown-black, the colour of blood that has been exposed to air for a very long time — and it moved. Slowly. A glacial flow, a tectonic creep, the walls breathing inward and outward with the same slow rhythm as the light in the corridor above.

The floor was wet. Not with water. With blood — thin, dilute, the consistency of weak tea, spreading in a film across the stone and pooling in the low places. The blood was warm. The blood was whispering. The blood was, Archimedes realised, Atropos.

She sat in the centre of the room, on a chair that was not a chair but a formation of the same blood-stone that made up the walls — a growth, an extrusion, shaped by centuries of occupation into something that approximated furniture. She was small. Smaller than he had expected. The word ancient had prepared him for something imposing — a crone, a matriarch, a figure of terrible dignity. What he found was a woman who looked like she had been left out in the rain.

Her body was — the word that came to mind was leaking. She sat very still, her hands in her lap, her back straight, her chin lifted with the careful posture of a person who knows that if they relax, something important will come apart. And from every surface of her body — her hands, her arms, her neck, the thin skin of her temples — blood seeped. Not flowing. Seeping. The slow, continuous exudation of a body that could no longer maintain the boundary between inside and outside, between self and medium, between the person and the substance the person was made of.

She was held together by wire. Archimedes could see it — thin copper wire, wound around her fingers, her wrists, her forearms, binding the flesh in place the way you bind a cracked pot with twine. The wire was tarnished, green with verdigris, and where it pressed into her skin, the skin had grown around it, incorporating the metal into the tissue, making it part of the body it was meant to contain.

Her face was the face from the entrance — Danaë’s face, the same bones, the same structure — but collapsed inward, the skin too thin, the cheekbones too prominent, the eyes too large in their sockets. She looked like a woman who had been beautiful and then been emptied, the beauty still present in the architecture but absent in the substance, the way a cathedral is still a cathedral after the roof falls in.

She was looking at him.

“You’re smaller than I expected,” she said.

Her voice was dry. Not weak — dry. The voice of a woman who had been talking for a very long time and had used up most of the moisture in her throat and was now speaking with whatever was left, which was not much, but was enough.

“I get that a lot,” Archimedes said.

“Sit.”

There was no second chair. He sat on the floor. The blood-film soaked into the seat of his tunic immediately, warm and thin, and he felt it — the whisper, the tug, the recognition. His blood responding to hers. Or rather, his blood responding to the information in hers, the four-hundred-year archive of probability and death and the terrible mathematics of a woman who had refused to die and been punished for the refusal with an existence that was worse than death because it was death, distributed, continuous, aware.

“You’ve seen the soldier,” Atropos said.

“I’ve examined him.”

“And?”

“Hematic suspension. The working that killed him didn’t complete. He’s trapped between states — not alive, not dead, not fully manifested. His consciousness is in the blood, in the earth beneath the Roman camp. He’s aware. He’s suffering.”

Atropos nodded. The motion was careful — she moved the way a person moves when they’re carrying a full cup, every gesture calibrated to avoid spilling. “The working was Lachesis’s. She was testing a new technique — using adopted lineages instead of her own blood. The theory was sound. The execution was not.”

“Adopted lineages?”

“The soldier was adopted into our bloodline. A ritual — blood exchange, fate-linking. It made him part of our probability map. When Lachesis performed the working, she used his branches instead of her own. His duplicates died. His blood manifested. But the adoption was incomplete — the soldier’s blood didn’t fully integrate with ours, and the collapse stalled. He’s stuck because the mathematics don’t recognise him as fully ours and can’t finish the equation.”

Archimedes processed this. The implications were — he searched for a word that was adequate and found none, because the implications were beyond the vocabulary of horror he had developed over twenty-three years of examining the dead.

“She used him as fuel,” he said.

“Yes.”

“She created duplicates of him in parallel branches and collapsed them to power a working.”

“Yes.”

“And the duplicates — the versions of Nerva who died in those branches — they manifested as blood. Sentient blood. Blood that remembered being him.”

“Yes.”

“And the working failed, so the blood is still there, still aware, still trying to be him.”

“Yes.”

“And Lachesis considers this an acceptable outcome.”

Atropos looked at him with eyes that were the colour of old wine — not the bright ruby of a young vintage but the deep, murky brown of something that had been in the bottle too long, that had oxidised, that had turned from pleasure into something more complex and less pleasant.

“Lachesis considers this a learning opportunity,” she said. “She will refine the technique. She will try again. She will keep trying until she can use adopted lineages as efficiently as her own blood, and when she can, she will adopt entire populations — cities, nations, the whole of the Mediterranean — and use them as fuel for workings so vast they will reshape the probability map of the entire world.”

“To what end?”

“To the end she has always pursued. Infinite branching. A universe where every possible version of every person exists simultaneously. No death, because every death creates a new branch where the person lives. No loss, because every loss is compensated by a gain in a parallel timeline. A perfect, infinite, self-sustaining system of existence where suffering is just — weather. A local phenomenon. Temporary. Meaningless.”

“That’s insane.”

“That’s Lachesis.” Atropos shifted in her chair. The wire around her wrists creaked. A drop of blood fell from her fingertip to the floor, and where it landed, the blood-film rippled outward in concentric circles, and the ripples carried whispers, and the whispers carried names. “She is not insane. She is logical. Her logic is simply operating from premises that are monstrous, and the conclusions she draws from those premises are — consistent. Internally consistent. That is what makes her dangerous. She is not wrong in her reasoning. She is wrong in her assumptions. And she cannot see the difference because the assumptions are centuries old and have become load-bearing.”

Archimedes looked at the walls. The blood-stone breathed. The whispers continued — thousands of them, layered, overlapping, a chorus of the dead-who-were-not-dead, the erased-who-remembered, the collapsed-who-persisted.

“And Clotho?” he asked. “What does Clotho want?”

Atropos was quiet for a moment. The quiet had texture — it was not the absence of sound but the presence of consideration, the specific silence of a person choosing their words with the care of someone who has learned, over centuries, that words have consequences and consequences have costs and costs are always paid in blood.

“Clotho wants to go back,” she said. “To the beginning. To the moment she cut her palm and saved her son. She wants to undo it. She wants to let the boy die, let Atalánta fall, let the eruption take everything. She wants to unwrite four hundred years of horror by removing the first act that made them possible.”

“The Deathless Proof.”

“The Deathless Proof would allow it. If solved, it would collapse every iteration of Danaë — every duplicate, every branch-self, every version of her that exists across every timeline — into a single point. One Danaë. One death. One ending. Clotho would use it to go back to the original branch, the one where she died and her son died and Atalánta fell, and she would let that branch be the real one, and all of this —” Atropos gestured at the Catacomb, at the walls, at herself. “All of this would never have happened.”

“That’s not how it works,” Archimedes said. “You can’t unwrite the past. The branches that were created, the people who lived in them, the blood that was spent — that’s real. That happened. You can’t make it un-happen.”

“I know,” Atropos said. “Clotho knows too, somewhere beneath the guilt. But guilt is not rational, and Clotho has had four hundred years to build a theology of self-punishment so elaborate that it has become indistinguishable from a plan.”

“And you? What do you want?”

Atropos looked at him. The look was long, steady, the look of a woman who has had centuries to consider her answer and has arrived at one that is simple and terrible and true.

“I want to stop,” she said. “I want to be the last Danaë. I want to choose one timeline — this one, the one we’re in, the one where Atalánta survived and the Catacomb exists and the blood whispers and the city is doubled and everything is broken and wrong and real — and I want to let all the others go. Collapse them. End them. Let the dead be dead. Let the blood be blood. Let the whispers stop.”

“That would kill you.”

“I am already dying. I have been dying for a hundred years. My body is forty percent blood — not blood doing the work of blood, but blood that has lost its allegiance to my body and is trying to return to the earth, to the Catacomb, to the collective. I am held together by wire and mathematics and the specific, stubborn refusal to dissolve that is the only inheritance I received from the woman I used to be.” She paused. “I do not want to be saved. I want to be finished.”

The word finished hung in the air of the Catacomb like a note struck on a bell — clear, resonant, fading slowly into the whispers that surrounded it.

Archimedes understood. He understood with the clarity of a man who had spent seven years studying the mechanics of a system designed to prevent exactly this: the ending. The closure. The singular, irreversible, permanent death that was the one thing métronurgy could not produce, because métronurgy’s fundamental operation was the creation of alternatives, and alternatives were the enemy of endings, and endings were the only thing that made a life a life instead of a process.

“The Deathless Proof,” he said. “You want me to solve it.”

“I want you to understand it. Solving it is your choice. But understanding it — seeing what it means, what it costs, what it makes possible — that is necessary. Because Clotho will ask you to solve it for her reasons, and Lachesis will try to prevent you from solving it for her reasons, and I am asking you to solve it for mine, and the three reasons are incompatible, and the incompatibility is the point.”

“The point of what?”

“The point of you being here. The point of the soldier. The point of everything that is about to happen.” Atropos leaned forward. The wire creaked. Blood seeped from the corners of her eyes — not weeping, but the simple mechanical failure of a body that could no longer contain itself. “You are a coroner, Archimedes. You speak for the dead. But the dead here are not dead — they are suspended, distributed, aware. They are in the walls and the earth and the blood and the rain. They are everywhere and they are suffering and they have been suffering all this time and no one has spoken for them because no one has understood what they are.”

She reached out. Her hand — wire-bound, blood-seeping, the fingers swollen and discoloured — touched his. The contact was electric. Not painful — electric. A surge of information, of memory, of the accumulated weight of a hundred years of dying without death, passing from her blood to his through the thin membrane of skin that separated them.

He saw — not with his eyes but with his blood — the Catacomb as she saw it. Not a tomb. Not a prison. A library. A record. Centuries of Danaës, each one a complete person, each one carrying a complete set of memories and experiences and the individual perspective of a consciousness that had lived and died and refused to stop. The whispers were not grief — or not only grief. They were testimony. They were the voices of the dead, speaking their truths into the stone, preserving themselves in the only medium that would hold them.

And they were waiting. Waiting for someone to listen. Waiting for someone to understand. Waiting for someone to do the mathematics that would either free them or end them, and they did not care which, because freedom and ending were, after four hundred years, the same thing.

Atropos released his hand. The vision faded. The Catacomb was just a Catacomb again — dark, warm, whispering, full of the dead who were not dead.

“Will you help?” she asked.

Archimedes looked at her — this ancient, leaking, wire-bound woman who wanted nothing more than to stop existing and could not, because the mathematics that sustained her were four hundred years deep and the only person who could solve them was a fifty-three-year-old coroner from Syracuse who had come to this island not to save anyone but to find out why his wife had been murdered.

“I’ll think about it,” he said.

Atropos almost smiled. The expression was brief, barely there, the ghost of an expression on a face that had forgotten how to make them. “That’s more than anyone has said in a century.”

“I’m not promising anything.”

“I know. Promises are branches. I’ve had enough of branches.”

She settled back in her chair. The blood-film on the floor rippled. The whispers rose and fell, a tide of voices, a sea of the almost-dead, and Archimedes sat in the middle of it and felt his blood hum with the resonance of four hundred years of suffering, and he thought about mathematics, and he thought about his wife, and he thought about the woman walking above him in the Doubled City who was made of blood and will and the refusal to dissolve, and he thought about the soldier in the ground who was screaming a name that no one could hear.

He thought about all of these things, and he did not solve anything, and the not-solving was, for now, enough.

He stood. His knees protested. His back protested. His blood protested — it wanted to stay, wanted to join the whispers, wanted to add its voice to the chorus of the almost-dead. He ignored it. He had been ignoring his blood for years. It was a habit, like breathing, like grief, like the specific, daily act of choosing to continue.

“I’ll come back,” he said.

“I know,” Atropos said. “They all come back. The Catacomb is patient.”

He climbed the ramp. He passed the embedded women, the whispering walls, the pulsing light. He emerged through the mouth of the carved Danaë into the doubled sunlight of the Doubled City, and the air was warm, and the sky was the colour of a bruise, and somewhere above him, the Cauldron — the volcano that erupted backwards — was exhaling a thin column of ash that rose into the atmosphere and carried with it the compressed remains of timelines that had never existed and people who had never lived and deaths that had never happened.

Artemisia was waiting.

“Well?” she said.

“I need to think.”

“About what?”

“About everything.”

She nodded. She did not press. She understood — because she was him, because she carried his capacity for silence the way she carried his jaw and his cheekbones and his habit of pressing his knuckles into the small of his back when his spine complained — that thinking, for Archimedes, was not a passive state but an active one, a process as physical as walking, as demanding as surgery, as necessary as breathing.

They walked back through the Doubled City in silence. The wrong-shadowed woman was still grinding ash. The children were still laughing before their mouths opened. The buildings were still doubled, still shimmering, still unable to decide which version of themselves to be.

Archimedes walked through all of it and saw none of it and thought about the Deathless Proof and did not solve it and did not try to solve it and knew, with the certainty of a man who has spent his life among the dead, that the solving would come when it came, and the coming would cost what it cost, and the cost would be paid in blood, because the cost was always paid in blood, and the blood always remembered.

He pressed his knuckles into the small of his back. He kept walking.
