Chapter Three

The Doubled City

The path from the Roman camp to the interior of the island climbed through a landscape that could not decide what it was.

Archimedes had seen volcanic terrain before — the slopes of Etna, the pumice fields of Lipari, the black glass beaches of Pantelleria where the obsidian caught the light and threw it back in colours that didn’t exist in the visible spectrum. Volcanic land had a personality: violent, fertile, the kind of ground that killed you and fed you in the same season, the geological equivalent of a parent who hit you and then made you dinner.

This was different.

The rock here was basalt, but it was wrong. Not in composition — he had tested it, scratching the surface with his knife, checking the grain, the weight, the way it fractured. Basalt. Ordinary basalt. But the formations were impossible. Columns of stone that twisted like wrung cloth. Boulders balanced on points so fine they should have toppled in the first breeze. A ridge of rock that ran for a hundred paces in a perfectly straight line, then turned at a right angle, then turned again, forming a shape that was, from above, unmistakably a letter — not Greek, not any alphabet Archimedes recognised, but a letter nonetheless, a symbol carved into the landscape by forces that were either geological or intentional and the distinction, here, seemed academic.

The vegetation was worse. Olive trees grew from cracks in the basalt, their trunks silver-grey, their leaves the dusty green of trees that have learned to survive on almost nothing. But some of them were doubled — two trunks occupying the same space, slightly out of phase, like a painting where the artist’s hand had trembled. If you looked at them straight on, you saw one tree. If you looked from the corner of your eye, you saw two, overlapping, each one casting a shadow that didn’t quite match the other.

Archimedes kept his eyes forward and walked.

Artemisia walked beside him, three paces to his left, matching his stride with the unconscious precision of a person who shared his gait because she shared his legs — or the memory of his legs, or the probability of his legs, or whatever it was that a duplicate carried of the original when the original was a body and the duplicate was blood that had learned to pretend.

They did not speak. The path was steep, and Archimedes’ knees were fifty-three years old, and the altitude was doing something to his breathing that he chose to attribute to the thin air rather than to the fact that he had not climbed a hill in two years and his body was reminding him, with the passive aggression of a neglected instrument, that disuse had consequences.

The Doubled City appeared gradually, the way a headache appears — not all at once but in increments, each one slightly worse than the last, until you realised you had been in pain for some time and the realisation itself was a kind of additional pain.

First: the walls. They rose from the ridge above the path, massive, cyclopean, built from blocks of stone so large that the engineering required to place them was either superhuman or supernatural and the distinction, again, seemed academic. The walls were intact — centuries of weather and neglect and the specific geological hostility of a volcanic island had not cracked them, had not shifted them, had not done anything except darken the stone to the colour of dried blood.

The walls were doubled.

Not two walls side by side. One wall, existing in two states simultaneously. If Archimedes focused — really focused, narrowing his attention to a single block of stone — he could see it: the block was in two places at once, separated by a distance so small it was measured not in inches but in intention. One version of the block was slightly to the left. The other was slightly to the right. The difference was perhaps a finger’s width. The effect was nauseating — a visual vibration, a shimmer that was not light but displacement, the optical signature of a reality that had been split and imperfectly rejoined.

“Don’t stare at it,” Artemisia said.

“I’m not staring.”

“You’re staring. Your left eye is twitching. That’s what happens when you stare at the doubling.”

He looked away. She was right — his left eye was twitching, a rapid, involuntary flutter of the eyelid that he associated with eyestrain but that was, he suspected, something else. His blood responding to the doubling. His blood recognising, in the split stone, the same principle that governed its own existence: the principle of a thing that was one thing and two things at the same time, held together by mathematics and will and the refusal to choose.

They passed through the gate.

The gate was a single arch — or two arches, overlapping — wide enough for a cart, tall enough for a man on horseback, carved with symbols that Archimedes did not recognise but that his blood did, because when he passed beneath them his veins hummed, a low vibration that started in his wrists and spread upward through his arms to his chest, where it settled into a rhythm that was not his heartbeat but was synchronised with it, a second pulse layered over the first like a harmony over a melody.

Inside the walls, the Doubled City opened like a wound.

It had been beautiful once. Archimedes could see that — could see it the way you see the face of a person who was beautiful before the illness, before the accident, before the thing that changed them. The bones were still there. The architecture was still there. Wide streets paved with fitted stone. Buildings of white limestone, two and three stories tall, with balconies and courtyards and the remains of gardens where jasmine and bougainvillea had once grown in terracotta pots. A central square with a fountain — dry now, the basin cracked, but the proportions were perfect, the kind of proportions that spoke of a civilisation that understood mathematics not as a tool but as an aesthetic, a way of making the world rhyme with itself.

But everything was doubled.

Every building existed in two states. Every street had two versions, slightly offset, like a photograph taken with a shaking hand. The fountain was two fountains, occupying the same space, one version a few degrees rotated from the other so that the basin appeared to have eight sides instead of four. The paving stones were two sets of paving stones, and walking on them felt like walking on a surface that was constantly shifting underfoot — not moving, exactly, but uncertain, as if the ground were perpetually deciding which version of itself to be and never quite committing.

And there were people.

Not many. Perhaps a hundred, scattered through the streets and squares and doorways of the Doubled City, going about the business of living in a place that was not entirely real. They were — Archimedes searched for the word and found it in the vocabulary of his profession — wrong. Not deformed. Not diseased. Wrong in the way that a reflection is wrong — reversed, slightly off, recognisably human but recognisably not-quite.

A woman sat in a doorway, grinding grain in a stone mortar. She was solid, present, real — except that her shadow fell in the wrong direction, pointing toward the sun instead of away from it. A man carried water from a well, his movements fluid and purposeful — except that the water in his bucket was not water but something thicker, darker, and it moved against the motion of the bucket rather than with it. Two children played in the square, chasing each other around the doubled fountain, laughing — except that the laughter arrived a half-second before their mouths opened, as if the sound were running ahead of the action that produced it.

“Duplicates,” Artemisia said quietly. “People from collapsed branches who didn’t fully dissolve. They’re — stuck. Like the city.”

“How long have they been here?”

“Some of them, centuries. The oldest ones are from the original collapse — the working Danaë performed to save Atalánta. They’ve been here since the beginning, living in the doubled version of the city, going through the motions of lives that ended four hundred years ago.”

Archimedes watched the woman grinding grain. Her hands moved with the mechanical rhythm of long practice, the pestle rising and falling in the mortar with a regularity that was almost hypnotic. But the grain was not grain. It was ash — fine, grey, the same ash that fell upward from the Cauldron — and the woman was grinding it with the focused attention of someone who believed, or had once believed, or was performing the memory of believing, that the grinding would produce flour.

“Do they know?” he asked. “That they’re duplicates?”

“Some do. Some don’t. Some know and pretend they don’t, because the pretending is easier than the knowing. Some knew once and forgot, because forgetting is what happens when you exist in a state that was never meant to be permanent.”

“And the Triarchs allow this?”

Artemisia’s mouth compressed. “The Triarchs need this. The duplicates are — anchors. They hold the doubling in place. Without them, the city would collapse into a single state, and the collapse would release four hundred years of accumulated branch-energy, and the release would —” She paused. “It would be bad.”

“How bad?”

“Red Rain bad. Except not rain. A flood. Every duplicate in the city, every branch-consciousness held in suspension, all of them collapsing at once, manifesting as blood. The city would drown in it.”

Archimedes absorbed this. He looked at the doubled buildings, the wrong-shadowed woman, the children whose laughter preceded their mouths, and he understood — with the cold, clinical clarity that was his gift and his curse — that he was standing in a graveyard. Not a graveyard of the dead but a graveyard of the almost-dead, the not-quite-dead, the dead-who-didn’t-know-they-were-dead. A city of ghosts who were not ghosts because ghosts were memories and these were people, real people, conscious people, trapped in a version of reality that had been collapsed around them like a building falling in slow motion, and they were still inside, and the falling had been going on that long, and it would go on forever unless someone solved the equation that would let it stop.

The Deathless Proof.

He understood, now, why the Triarchs wanted it solved. Not just for themselves — for this. For the hundred souls in the Doubled City, and the thousands more in the earth beneath the Archipelago, and the tens of thousands whose blood had been spent over four centuries of workings, all of them suspended, all of them aware, all of them waiting for an ending that the mathematics had not yet provided.

“Where are they?” he asked. “The Triarchs.”

“The Catacomb,” Artemisia said. “Below the city. Where they keep their dead.”

“Their dead.”

“Their other selves. The iterations that didn’t survive. Four hundred years of Danaës, stacked and preserved and — whispering.” She looked at him, and in her eyes was something he recognised because it was the same thing he saw in his own eyes every morning: the specific, exhausting terror of a person who knows exactly what they are and cannot stop being it. “You don’t have to go down there.”

“Yes I do.”

“You could leave. Take a boat. Go back to Syracuse. Forget the soldier, forget the Triarchs, forget —” She stopped. The word she didn’t say hung between them like smoke.

Me.

Forget me. That was what she meant. Leave the Archipelago, leave the Doubled City, leave the woman who was not his sister but his duplicate, the blood-made-flesh who existed because he existed, who would dissolve if the mathematics that sustained her were ever resolved, who was, in the most literal sense possible, the cost of his survival made into a person.

“I’m not leaving,” he said.

“Why not?”

Because of Elara. Because the Triarchs had killed his wife — had pruned her from every branch, had erased her from the probability map with the thoroughness of someone erasing a mistake from a ledger — and he needed to know why. Because the why was the only thing left, the last calculation, the final entry in the ledger of a marriage that had ended not with a death but with an absence so total it felt like the universe had been edited.

Because of Artemisia. Because she was him, and he was her, and the bond between them was not love in any sense he could name but was something deeper, something structural, something that lived in the blood they shared and the probability they had split and the impossible, tender, horrifying fact that she was alive because a version of her had died, and he was alive because a version of him had died, and they were each other’s cost, each other’s debt, each other’s proof that survival was never free.

Because of the soldier. Because Lucius Nerva, twenty-four, from Brundisium, good with a pilum, bad at dice, was in the ground beneath the Roman camp, suspended between states, aware and suffering, and Archimedes was a coroner, and a coroner’s job was to speak for the dead, and Nerva was not dead, not fully, and someone needed to speak for him.

He said none of this. He said: “Because I have work to do.”

Artemisia looked at him for a long moment. Then she almost smiled — barely, not fully, the expression stopping just short of completion the way everything about her stopped just short of completion, held back by the fundamental incompleteness of a being that was neither alive nor dead nor anything except itself.

“You always have work to do,” she said.

“Yes.”

“It’s how you avoid everything else.”

“Yes.”

“Is it working?”

He considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. “No,” he said. “But it’s the best I have.”

She nodded. She understood. She was, after all, him — or the memory of him, or the possibility of him, or the version of him that had taken a different shape and learned to hold it. She understood avoidance the way she understood breathing: as a necessary function, performed continuously, noticed only when it failed.

“The Catacomb is this way,” she said, and turned, and walked into the Doubled City, and Archimedes followed, and the doubled buildings rose around them like the walls of a maze that had been designed by someone who believed that the way out was the same as the way in, and the only difference was which version of the path you chose to walk.

The ground beneath their feet was warm.

It was always warm, here. The blood beneath the city — centuries of it, layered and compressed and whispering — generated heat the way a compost heap generates heat: through the slow, continuous process of something that was not quite alive and not quite dead breaking down into something that was neither.

Archimedes walked on the warm ground and listened to the whispers and did not look at the doubled buildings and did not think about his wife and did not think about the soldier and did not think about the woman walking beside him who was made of blood and will and the stubborn refusal to dissolve.

He thought about mathematics.

Mathematics was clean. Mathematics was honest. It did not pretend to be something it was not, did not hide its costs in the spaces between its terms, did not ask you to love it while it killed the people you loved.

The Catacomb waited below. The dead waited inside it. They had been waiting for a very long time.
