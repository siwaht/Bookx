Chapter Twenty-Five

What Decimus Carried

Between the Epilogue sections — after the Romans depart

Decimus stood at the stern of the last galley and watched the island disappear.

It did not disappear the way islands usually disappeared — gradually, the outline softening, the details dissolving into distance until the island was just a shape on the horizon and then just a memory of a shape. This island disappeared differently. It seemed to recede not in space but in probability, as if the distance between the galley and the Archipelago were not measured in miles but in the likelihood of the island being real. The further they sailed, the less certain the island seemed. The less certain, the less visible. By the time they had been at sea for an hour, the Archipelago of Ash was not a shape on the horizon but a quality of the light — a reddish tinge, a warmth in the air that should not have been there, a specific, persistent feeling that the world behind them was different from the world ahead.

Decimus kept his hand in his pocket.

The dice were warm. They were always warm now — not the warmth of a man’s hand, not the warmth of cloth and friction and the ordinary heat of a body carrying an object. A different warmth. The warmth of blood. The warmth of a consciousness that had been compressed into the medium of the dice the way a message is compressed into ink, present in every molecule, distributed through the material, carried.

You’re going to drop them, said the voice in his blood.

“I’m not going to drop them.”

You’ve been holding them for three hours. Your hand is cramping.

“My hand is fine.”

Your hand is not fine. I can feel your hand. Your hand is cramping.

Decimus shifted his grip. The dice settled into the new position. The warmth redistributed itself, finding the new contact points, the new surfaces, the new places where the bone of his fingers pressed against the bone of the dice and the warmth passed between them.

“Better?” he asked.

Better.

This was what it was like now. This was what the coroner had meant when he said Nerva would be with him — not beside him, not in the ground beneath his feet, but in him. In his blood. In a distributed way that a consciousness could exist in a medium that was not a body, carried in the blood of a person who had been part of the same lineage, the same adoption, the same improbable chain of events that had linked a Roman soldier to a blood-made woman to a coroner from Syracuse to a four-hundred-year-old queen who had broken the world with love.

It was strange. It was, Decimus thought, the strangest thing that had ever happened to him, and he had spent forty-seven days on an island where the rain was red and the ground was warm and the dead talked from beneath the earth.

But it was also — not bad. Not the horror he had expected. Not the claustrophobic terror of a man who has had something inserted into his consciousness without his consent. It was more like — company. The comfortable company of a person you know well enough that their presence does not require explanation or performance. The company of a man who cheated at dice and lost and was generous about it, who complained about the food and the weather and the quality of the local wine, who had a mother in Brundisium with red hands from the dye works and a girl he had left behind whose name he had finally told Decimus, on the last night on the island, in the blood, in the dark.

Her name was Lucia.

You should write to her, Nerva said.

“I know.”

You’ve been saying you know for three days.

“I’ll write to her when we reach port.”

What will you say?

Decimus thought about this. He thought about it the way he thought about difficult things — slowly, with methodical patience of a man who had learned that rushing toward hard questions produced bad answers. He thought about what you said to a woman whose man had died in a way that could not be explained, in a place that could not be named, for reasons that could not be described without sounding like the ravings of a man who had been too long at sea.

“I’ll say he was brave,” Decimus said. “I’ll say he died doing his duty. I’ll say he was the best man I knew and the worst dice player I ever met and that the century is poorer for his absence.”

That’s all true.

“I know.”

You could also tell her about the lentils.

“The lentils.”

The last meal. The good lentils. Tell her he ate good lentils on his last night and that the cook was wasted on the army and that he complained about the wine but drank it anyway because complaining about wine and drinking it anyway was the most Roman thing he knew how to do.

Decimus almost smiled. The almost-smile was becoming a habit — the specific, involuntary contraction of the muscles around his mouth that happened when Nerva said something that was both funny and true, which was most of what Nerva said, which was most of what Nerva had always said, which was the thing Decimus had missed most in the days between the death and the braiding, the specific, irreplaceable quality of a person who found the world funny and was not afraid to say so.

“I’ll tell her about the lentils,” he said.

Good. She’ll like that. She likes food. She’s a good cook herself. Better than Macro, probably, though don’t tell Macro I said that.

“Macro can’t hear you.”

He might. I’m not entirely sure how this works.

“Neither am I.”

The coroner would know.

“The coroner is on the island.”

Right. A pause. The warmth in the dice shifted — not physically, not in any way that Decimus could have described to someone who had not felt it, but perceptibly, the way the quality of light shifts when a cloud passes over the sun. Do you think he’s all right? The coroner. And the woman.

“Artemisia.”

Yes. Do you think they’re all right?

Decimus thought about the last time he had seen them — standing on the beach, side by side, closer than two people usually stood, the visible intimacy of two people who shared a blood and a consciousness and a history that included birth and death and the impossible fact of a woman made of blood who had learned to hold a shape. He thought about the way they had spoken in unison — his voice and her voice, overlapping, harmonising, the two-note chord of a self that was two selves and one self and both at once.

“I think they’re different,” he said. “I don’t know if different is the same as all right.”

Different can be all right.

“Can it?”

I’m different. I’m in your blood and I’m in the dice and I’m in the blood of everyone who was part of the lineage and I’m — distributed. Spread across the medium. Present everywhere, concentrated nowhere. A pause. It’s not what I expected death to be.

“What did you expect?”

Nothing. I expected nothing. The way sleep is nothing — you close your eyes and then you open them and time has passed and you don’t know how much. I expected that. Another pause. This is better than nothing. This is — something. It’s strange and it’s not what I would have chosen and I miss having a body, I miss the specific, physical reality of being a person who has a body, I miss the weight of it and the warmth of it and the specific, undignified inconvenience of it. But I’m here. I’m still here. And you’re here. And the dice are warm.

“The dice are warm,” Decimus agreed.

That’s enough. For now, that’s enough.

The island was gone. The horizon was empty — just sea and sky, the grey-blue expanse of the Mediterranean in the early morning, the light flat and even and carrying no particular quality except the quality of being ordinary. The ordinary light of an ordinary sea on an ordinary morning.

Decimus stood at the stern and looked at the empty horizon and felt the warmth in his pocket and thought about Lucia and the letter he would write and the lentils he would describe and the man he would try to make real for a woman who had loved him and would never know exactly how he had died or exactly what he had become.

He would tell her the true things. The dice. The complaints. The generosity. The specific, irreducible fact of a man who had been alive and was now something else, something that had no name because it had no precedent, something that was not death and not life but a third thing, a new thing, a thing that the world had not had a word for until forty-seven days ago on an island where the rain was red and the ground was warm and a coroner from Syracuse had solved a mathematics problem that changed the nature of what it meant to be a person.

He would tell her the true things, and the true things would not be enough, and the not-enough was the whole of it, and the whole of it was what he had, and having it was better than having nothing.

Decimus.

“Yes.”

Thank you. For staying. For the dice. For talking to me when I was in the ground.

“You would have done the same.”

I would have complained more.

“You would have complained constantly.”

Yes. But I would have stayed.

“I know.”

The galley moved through the grey water. The oars rose and fell. The wake closed behind them like a wound healing. The island was gone, and the blood was warm, and the dice were warm, and somewhere in the distributed consciousness of a lineage that was dissolving as the distance grew, a young man from Brundisium who had been good with a pilum and bad at dice was carrying the irreducible weight of a life that had been lived and was not, quite, over.

Decimus kept his hand in his pocket.

He kept it there all the way to port.

He kept it there when he wrote the letter.

He kept it there for the rest of his life — not always, not every moment, but often enough that the warmth became familiar, became part of the texture of his days, became the specific, persistent reminder that the dead were not gone, that the gone were not absent, that the people we loved were carried in us the way blood was carried in the body: continuously, invisibly, doing the work of keeping us alive.

He kept the dice.

He never played with them.

Some things were too honest to gamble with.
