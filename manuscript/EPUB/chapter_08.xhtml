Chapter Eight

The Soldier's Watch

Decimus could hear the dead man talking.

This was not, in his experience, normal. Decimus had been a legionary for eleven years, and in that time he had seen men die in a variety of ways — sword, spear, disease, the specific and undignified death that came from eating bad oysters in Brundisium — and none of them had continued speaking afterward. Death, in Decimus’s understanding, was a boundary. You crossed it. You did not come back. You did not whisper from beneath the ground at three in the morning while a man was trying to take a piss.

But Nerva was whispering.

Decimus stood at the latrine ditch — a trench dug at the camp’s eastern perimeter, lined with lime, covered with a plank that served as both seat and dignity — and he listened. The whisper came from below. Not from the trench itself — which contained only the expected contents of a military latrine — but from deeper, from the earth beneath, from the layer of volcanic rock and compressed ash and whatever else made up the geology of this godforsaken island.

The whisper said his name.

Decimus.

Not loudly. Not urgently. The way you say a friend’s name when you want their attention but don’t want to startle them. The way Nerva had said it, a hundred times, across a hundred watches, when he wanted to borrow Decimus’s whetstone or complain about the food or ask whether Decimus thought the stars looked wrong tonight.

Decimus. Can you hear me?

“I can hear you,” Decimus said, and immediately felt foolish, because he was talking to a latrine trench, and if anyone from the century saw him, the jokes would last longer than the campaign.

I’m cold.

“You’re dead.”

I know. I’m still cold.

Decimus finished his business. He stood. He looked down at the trench, at the dark earth beyond the lime, at the place where the whisper seemed to originate — a spot about two feet below the surface, slightly to the left of the plank, where the soil was darker than the surrounding earth. Not wet. Dark. The darkness of blood that had soaked into volcanic rock and been absorbed, the way ink is absorbed by parchment, becoming part of the medium, inseparable, permanent.

“Nerva,” he said. “What happened to you?”

I don’t know. I was sleeping. Then I was — somewhere else. A place that looked like the camp but wasn’t. Everything was the same but — thinner. Like a painting of the camp instead of the camp. And there was a woman. Young. She looked at me and she said something I didn’t understand and then I was — here. In the ground. In the dark. And I can feel the earth and I can feel the rock and I can feel the worms and I can feel you, Decimus, I can feel your feet on the ground above me, and your feet are warm, and the warmth is the only thing I can feel that isn’t cold.

Decimus sat down on the plank. He sat there for a long time, in the dark, at the edge of the camp, listening to his dead friend talk from beneath the earth, and he did not cry, because legionaries did not cry, and he did not scream, because screaming would bring the watch, and he did not pray, because the gods had clearly lost interest in this particular corner of the world.

He sat, and he listened, and the listening was the only thing he could offer, and the offering was not enough, and the not-enough was the whole of it.

In the morning, Decimus went to find the coroner.

He found him in the examination tent, sitting on the ground beside the table where Nerva’s body lay, writing in a notation that looked like no language Decimus had ever seen. The coroner looked up when Decimus entered. His eyes were red — not from crying but from the specific redness of a man who had not slept and whose body was expressing its displeasure through the medium of inflamed conjunctivae.

“You’re Decimus,” the coroner said.

“Yes.”

“The praetor said you could hear the blood. The blood in the ground. Nerva’s blood.”

“It’s not just blood. It’s him. He talks. He says my name. He says he’s cold.”

The coroner set down his pen. He looked at Decimus with an attention that was — Decimus searched for the word — clinical. Clinical. Not cold, not unkind, but precise — the attention of a man looking at Decimus as a source of information, a witness, a data point in a calculation Decimus could not see.

“When did it start?” the coroner asked.

“The night he died. The watch found him — I was on the watch, I was the one who found him. He was in his bunk. No breath. No pulse. And the screaming — it started under him, in the ground, and it went on for hours, and then it stopped, and the ground was wet, and the wet was warm, and I touched it, and it —”

He stopped. The memory was not a memory he wanted to revisit, but the coroner’s attention was pulling it out of him the way a surgeon pulls a splinter — with steady, impersonal pressure, the assumption that the extraction was necessary and the pain was temporary.

“It grabbed me,” Decimus said. “The blood. It formed a hand — I told the praetor, he drew it — and the hand grabbed my wrist, and it was warm, and it had fingernails, and it squeezed, and I could feel — I could feel him. Nerva. In the blood. Not his body. His — I don’t know the word. His self. The thing that made him Nerva and not someone else. It was in the blood, and the blood was holding my wrist, and for a moment I could hear everything he was thinking, everything he was feeling, and what he was feeling was —”

“Terror,” the coroner said.

“No. Confusion. He didn’t understand what had happened. He didn’t know he was dead. He thought he was dreaming. He thought the blood was a dream and the hand was a dream and I was a dream, and he was trying to wake up, and the trying was — it was the worst part. The trying. Because he couldn’t wake up. Because it wasn’t a dream.”

The coroner was quiet for a moment. Then he stood — slowly, his back protesting, his knees protesting, the body of a man who had been sitting on the ground for too long — and he walked to the table and pulled back the cloth that covered Nerva’s body.

“Look at him,” the coroner said.

Decimus looked. He had seen the body before — had helped carry it, had stood guard while the coroner examined it — but he had not looked. Not really. He had done what soldiers did with the dead: acknowledged them, catalogued them, filed them in the part of the mind that held the names and faces of the fallen, and moved on. Looking — really looking, with the attention the coroner was asking for — was different.

Nerva looked wrong. Decimus had seen dead men. Dead men looked like sleeping men who had forgotten how to breathe. Nerva looked empty. The skin was too loose, the features too flat, the body too light. He looked like a wineskin that had been drained and left in the sun — the shape was right but the substance was gone, and the absence of the substance was visible in every fold and crease and hollow.

“His blood is in the ground,” the coroner said. “All of it. Not just the fluid — the information. The consciousness. The thing that made him Nerva. It was extracted — not by a wound, not by a needle, but by a working. A calculation. Someone reached into his probability map and collapsed every branch where he was alive, and the collapse pulled his blood out of his body and into the earth, and the blood carried him with it.”

“Can you put it back?”

The question was simple. The question was, Decimus knew, probably stupid. But the question was also the only question that mattered, because Nerva was in the ground, and Nerva was cold, and Nerva was confused, and Nerva was his friend, and friendship — even the rough, practical, unsentimental friendship of two legionaries who had shared a tent for three years — demanded that the question be asked.

The coroner looked at him. The clinical attention softened — not much, not dramatically, but enough for Decimus to see, behind the professional mask, a man who understood the question and respected it and wished he had a better answer.

“I don’t know,” the coroner said. “The working that did this was — incomplete. The collapse didn’t finish. Nerva is suspended between states — not alive, not dead, not fully in the blood or out of the body. In theory, if the working could be completed — or reversed — the blood might return. The consciousness might reintegrate. He might —”

“Live?”

“Exist. In some form. I can’t promise life. I can’t promise anything. But I can promise that I’m trying to understand what happened, and understanding is the first step toward — something. Reversal. Resolution. An ending that is less terrible than the current state.”

Decimus looked at the body. He looked at the coroner. He made a decision that was not a decision but a commitment — the kind of commitment a soldier makes not with his mind but with his gut, the place where loyalty lives, below reason, below calculation, in the animal part of the self that knows what it owes and pays without being asked.

“What do you need?” he asked.

“I need to see the blood. The blood in the ground. I need to examine it the way I examined the body — systematically, thoroughly, with the attention it deserves. And I need someone who can communicate with it. Someone the blood recognises. Someone it trusts.”

“It trusts me. Nerva trusts me. We shared a tent for three years. I know his mother’s name. I know the girl he left in Brundisium. I know he cheats at dice — not well, everyone knows, but no one says anything because he loses more than he wins and the losing makes him generous.”

The coroner almost smiled. The expression was brief — a flicker, a ghost, the memory of a smile rather than the thing itself. But it was there, and Decimus saw it, and the seeing was — comforting. Because it meant the coroner was human. Because it meant the coroner understood that Nerva was not a data point or a case study or a mathematical problem but a man who cheated at dice and lost and was generous about it.

“Tonight,” the coroner said. “After dark. Meet me at the latrine trench. Bring a lamp. And bring something of Nerva’s — something personal. Something that carries his — his signature. His smell. His history.”

“His dice.”

“His dice will do.”

Decimus nodded. He turned to go. At the tent flap, he stopped.

“Coroner.”

“Yes.”

“Is he suffering? Nerva. In the ground. Is he in pain?”

The coroner was quiet for a long time. The quiet was not evasion — it was consideration, the specific silence of a man who was choosing between a comfortable lie and an uncomfortable truth and had decided, as he always decided, in favour of the truth.

“He’s confused,” the coroner said. “Confusion is its own kind of suffering. But he’s not in pain. Not physical pain. The blood doesn’t have nerve endings. It has — memory. And memory can hurt, but memory can also comfort, and right now, Nerva’s memory is holding him together. His memory of you. Of the camp. Of the life he had before the working took him. The memory is — an anchor. It keeps him coherent. It keeps him Nerva.”

“For how long?”

“I don’t know. Days. Weeks. Eventually, the memory fades, and the coherence fades with it, and the blood becomes — just blood. Inert. Silent. But that hasn’t happened yet. He’s still in there. He’s still Nerva.”

Decimus nodded. He pushed through the tent flap and walked into the morning, and the camp was waking around him, and the men were rising, and the fires were burning, and the daily machinery of survival was grinding into motion, and Decimus walked through all of it and felt none of it because his mind was in the ground, with his friend, in the dark, in the cold, in the blood that remembered being a man who cheated at dice and lost and was generous about it.

He went to Nerva’s bunk. The bunk had not been reassigned — no one wanted it, because the ground beneath it was still warm, still dark, still whispering. Decimus reached under the thin mattress and found what he was looking for: a leather pouch, small, worn soft by years of handling, containing six bone dice. The dice were loaded — badly, obviously, the kind of loading that fooled no one and was not meant to fool anyone but was instead a kind of joke, a running gag, a thing that Nerva did because the doing of it made people laugh and the laughter was worth more than the winning.

Decimus held the dice in his palm. They were warm. Not from the bunk — from something else. From the residual heat of a man’s hand, preserved in bone, the way a man’s voice was preserved in blood, the way a man’s self was preserved in the earth beneath a latrine trench on a volcanic island at the edge of the known world.

He closed his fist around the dice and held them tight and did not cry, because legionaries did not cry, and the not-crying was its own kind of discipline, and discipline was the only thing he had left.

Tonight. The latrine trench. The lamp. The dice.

He would speak to his friend. He would listen. He would do what soldiers did for their fallen: he would bear witness. He would remember. He would not look away.

It was not enough. It was never enough. But it was what he had, and having it was better than having nothing, and having nothing was not an option, because Nerva was in the ground, and Nerva was cold, and Nerva had said his name, and a man who hears his name called by the dead does not get to walk away.

Not if he wants to live with himself afterward.

And Decimus, for all his faults — and they were numerous, and he knew them, and he carried them the way he carried his kit, with the resigned familiarity of a man who has learned that the weight is part of the journey — wanted to live with himself.

So he would go. Tonight. To the trench. To the blood. To the friend who was not dead and not alive and not anything except cold and confused and calling his name.

He would go, and he would listen, and the listening would be the bravest thing he had ever done.

Braver than battle. Braver than the charge. Braver than the wall of shields and the clash of iron and the specific, adrenalised courage that came from knowing that the man beside you would die for you and you would die for him.

Because this courage was not the courage of action. It was the courage of presence. The courage of sitting in the dark beside something terrible and not running. The courage of hearing a dead man’s voice and answering.

Decimus put the dice in his pocket and went to find breakfast, because the dead could wait and the living needed to eat, and the eating was its own kind of courage — the courage of continuing, of putting one foot in front of the other, of believing that the next meal mattered even when the ground beneath you was full of the dead and the sky above you was wrong and the world had become a place where the worst thing you could imagine was not the worst thing that could happen.

He ate. The porridge was bad. He ate it anyway.

Nerva would have complained about the porridge. Nerva complained about everything — the food, the weather, the quality of the local wine, the specific and personal injustice of being stationed on an island where the rain was red and the ground was warm and the dice, for once, were not the most dishonest thing in the room.

Decimus missed the complaining. He missed it the way you miss a sound you’ve heard every day for three years — not consciously, not dramatically, but in the specific, physical way that the ear misses a frequency it has grown accustomed to. The absence of the complaining was louder than the complaining had ever been.

He finished the porridge. He washed the bowl. He went to his post.

The day passed. The sun moved across the doubled sky. The camp functioned — barely, grudgingly, the way a machine functions when several of its parts are missing and the remaining parts are held together by habit and the specific, stubborn refusal of Roman engineering to admit defeat.

Night came.

Decimus went to the trench.

The coroner was already there.
