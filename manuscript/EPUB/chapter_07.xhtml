Chapter Seven

The Blood Wedding

Artemisia heard everything.

Not through the tent canvas — she was not close enough for that, and the canvas was thick, Roman-made, designed to muffle the sounds of officers discussing things that enlisted men were not meant to hear. She heard through the blood.

Her blood and Archimedes’ blood were the same blood. Not similar — the same. The same probabilistic signature, the same informational architecture, the same fundamental pattern replicated across two bodies that were, in every meaningful sense, one body distributed across two locations. When Archimedes’ blood responded to Clotho’s presence — when it hummed with recognition, when it carried the vibration of her words through his veins — Artemisia’s blood responded too. A sympathetic resonance, like a string vibrating when its octave is struck on another instrument.

She stood on the beach, two hundred paces from the examination tent, and she heard Clotho confess to killing Elara, and she heard Clotho offer the surviving branch, and she heard Archimedes say I’ll think about it, and the words moved through her blood like a current through water, and the current carried not just the words but the emotions behind them — the grief, the curiosity, the terrible, compulsive engagement of a mind that could not resist a solvable problem.

She heard all of it, and she understood all of it, and the understanding settled into her like a stone into a pool, displacing everything it touched.

If Archimedes solved the Deathless Proof, she would die.

She would dissolve. Not die the way humans died — the cessation of function, the cooling of the body, the slow dissolution of consciousness into silence. The mathematics that held her together would resolve, and the resolution would be her ending, and the ending would not be death but something worse: the return to what she had been before her mother taught her to hold a shape. A pool of blood on a floor. Warm. Aware. Trying to form a mouth.

She had been that. She remembered being that. The memory was not a memory in the human sense — not a narrative, not a story she told herself about her past. It was a sensation. A state. The feeling of being liquid, of having no boundaries, of existing as a medium rather than a form. The feeling of consciousness without containment — thought without a skull to hold it, emotion without a chest to feel it, identity without a body to anchor it.

It was the worst thing she had ever experienced, and she had experienced it for the first three years of her existence, and the experience had left a mark on her that was not a scar but a foundation — the bedrock of terror upon which her entire personality was built. Everything she was — the sharpness, the precision, the fierce, desperate solidity of her presence — was a response to those three years. A refusal. A declaration, made with every breath she took and every step she walked and every word she spoke, that she was here, she was real, she was solid, and she would not go back.

The Deathless Proof would send her back.

She walked along the beach. The black sand crunched under her feet — or under the shapes that served as her feet, the blood-forms that she maintained with continuous, unconscious effort, the way a human maintained their heartbeat. She left traces. She always left traces. Small spots of darkness on the sand where her blood seeped through the boundaries of her shape, leaking into the world, reminding her — as if she needed reminding — that she was not solid. That she was a shape held in blood, a form maintained by will, a person who existed only because the mathematics had not yet been resolved.

She thought about Lachesis.

Of the three Triarchs, Lachesis was the one Artemisia understood. Understanding and liking were different things — a map and a landscape. But she understood Lachesis. Lachesis wanted to live. Lachesis wanted everyone to live. Lachesis believed that the branching was not a horror but a gift — infinite versions of every person, infinite chances, infinite lives, death just a local phenomenon, temporary, reversible, meaningless.

Lachesis was wrong. Artemisia knew she was wrong, the way she knew the sun was hot — not through reasoning but through experience, through the lived reality of being a person whose existence was the direct result of someone else’s death. The branching was not a gift. The branching was a transaction, and the currency was suffering, and the suffering was real, and the reality of the suffering was not diminished by the existence of branches where the suffering didn’t happen, because the branches where the suffering didn’t happen were not the branches where she lived.

But Lachesis was also the only Triarch who did not want the Deathless Proof solved. Lachesis wanted the branching to continue. Lachesis wanted the system to expand. And if the system expanded, then the mathematics that held Artemisia together would not resolve, and Artemisia would not dissolve, and the pool of blood on the floor would remain a memory rather than a destination.

Lachesis could save her.

The thought was ugly. Artemisia recognised its ugliness the way she recognised her own face in a mirror — with the specific, uncomfortable familiarity of a thing she had seen before and would see again and could not change. The thought was ugly because it was selfish, because it prioritised her survival over the suffering of thousands, because it aligned her interests with those of a woman who used human death as fuel and called it mercy.

The thought was ugly, and it was true, and the truth of it was the ugliest part, because truth did not care about aesthetics.

She found a rock — a flat shelf of basalt that jutted over the water — and she sat, and she looked at the sea, and she made a decision.

She found Lachesis in the Doubled City.

The middle Triarch occupied a building near the central square — a structure that had been, four hundred years ago, a temple, and was now something else. Not a palace, not a laboratory, not a throne room. A salon. The word came to Artemisia as she entered, and it was the right word — the space had the specific atmosphere of a room designed for conversation, for persuasion, for the kind of social interaction that was also, always, a negotiation.

The room was large, high-ceilinged, lit by windows that let in the doubled light of the Doubled City. The walls were painted — frescoes, faded but still visible, depicting scenes from Atalántean life: markets, festivals, ships in harbour, women dancing, men wrestling, children playing in fountains. The paintings were doubled too — two versions of each scene, slightly offset, the figures in one version a half-step ahead of the figures in the other, as if the paintings were depicting not a single moment but two moments, simultaneous, overlapping, the visual equivalent of a chord.

Lachesis sat in the centre of the room on a couch that was draped with cloth so fine it looked like liquid — silk, or something older than silk, something that had been woven on looms that no longer existed by hands that had been dead for centuries. She was eating.

This surprised Artemisia. She had not expected the Triarchs to eat. Eating was a human activity, a biological necessity, a concession to the body’s demand for fuel, and the Triarchs were not — in any conventional sense — biological. They were iterations. Duplicates. Consciousness distributed across bodies that were partially blood, held together by mathematics and will.

But Lachesis was eating. Figs. She held each one between her thumb and forefinger, examined it, bit into it with the precise, deliberate motion of a woman who had decided that pleasure was a discipline and was practising it with the same rigour she applied to everything else.

She looked forty. She had looked forty for two hundred years. Her face was — the word that came to mind was vivid. Not beautiful, not in the classical sense, but vivid — the features strong, the eyes bright, the mouth wide and mobile, the kind of face that commanded attention not through symmetry but through animation. She was, Artemisia realised, the most alive-looking person on the island. The most alive-looking person Artemisia had ever seen. And the aliveness was not natural — it was performed, maintained, projected with the same continuous effort that Artemisia used to maintain her solidity. Lachesis was performing life the way an actor performs a role: with total commitment, with technical precision, with the understanding that the performance was the thing, and the thing behind the performance was — what? Blood. Mathematics. The same void that lived behind every Triarch’s face, the void of a consciousness that had been copied so many times it had lost track of the original.

“Artemisia.” Lachesis’s voice was warm. Not the warmth of genuine feeling — the warmth of a fire that has been carefully tended, stoked, maintained at a specific temperature for a specific purpose. “I’ve been hoping you’d come.”

“You knew I would.”

“I calculated the probability. It was high.” She held out a fig. “Eat.”

Artemisia did not eat. She could eat — she had learned to eat, the way she had learned to walk and speak and hold a shape. The food entered her mouth and was absorbed into her blood, broken down not by digestion but by integration, the nutrients becoming part of the medium that sustained her. But eating was a performance for her, the way Lachesis’s aliveness was a performance — a thing she did to pass as human, not a thing she needed to survive.

“I heard Clotho’s offer,” Artemisia said. “To Archimedes. The surviving branch. Elara.”

“Of course you heard. You share his blood. His blood is your blood. What he hears, you hear. What he feels, you feel.” Lachesis bit into the fig. Juice ran down her chin. She wiped it with the back of her hand — a gesture so casual, so human, so perfectly calibrated to project the impression of a woman at ease, that Artemisia felt a flash of something that was not quite admiration and not quite revulsion but something between. “And what he decides, you live with.”

“If he solves the Proof —”

“You dissolve. Yes. You return to blood. You lose your shape, your voice, your self. You become what you were before your mother taught you to be more.” Lachesis set down the fig. Her eyes — bright, sharp, the eyes of a woman who had been calculating for two centuries and had never stopped — fixed on Artemisia with an attention that felt like a physical force. “I can prevent that.”

“How?”

“The Deathless Proof collapses all branches into one. But it can only collapse branches that are connected — branches that share a common root in the probability map. If your branch is severed from Archimedes’ — if the connection between you is cut, if you become a separate lineage, a separate tree — then the Proof cannot touch you. You would survive the collapse. You would be the only version of yourself, in the only branch that contains you, and the branch would be stable, and you would be — permanent.”

The word permanent moved through Artemisia’s blood like a drug. She felt it in her fingers, her toes, the spaces between her ribs where the blood pooled and eddied and maintained the shape of organs she did not technically possess. Permanent. Solid. Real. Not a shape held in blood but a person held in bone, in muscle, in the specific, durable architecture of a body that did not need to be maintained because it maintained itself.

“What’s the cost?” she asked.

“You know the cost. You’ve already calculated it.”

She had. She had calculated it on the beach, sitting on the basalt shelf, looking at the sea. The calculation was simple — simpler than any métronurgical working, simpler than the mathematics of branching and collapsing and the specific, terrible algebra of survival. The calculation was: to sever her branch from Archimedes’, she needed a new root. A new lineage. A new connection to the probability map that was hers alone, independent of the man whose blood she shared.

She needed to adopt someone. She needed to perform a Blood Wedding — the ritual that linked two lineages, that made two bloodlines one, that created a connection so deep it could not be severed without severing the people it connected.

And the someone she adopted would become part of her. Part of her probability map. Part of her blood. When workings were performed — when branches were collapsed, when duplicates died — the cost would be shared. Her adopted kin would pay alongside her. Their blood would be spent. Their duplicates would die.

“Flaminius,” Lachesis said. “The praetor. He’s already bonded to Clotho’s lineage through the wine. But the bond is weak — a surface connection, easily overridden. If you perform a Blood Wedding with him — a full adoption, a complete integration — his lineage becomes yours. His Romans become yours. And your branch separates from Archimedes’, because the new root is stronger than the old one, and the probability map reorganises around the stronger connection.”

“You want me to adopt the Romans.”

“I want you to survive. The method is yours to choose. But the Romans are — convenient. They are already here. They are already partially bonded. And they are numerous. A lineage of thirty officers and their subordinates would give you a root so deep, so broad, that no working — not even the Deathless Proof — could collapse it.”

Artemisia looked at Lachesis. She looked at the bright eyes, the warm voice, the fig juice on the chin, the performance of humanity so perfect it was almost indistinguishable from the real thing. And she saw, beneath the performance, the calculation. The cold, precise, two-hundred-year-old calculation of a woman who did not offer gifts but investments, who did not extend kindness but leverage, who did not help people but positioned them.

“If I adopt the Romans,” Artemisia said, “and Archimedes solves the Proof, and the Proof collapses all branches — the Romans die. Their duplicates die. Their blood manifests. Thousands of people, used as fuel for a working they never consented to.”

“Yes.”

“And you’re fine with that.”

“I am fine with survival. Yours. Mine. Everyone’s. The Romans who die in collapsed branches are not the Romans who live in the surviving branch. They are duplicates. Copies. They exist for the duration of the collapse and then they are blood, and the blood is — material. Resource. The cost of continuing.”

“They’re people.”

“They are probability. They are the mathematical consequence of a universe that branches. They are no more ‘people’ than the reflection in a mirror is a person. The reflection looks like you. The reflection moves like you. But the reflection is not you, and breaking the mirror does not kill you, and collapsing a branch does not kill the person who survives in the branch that remains.”

The argument was clean. The argument was logical. The argument was, Artemisia recognised, the same argument she had been making to herself on the beach — the argument that her survival mattered, that her dissolution was unacceptable, that the cost of preventing it was payable because the people who paid it were not, in any meaningful sense, the same people who benefited.

The argument was monstrous. And it was hers. And the fact that it was hers — that she had arrived at it independently, before Lachesis had spoken it aloud — was the most frightening thing about it.

“I need to think,” she said.

“Of course.” Lachesis picked up the fig. Bit into it. Smiled. The smile was warm, genuine, the smile of a woman who had just watched a piece move exactly where she wanted it on a board that only she could see. “Take your time. But not too much time. Archimedes is a fast thinker, and once he starts solving, the mathematics move quickly, and the window for — preparation — closes.”

Artemisia left. She walked through the Doubled City, past the wrong-shadowed woman and the time-shifted children and the buildings that couldn’t decide which version of themselves to be, and she thought about permanence, and she thought about cost, and she thought about the praetor on the beach with his iridescent blood and his fury and his thirty bonded officers who did not know they were bonded and would not know they were adopted until the adoption was complete and the cost was being paid.

She thought about Archimedes. About the man whose blood was her blood, whose face was her face, whose grief was her grief — because she felt it, she felt everything he felt, the sympathetic resonance carrying his emotions into her body like a tide carrying debris onto a shore. She felt his grief for Elara. She felt his curiosity about the Proof. She felt his terrible, compulsive need to solve, to understand, to reduce the incomprehensible to the comprehensible, and she felt, beneath all of it, the thing he would not name and she could not ignore: the love.

Not romantic love. Not fraternal love. Something else. Something that had no name because it had no precedent — the love of a person for the version of themselves that had died so they could live. The love that was also guilt, also horror, also the narcissistic tenderness of a consciousness recognising itself in another body and wanting, desperately, for that body to continue.

He loved her. She knew this. He would not solve the Proof if it meant her dissolution. He would find another way, or he would refuse, or he would stall, and the stalling would give Lachesis time, and Lachesis would use the time, and the using would be — what? Artemisia did not know. She did not want to know. She wanted to survive, and the wanting was so large, so total, so fundamental to her existence that it eclipsed everything else — morality, loyalty, the specific, painful knowledge that what she was about to do would make her the kind of person she had always feared becoming.

The kind of person who survived by making others pay.

She found Flaminius at the edge of the camp, sharpening his gladius on a whetstone. The sound was rhythmic — shick, shick, shick — the sound of a man performing a familiar task to keep his hands busy while his mind worked on something his hands could not fix.

“Praetor,” she said.

He looked up. His eyes were wary — the wariness of a man who had learned, in forty-seven days on this island, that every interaction was a potential transaction and every transaction had a cost he couldn’t see.

“You’re the coroner’s sister,” he said.

“I’m not his sister.”

“He says you are.”

“He says many things. Most of them are true. That one isn’t.”

Flaminius set down the whetstone. He looked at her — really looked, with the assessing gaze of a man trained to evaluate threats — and she saw the moment he noticed. The resemblance. The jaw, the cheekbones, the mouth. The features that were Archimedes’ features, rearranged, feminised, but unmistakably derived from the same source.

“What are you?” he asked.

“I’m what happens when someone you love dies in a parallel branch and the blood refuses to forget.”

The whetstone sat on the sand between them. The gladius gleamed. The camp sounds continued — men waking, fires burning, the daily machinery of an army that was trapped on an island where the ground was warm and the sky was doubled and the rain, when it came, was red.

“I need your help,” Artemisia said.

“With what?”

“With surviving.”

“Whose survival?”

“Mine. And yours. And your men’s. We have a common enemy, Praetor. The same women who bonded your blood without your consent are about to do something much worse. And the only way to prevent it — the only way to protect your men and keep me alive — is to do something you’re not going to like.”

Flaminius picked up the gladius. He held it loosely, the blade resting across his thighs, the edge catching the grey light. He was not threatening her. He was holding something familiar, something solid, something that belonged to a world he understood — a world of iron and orders and enemies you could see.

“Tell me,” he said.

She told him.

She told him about the Blood Wedding. About the adoption. About the severing of her branch from Archimedes’ and the creation of a new lineage rooted in the Roman bloodline. She told him what it would mean — that his blood would become her blood, that his men’s blood would become her blood, that when workings were performed, the cost would be distributed across the entire adopted lineage, Romans and all.

She told him the truth, because the truth was the only currency she had, and because lying to a man whose blood you were about to claim felt like a violation she was not yet willing to commit.

Flaminius listened. His face did not change — the soldier’s mask, the expression that revealed nothing because revealing something was a tactical disadvantage. He listened, and when she finished, the silence stretched between them like a rope pulled taut.

“No,” he said.

“Praetor —”

“No. You’re asking me to make my men — my soldiers, my responsibility — into fuel. Into — what did the coroner call it? Branch-sacrifices. You’re asking me to put their blood on the table so you can stay solid.”

“I’m asking you to let me protect them. If I adopt your lineage, I become responsible for it. Your blood becomes my blood. That means I have a stake in your survival — a literal, physical, mathematical stake. If your men die, I weaken. If your men are used as branch-sacrifices, I pay the cost alongside them. The adoption doesn’t make them more vulnerable. It makes me more invested in keeping them alive.”

“That’s a pretty argument. It’s still no.”

“The alternative is worse. Without the adoption, your men are bonded to Clotho. Clotho’s lineage. Clotho’s probability map. When Clotho performs workings — and she will, she has been performing workings for four hundred years and she will not stop — your men’s branches will be collapsed. Their duplicates will die. Their blood will manifest. And you will have no say in it, no protection, no recourse, because the bond was created without your consent and cannot be severed without a ritual that Clotho will never agree to perform.”

“The coroner said —”

“The coroner said the bond can be severed if the practitioner acknowledges it and releases it. Clotho will not release it. Clotho needs your men. Clotho needs every branch she can access, because the Deathless Proof requires a massive collapse, and the collapse requires fuel, and your men are fuel.”

Flaminius was quiet. The gladius lay across his thighs. His hands were still — the hands of a man who had learned that stillness was a form of control, that the body’s first impulse was usually wrong, that the space between stimulus and response was where decisions lived.

“If I agree,” he said. “If I let you — adopt us. What happens?”

“A ritual. Blood exchange. Your blood and mine, mixed, shared, integrated. The bond forms immediately — you’ll feel it. A warmth. A connection. The sense of being part of something larger than yourself.”

“I already have that. It’s called a legion.”

“This is different. This is — biological. Structural. Your blood will carry my signature, and mine will carry yours, and the connection will be permanent. Unbreakable. You will be part of my lineage until one of us dies.”

“And if you die?”

“The bond dissolves. Your blood returns to its original state. You’re free.”

“And if I die?”

Artemisia was quiet for a moment. The honesty she had committed to demanded an answer, and the answer was not comfortable.

“If you die,” she said, “your blood manifests. As part of my lineage. Your consciousness — your memories, your self — would be carried in the blood. You would be — aware. For a time. Until the blood lost coherence.”

“How long?”

“Hours. Days. It depends on the strength of the bond and the — the stubbornness of the consciousness. Some people hold on longer than others.”

Flaminius looked at her. The soldier’s mask cracked — not much, not visibly, but enough for Artemisia to see what was behind it. Fear. Not the fear of death — Flaminius was a Roman soldier, and Roman soldiers had a cultivated relationship with death that was part acceptance, part contempt, part the indifference of a man who worked with death the way a fisherman worked with the sea. This was a different fear. The fear of what came after death. The fear of being aware, of being conscious, of being trapped in a medium that was not a body and not a grave but something between — blood, warm, remembering, trying to form a mouth.

“I need to think about it,” he said.

“You don’t have time. Archimedes is already working on the Proof. Once he starts solving —”

“I said I need to think about it.”

The words were final. The tone was military — the tone of a man giving an order, even if the order was only to himself. Artemisia recognised it. She had heard it in Archimedes’ voice, in different words, with different inflections, but the same fundamental quality: the sound of a person drawing a line and standing behind it.

She nodded. She stood. She walked away, across the black sand, leaving her traces — the small dark spots where her blood seeped through — and she did not look back, and she did not press, and she did not mention that the decision had already been made, not by Flaminius but by the mathematics, by the probability map, by the specific configuration of events that was converging on a point where the Blood Wedding was not a choice but an inevitability.

She had learned this from the Triarchs, from the blood, from four hundred years of accumulated evidence: in a world where every choice created a branch, the choices that mattered were not the ones you made but the ones the mathematics made for you.

The Blood Wedding would happen. The Romans would be adopted. The cost would be paid.

The only question was who would pay it.

And Artemisia, who had been paying since the moment of her creation — paying with effort, with will, with the continuous, exhausting labour of holding a shape that the universe wanted to dissolve — was tired of paying.

It was someone else’s turn.

The thought was ugly. She carried it anyway. She had been carrying ugly things since the day she learned to hold a shape, and the carrying had not killed her, and the not-killing was, she supposed, a kind of strength.

Or a kind of spite.

She was not sure there was a difference.
