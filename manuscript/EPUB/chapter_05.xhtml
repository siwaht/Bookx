Chapter Five

The Praetor's Knife

Flaminius could not sleep.

This was not new. He had not slept properly since the Red Rain — forty-one days ago, though he had stopped counting because counting implied that the days were accumulating toward something, a threshold, a resolution, and Flaminius had been a soldier long enough to know that some situations did not resolve. They simply continued, the way a wound continues when the body lacks the resources to close it, the way a siege continues when neither side can afford to retreat.

He lay on his cot in the praetor’s tent and stared at the canvas ceiling and listened to the camp.

A Roman camp at night had a specific sound — the creak of leather, the clink of iron, the low murmur of men on watch, the occasional bark of a centurion correcting someone’s posture or someone’s attitude or someone’s existence. It was a sound Flaminius had grown up with, the sound of his father’s camp and his grandfather’s camp, the sound of an empire that never slept because sleeping meant trusting the darkness, and Rome did not trust anything it could not tax.

The camp on the Archipelago had a different sound.

Beneath the leather and iron and murmur, there was a hum. Low, continuous, felt more than heard — a vibration that lived in the ground and climbed through the tent pegs and the cot legs and the bones of Flaminius’s spine, settling into the base of his skull like a headache that had decided to become permanent. The hum was the blood. The blood beneath the camp, beneath the island, beneath everything — centuries of it, compressed and whispering, generating a frequency that was not quite sound and not quite silence but something between, something that made the teeth ache and the dreams vivid and the waking hours feel like a continuation of the dreams rather than an escape from them.

Flaminius sat up. His body protested — thirty-six years old and already creaking, the accumulated damage of fifteen years of campaigning settling into his joints like rust into iron. His left shoulder, dislocated in Hispania and never properly reset, ground when he moved it. His right knee, twisted in a fall from a horse in Gaul, clicked with every step. His hands, scarred from a dozen engagements, ached in the cold and the damp and the specific atmospheric pressure of an island where the weather was not weather but a symptom.

He dressed in the dark. Tunic, belt, sandals. No armour — he had stopped wearing armour three weeks ago, when he realised that the things on this island that could kill him were not deterred by iron. He buckled his gladius to his hip out of habit, the way a man buckles his belt — not because he expected to use it but because the weight of it was familiar, and familiarity was the only comfort available.

He pushed through the tent flap into the night.

The camp was quiet. The watch fires burned at the perimeter, casting pools of orange light that made the darkness between them seem darker. The sentries stood at their posts, facing outward, watching the tree line and the beach and the black water beyond, and Flaminius knew — because he had spoken to each of them, because speaking to his men was the one thing he could still do that felt like command — that they were not watching for enemies. They were watching for the ground to open. They were watching for the sky to bleed. They were watching for the thing that had taken Nerva and the six before him, the thing that came from below and above and inside, the thing that was not an enemy because an enemy could be fought and this could not be fought because it was not a thing but a process, a mechanism, a mathematics that used human blood as its medium and human death as its fuel.

Flaminius walked to the edge of the camp.

The beach stretched before him — black sand, black water, black sky. The stars were wrong here. Not in their positions — he had checked, obsessively, with the navigational instruments he carried, and the stars were where they should be, Polaris in the north, Canopus in the south, the familiar constellations wheeling overhead with the mechanical precision of a universe that did not care what happened on the islands beneath it. But the stars were wrong in their quality. They flickered. Not the gentle scintillation of starlight passing through atmosphere — a harder flicker, a stutter, as if the stars were being seen through two layers of air instead of one, two versions of the atmosphere overlapping, each one refracting the light slightly differently.

The doubling. Even the sky was doubled here.

He sat on the sand. It was cold — the volcanic grit held heat during the day and released it at night, and by this hour the beach was the temperature of a stone floor in winter. He sat anyway. The cold was real. The cold was singular. The cold did not have a duplicate.

He thought about home.

Home was a villa outside Praeneste — modest by senatorial standards, comfortable by anyone else’s. A courtyard with a fig tree. A kitchen that smelled of bread and rosemary. A wife who wrote letters in a hand so precise it looked like typesetting, and two daughters who wrote letters in hands so chaotic they looked like the aftermath of a small explosion, and a dog that was too old to hunt and too stubborn to die and slept in the doorway of the kitchen because the warmth from the oven was good for its joints.

He had not received a letter in forty-seven days. The supply ships had stopped coming after the second Red Rain, when the sailors who crewed them refused to approach the Archipelago because the water around the islands was — they said — warm. Not hot. Warm. Body-warm. The temperature of blood.

Flaminius did not blame the sailors. He would not have approached either.

He heard footsteps on the sand behind him. Not the heavy tread of a legionary in sandals — lighter, more deliberate, the footsteps of someone who was choosing where to place each foot with a care that suggested the ground was not entirely trustworthy.

“You should be sleeping,” he said, without turning.

“So should you.” The coroner’s voice. Archimedes. The man the women had summoned, the man who spoke of blood and mathematics and branches with the calm authority of someone describing the weather. Flaminius did not trust him. Flaminius did not trust anyone on this island. But the coroner was the closest thing to an ally he had found, because the coroner was afraid — Flaminius could see it, the way a soldier sees fear in another soldier, not in the face but in the hands, in the way they held things, in the way they put things down — and a man who was afraid was a man who understood the situation, and a man who understood the situation was more useful than a man who didn’t.

Archimedes sat beside him on the sand. He moved stiffly — the man was fifty-three and moved like sixty, the body of a scholar who had spent too many years bent over tables and not enough years doing anything that required his legs to remember they were legs.

They sat in silence for a while. The waves came in. The waves went out. The sound was almost normal.

“I examined the body,” Archimedes said.

“I know. Your report is on my desk. I read it three times. I understood perhaps a third of it.”

“Which third?”

“The third that said my soldier was used as fuel for a ritual performed by women who are four hundred years old and want to die but can’t.”

“That’s a reasonable summary.”

Flaminius picked up a handful of sand. Let it fall through his fingers. The grains were sharp — volcanic glass, pulverised by centuries of waves, still carrying edges that could cut if you pressed hard enough. He pressed hard enough. A thin line of blood appeared on his palm.

“Don’t do that,” Archimedes said. His voice was sharp — sharper than Flaminius had heard it, the professional alarm of a man who understood what blood meant on this island.

Flaminius looked at his palm. The cut was small — a scratch, nothing, the kind of wound a man accumulates a dozen times a day without noticing. But the blood that welled from it was — he watched it, fascinated despite himself — behaving strangely. It pooled in the cut, as blood does. But instead of spreading along the lines of his palm, following the creases and whorls of his skin, it gathered. Concentrated. Formed a single, perfect bead that sat on the surface of the wound like a jewel on a setting, and the bead was not red but iridescent — the same shimmer Archimedes had described in his report, the shimmer of blood that carried more information than blood should be able to carry.

“That’s new,” Flaminius said.

Archimedes was staring at the bead. His face had gone very still — the stillness of a man who is seeing something he has theorised about but never witnessed, the stillness of a hypothesis becoming an observation.

“When did you exchange blood with them?” he asked. “The Triarchs. When did the ritual happen?”

“I didn’t — there was no ritual. I never agreed to any —”

“The adoption. The blood-bond. When did it happen?”

Flaminius felt something cold move through him. Not the cold of the sand or the cold of the night but a different cold, an internal cold, the temperature of a realisation arriving.

“Three weeks ago,” he said. “When we first made contact with the — the women. Clotho. The one who looks sixteen. She came to the camp under a flag of parley. She wanted to negotiate. I offered her wine. She offered me —” He stopped. “She offered me a cup. She said it was a local custom. A gesture of hospitality. I drank it.”

“What was in the cup?”

“Wine. I thought. It tasted like wine. Slightly — thicker. Warmer than it should have been.”

Archimedes closed his eyes. Opened them. “It wasn’t wine. It was blood. Her blood. Mixed with wine to mask the taste and the texture. When you drank it, you ingested her blood, and her blood carried her probabilistic signature, and the signature integrated with yours, and now —”

“Now what?”

“Now you’re part of her lineage. Part of the Triarchs’ bloodline. When they perform workings, they can use your branches — your parallel selves, your duplicates — as fuel. Your blood is their blood. Your death, in any branch, feeds their calculations.”

The cold spread. It filled Flaminius’s chest, his stomach, the spaces between his ribs where the breath should have been. He looked at the bead of blood on his palm — iridescent, shimmering, carrying the signature of a four-hundred-year-old woman who had tricked him with a cup of doctored wine — and he understood, with the sudden, total clarity of a man who has just realised he is standing in a trap that closed weeks ago, that he was no longer entirely his own.

“My men,” he said. “The wine — she brought enough for the officers. The centurions. The tribunes. We all drank.”

Archimedes said nothing. His silence was answer enough.

“How many?” Flaminius asked. “How many of my men are — bonded? Adopted? Whatever the word is.”

“Everyone who drank.”

“That’s thirty men. Thirty officers.”

“And through them, potentially, their subordinates. The bond propagates through command structures — not automatically, but if a bonded officer gives an order that a subordinate follows, the act of obedience creates a weak link. Not a full adoption, but enough for a skilled practitioner to exploit.”

Flaminius stood. The motion was abrupt — military, the snap of a body trained to move from stillness to action without the intervening stages of consideration and doubt. He stood and he looked at the camp — his camp, his men, his responsibility — and he saw it differently now. Not a military installation but a farm. A herd. A collection of bodies whose blood had been claimed by women who were older than Rome and who used human death the way Rome used human labour: as a resource, renewable, expendable, the cost of doing business.

“I’m going to kill them,” he said. His voice was level. The levelness was not calm — it was compression, the sound of a fury so large it could only be expressed by making itself small. “The women. The Triarchs. I’m going to find them and I’m going to kill them.”

“You can’t.”

“Watch me.”

“I mean you literally cannot. They are four hundred years old. They have survived everything — war, plague, assassination, the collapse of the civilisation that created them. They are held together by mathematics that you do not understand and cannot disrupt. Your gladius is a piece of iron. Their bodies are partially blood. You would be stabbing a river.”

“Then I’ll dam the river.”

“With what?”

Flaminius turned to him. The fury was in his eyes now — not the hot, theatrical fury of a man performing anger but the cold, architectural fury of a man building something out of it, a structure, a plan, a purpose that would outlast the emotion that created it.

“With you,” he said. “You understand this. The mathematics. The blood. The — branches. You understand how it works. So you’re going to tell me how to undo it. How to sever the bond. How to get my men’s blood back.”

“It’s not that simple.”

“Make it that simple.”

Archimedes looked at him. The coroner’s face was tired — not the tiredness of a single sleepless night but the tiredness of years, the accumulated exhaustion of a man who had been carrying something heavy for so long that the carrying had become indistinguishable from the man. But beneath the tiredness, Flaminius saw something else. Something that looked, in the dim light of the stars, like the beginning of a decision.

“To sever a blood-bond,” Archimedes said slowly, “the bonded party must be acknowledged as kin by the practitioner who created the bond. The practitioner must formally recognise the bond, accept responsibility for it, and then release it. It’s a ritual — not a working, not a calculation, but a ritual. An act of will.”

“So we make them release us.”

“You can’t make a Triarch do anything. But you can negotiate. You can offer something they want in exchange for something you need.”

“What do they want?”

Archimedes was quiet for a long time. The waves came in. The waves went out. The stars flickered through their doubled atmosphere.

“They want me to solve the Deathless Proof,” he said. “At least two of them do. The Proof is a calculation that would collapse all their duplicate selves into one — ending the branching, ending the duplication, ending four centuries of distributed existence. It would let them die.”

“Then solve it. Solve it and trade the solution for my men’s freedom.”

“If I solve it, people die. Not just the Triarchs — everyone connected to them. Every duplicate, every branch-self, every consciousness suspended in the blood and the stone and the earth. Thousands of people. Tens of thousands. The entire Catacomb. The entire Doubled City.”

“Those aren’t people. You said so yourself — they’re duplicates. Copies. Blood that thinks it’s alive.”

“They are people.” Archimedes’ voice was quiet, but the quietness had an edge, the way a scalpel is quiet. “They are conscious. They suffer. They remember. The fact that they exist in a medium you find disturbing does not make them less real.”

Flaminius stared at him. The fury was still there — it would be there for a long time, he suspected, possibly forever, because this was not the kind of fury that burned out but the kind that banked, that smouldered, that waited — but alongside the fury, something else was forming. Not respect, exactly. Recognition. The recognition of a man who, when confronted with a moral complexity that most people would simplify for the sake of action, refused to simplify.

It was infuriating. It was also, Flaminius admitted to himself in the privacy of his own skull, admirable.

“So what do we do?” he asked.

“We learn. We understand. We find out exactly what the Triarchs are planning, exactly what the Deathless Proof would do, exactly what the alternatives are. And then we make a choice.”

“I’m a soldier. I don’t make choices. I execute orders.”

“Whose orders?”

The question landed like a stone in still water. Flaminius opened his mouth to answer — Rome’s orders, the Senate’s orders, the orders of the empire that had sent him here to claim these islands for the glory and profit of the Republic — and found that the answer, which had been automatic for fifteen years, was no longer automatic. Rome had sent him here. Rome had not sent supplies. Rome had not sent reinforcements. Rome had not sent a single message in forty-seven days. Rome had, in the specific and practical sense that mattered to a soldier in the field, abandoned him.

He was on his own. His men were on their own. And the only person on this island who seemed to understand what was happening was a fifty-three-year-old coroner who spoke about blood the way other men spoke about weather — as a force that could be observed and predicted but not controlled.

“Fine,” Flaminius said. “We learn. But I want a timeline. I want a plan. I want to know, within a week, whether there is a way to get my men off this island with their blood still in their bodies and their souls still in their blood. And if there isn’t —”

“If there isn’t?”

Flaminius looked at the sea. The water was dark, flat, calm in the way that deep water is calm — not peaceful but patient, the calm of something that has all the time in the world and knows it.

“If there isn’t,” he said, “then I’ll find another way. I always do.”

He turned and walked back toward the camp. His footsteps were heavy on the black sand — the footsteps of a man carrying more than his body, carrying the weight of thirty bonded officers and the soldiers beneath them and the knowledge that the ground he walked on was warm with the blood of the dead and the sky above him was doubled and the stars were wrong and the empire that had made him was very far away and getting farther.

Archimedes watched him go.

The coroner sat on the cold sand and looked at the sea and thought about blood-bonds and Deathless Proofs and the specific, terrible mathematics of a world where every act of survival created a victim, and every victim was you, and the you that survived had to live with the knowledge that the you that didn’t was still out there, still aware, still screaming your name into the earth.

He thought about Elara.

He thought about Artemisia.

He thought about the bead of iridescent blood on the praetor’s palm, and what it meant, and what it would cost, and who would pay.

The waves came in. The waves went out.

The blood beneath the island whispered.

Archimedes listened, and did not sleep, and the not-sleeping was its own kind of vigil — a watch kept not for enemies but for answers, in a darkness that was not dark enough to hide the questions.
