Chapter Fifteen

The Mathematics of Mercy

Lachesis’s salon smelled of figs and ink.

The figs were fresh — a bowl of them on a low table beside the couch, their skins split, their flesh pink and glistening, the specific sweetness of a fruit that had been picked at the exact moment of ripeness filling the room with an aroma that was almost obscenely pleasant. The ink was old — the smell of iron gall and gum arabic, the smell of documents and calculations and the specific, permanent record of a mind that had been working for two centuries and had written everything down.

The walls were covered in mathematics.

Not the frescoes Archimedes had seen on his first visit — those had been painted over, or rather, painted on top of. Every surface that could hold ink held ink. Equations, diagrams, probability trees, the specific notation of métronurgical calculation that Archimedes recognised from his own research but had never seen at this scale, at this density, at this level of — he searched for the word — ambition. The mathematics on Lachesis’s walls were not the mathematics of a practitioner. They were the mathematics of a theorist. A visionary. A woman who had looked at the branching structure of reality and seen not a horror but a system, and had spent two hundred years trying to understand the system well enough to control it.

Archimedes stood in the centre of the room and turned slowly, reading the walls the way he read a body — systematically, thoroughly, starting at the top left and working his way across and down, letting the information accumulate, letting the patterns emerge, letting the mathematics speak in their own language before he attempted to translate.

The language was — beautiful.

He hated that it was beautiful. He wanted it to be ugly, wanted the mathematics of mass death to be crude and brutal and obviously wrong, the way a murder weapon is obviously wrong — a thing designed for harm, carrying the signature of its purpose in every line and curve. But the mathematics were not crude. They were elegant. They were the work of a mind that understood probability the way a musician understood harmony — intuitively, structurally, with deep comprehension that came not from study but from immersion, from living inside the mathematics.

“You’re impressed,” Lachesis said. She was on the couch, eating a fig, watching him with the bright, attentive eyes of a woman who had been waiting for someone to appreciate her work and was enjoying the appreciation.

“I’m — reading,” Archimedes said.

“You’re impressed. Your blood is responding. I can feel it — the resonance. Your blood recognises the mathematics the way a speaker recognises their native language. You were born for this, Archimedes. Not the coroner’s table. Not the examination of the dead. This. The mathematics of the living. The calculation of possibility.”

He did not respond. He was reading.

The equations on the walls described — he followed them, line by line, symbol by symbol, the way he followed the vessels of a body during an examination, tracing the flow from origin to terminus — the complete mechanics of branch-collapse. Not the crude, violent collapse he had performed on the beach, the desperate, painful working that had killed his duplicate and produced a barrier for the Romans. This was refined. This was precise. This was the difference between a field amputation and a surgical procedure — the same fundamental operation, performed with tools and techniques that transformed it from butchery into art.

The key was the probability gradient. Lachesis had discovered — or developed, or intuited, the distinction was unclear — a method for identifying branches that were already unstable. Branches that were, in the language of the mathematics, tending toward collapse — timelines where the probability of continued existence was low, where the events that sustained them were unlikely, where the people in them were already, in a statistical sense, dying.

By targeting these branches — by collapsing timelines that were going to collapse anyway — Lachesis could harvest the resulting blood with minimal additional suffering. The people in the targeted branches were already experiencing an existential instability — a timeline that was losing coherence. The collapse simply — accelerated the process. Brought the ending forward. Converted the slow, painful dissolution of an unstable branch into a quick, controlled collapse that produced blood with maximum informational content and minimum additional suffering.

Minimum. Not zero. Archimedes noted this. The mathematics were honest — more honest than Lachesis’s rhetoric in the square. The equations showed clearly that the collapse, however refined, however targeted, however precisely calibrated, still involved suffering. The consciousness in the collapsing branch still experienced its own dissolution. The blood still carried the memory of that experience. The screaming was quieter, but it was still screaming.

“You see it,” Lachesis said.

“I see the gradient targeting. The identification of unstable branches. The selective collapse.”

“And?”

“And I see the residual suffering coefficient. The term in the equation that represents the consciousness of the person being collapsed. It’s smaller than in a crude collapse — much smaller. But it’s not zero. It can’t be zero. The mathematics won’t allow it.”

“Not yet. But the coefficient is a function of technique. As the technique improves, the coefficient decreases. In two hundred years, I’ve reduced it by a factor of — ” She paused, calculating. “Ninety-three percent. The suffering in a modern, targeted collapse is seven percent of what it was in Clotho’s original workings. Seven percent. And with another century of refinement —”

“Another century of practice. Another century of collapsing branches and harvesting blood and reducing the suffering by increments while the suffering continues.”

“Yes. That is how progress works. That is how every technology works. The first surgery was butchery. The first ship was a log. The first fire was an accident. Everything begins in crudeness and moves toward refinement, and the refinement requires practice, and the practice requires — material.”

“The material is people.”

“The material is probability. The material is the mathematical consequence of a universe that branches. I did not create the branching. I did not invent the collapse. I am working with the reality I was given — the reality that Clotho created four hundred years ago when she cut her palm and saved her son and broke the boundary between one timeline and many. The branching exists. The collapse exists. The suffering exists. I am trying to manage it. To reduce it. To turn a catastrophe into a system and a system into a — a gift.”

Archimedes turned from the wall. He looked at Lachesis. He looked at the figs, the ink, the couch, the bright eyes, the warm smile, the performance of reasonableness that was so complete, so polished, so perfectly calibrated that it was almost impossible to see the thing behind it.

Almost.

“Show me the Deathless Proof,” he said.

The smile flickered. Not much — a tremor, a micro-expression, the involuntary contraction of muscles that betrayed an emotion the conscious mind was trying to suppress. The emotion was — Archimedes read it the way he read a body, through the details, the involuntary signs, the things the subject could not control — fear.

Lachesis was afraid of the Deathless Proof.

“The Proof is not mine to show,” she said. “The Proof is Clotho’s. She formulated it. She has been working on it for four hundred years. I have — my own version. My own approach. But the Proof itself — the core calculation, the equation that would collapse all branches into one — that is Clotho’s work.”

“You have a version.”

“I have an alternative. A different approach to the same problem. Clotho’s Proof collapses everything — all branches, all timelines, all duplicates. One survivor. One reality. Everything else becomes blood. My approach is — different. My approach preserves the branching. My approach manages it. My approach turns the infinite tree of possibility into a — a garden. Pruned. Maintained. Cultivated. The branches that are healthy survive. The branches that are dying are harvested. The blood that results is used to nourish the surviving branches. A cycle. A system. Sustainable. Eternal.”

“Show me.”

She stood. She walked to the far wall — the wall that was most densely covered in equations, the wall that Archimedes had not yet reached in his systematic reading. She placed her hand on the wall, and the equations — responded. They moved. The ink shifted on the surface, rearranging itself, the symbols flowing like water, reorganising into a new configuration that was — Archimedes stared — a complete mathematical framework. A system of equations that described not a single collapse but a continuous process, a perpetual cycle of branching and collapsing and harvesting and nourishing that would, if implemented, turn the probability map of the entire world into a managed resource.

He read it the way he read a body — systematically, thoroughly, following the logic from premise to conclusion, checking each step, testing each assumption, looking for the flaw that would invalidate the whole.

He found it.

The flaw was not in the mathematics. The mathematics were — he hated admitting it — flawless. The equations balanced. The logic was sound. The system, as described, would work. It would manage the branching. It would reduce the suffering. It would turn the chaotic, violent, uncontrolled process of branch-collapse into a smooth, efficient, sustainable cycle that would continue indefinitely, producing blood and power and the terrible resource that métronurgy required.

The flaw was in the scale.

Lachesis’s system required — Archimedes followed the numbers, checked them, checked them again — a population. Not the hundred duplicates in the Doubled City. Not the three hundred Romans in the camp. A population. Millions of people, adopted into the Triarchs’ lineage, their branches available for collapse, their blood available for harvest. The system was sustainable only at scale — the way a farm needs enough land, the way an empire needs enough subjects.

Lachesis was not proposing a garden. She was proposing a farm. A hematic farm. A system in which the entire human population of the Mediterranean — adopted, bonded, linked to the Triarchs’ lineage through rituals performed without consent — would serve as the substrate for an infinite cycle of branching and collapsing, their parallel selves dying in collapsed timelines so that their primary selves could live in the surviving one, their blood falling as rain, their memories enriching the soil, their suffering reduced to seven percent and declining.

A farm where death was just weather.

“You see it,” Lachesis said.

“I see it.”

“And?”

“And it’s the most elegant atrocity I’ve ever encountered.”

The word atrocity landed in the room like a blade. Lachesis’s expression did not change — the bright eyes, the warm smile, the posture of a woman who was offering a gift — but something behind the expression hardened. A wall going up. A door closing. The defensive response of a person who has been called what they are and does not accept the name.

“It’s survival,” she said. “It’s the survival of the species. It’s the management of a reality that is already branching, already collapsing, already producing blood and suffering and the specific, terrible cost of a universe that was broken four hundred years ago. I am not creating the horror. I am organising it. I am reducing it. I am turning chaos into order and waste into resource and death into — life.”

“You’re turning people into livestock.”

“I’m turning probability into sustainability. The people in the collapsed branches are not livestock. They are — iterations. Versions. The mathematical consequence of a branching universe. They are no more ‘people’ than the shadow on the wall is a person. They look like people. They feel like people. But they are not the people who survive. They are the cost of the people who survive. And the cost is — manageable. Reducible. Approaching zero.”

“Approaching. Never reaching.”

“Nothing reaches zero. Entropy doesn’t reach zero. Friction doesn’t reach zero. Suffering doesn’t reach zero. The goal is not perfection. The goal is improvement. The goal is a world where the suffering is less than it was yesterday and more than it will be tomorrow. That is the only honest goal. That is the only achievable goal. Everything else is — fantasy.”

Archimedes looked at the wall. He looked at the mathematics — the elegant, flawless, monstrous mathematics of a system that would turn humanity into a managed resource and call it mercy. He looked at the numbers and the symbols and the precise notation of a mind that had spent two centuries refining the mechanics of mass death into something that looked, on paper, like progress.

He memorised it — every equation, every variable, every coefficient and constant and hidden assumption. Completely, permanently.

“Thank you,” he said. “For showing me.”

“Will you help me build it?”

“No.”

“Will you help Clotho destroy it?”

“I don’t know yet.”

“Then what will you do?”

Archimedes looked at her. He looked at the bright eyes, the warm smile, the two-hundred-year-old woman who had built a mathematical framework for the management of human suffering and believed — genuinely, completely, with the total conviction of a mind that had been thinking about this for longer than most civilisations lasted — that she was right.

“I’ll solve the Deathless Proof,” he said. “Not for Clotho. Not for Atropos. For the mathematics. Because the Proof is the answer to the question that all three of you are asking — the question of whether the branching can be ended or only managed, whether the suffering can be stopped or only reduced, whether death is an ending or just a transition. The Proof will answer the question. And the answer will determine — everything.”

“And if the answer is that the branching cannot be ended? That the suffering cannot be stopped? That my system is the best available option?”

“Then I’ll know. And knowing is — enough. Knowing is always enough. Because knowing is the difference between a choice and a guess, and I would rather make a choice I hate than a guess I can’t verify.”

He left. He walked out of the salon, out of the Doubled City, down the path to the Roman camp, and the mathematics followed him — not on paper, not in ink, but in his blood, where the equations lived now, where the numbers pulsed with the rhythm of his heartbeat, where the elegant, flawless, monstrous system of Lachesis’s design sat alongside the crude, painful, honest working he had performed on the beach, and the two systems existed in his blood the way two branches existed in the probability map — simultaneously, incompatibly, each one real, each one true, each one demanding a choice that he was not yet ready to make.

He would make it. Soon. The mathematics were converging. The Proof was taking shape — not in his conscious mind but in his blood, where the information lived, where the patterns formed, where the answer was assembling itself from everything he had seen and heard and felt since arriving on the island.
