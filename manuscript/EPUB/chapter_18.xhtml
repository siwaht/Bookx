Chapter Eighteen

Lachesis Makes Her Move

The sky turned red at noon.

Not the gradual shift of the previous Red Rain — the slow bleed from grey to pink to crimson that had given the camp time to prepare, time to raise shields, time to assume the futile defensive posture of men who had learned that the enemy was the weather. This was instant. One moment the sky was grey. The next it was red — arterial red, the red of a wound that has just been opened, bright and wet and pulsing with the rhythm of a heart that was not a heart but a working, a calculation, a mathematics so vast that the sky itself was its medium.

Archimedes was in the examination tent when it happened. He felt it before he saw it — the probability map flaring in his blood, the branches shuddering, the seismic vibration of a reality that was being restructured at a fundamental level. He dropped his pen. He ran.

Outside, the camp was — chaos was too small a word. The camp was experiencing something that had no word, because the thing that was happening had no precedent. The Red Rain was not falling. The Red Rain was rising.

From the ground. From the earth. From the blood-saturated volcanic rock beneath the camp, beneath the island, beneath everything. The blood was coming up — not as rain, not as the slow seep of a wound. As a tide. A flood. A vertical river of red that erupted from the soil in geysers, in fountains, in columns of blood that rose ten feet, twenty feet, fifty feet into the air before arcing outward, curving, flowing not downward but — Archimedes tracked the trajectory with the automatic precision of a man who had spent his life observing the behaviour of fluids — toward the Cauldron.

The volcano. The volcano that erupted backwards. The volcano where ash fell upward and time flowed in directions that time was not supposed to flow.

The blood was flowing toward the Cauldron. Every drop. Every geyser. Every column and fountain and eruption of red. The blood was being pulled — drawn, summoned, commanded — from the earth and the stone and the foundations of the Doubled City and the walls of the Catacomb and the ground beneath the Roman camp, pulled upward and outward and toward the mountain that sat at the centre of the Archipelago like a heart at the centre of a body.

Lachesis was feeding the Cauldron.

Archimedes understood this with the instant, blood-level comprehension that his first working had given him. He could feel the mathematics — not read them, not calculate them, but feel them, the way you feel a change in air pressure before a storm. Lachesis was performing a working of a scale that dwarfed anything he had encountered. She was not collapsing a branch. She was not harvesting a lineage. She was reaching into the probability map of the entire Archipelago — every person, every duplicate, every consciousness suspended in the blood and the stone and the earth — and she was pulling.

She was pulling them into the Cauldron.

She was feeding four centuries of accumulated branch-blood into the volcano, and the volcano was — Archimedes could feel it, could feel the mountain responding, could feel the geological and métronurgical forces interacting in ways that the mathematics of neither discipline could fully describe — the volcano was converting the blood into something else. Into energy. Into potential. Into the raw, undifferentiated power that a working of truly cosmic scale would require.

She was preparing.

She was preparing for the mass adoption.

The ritual she had described to Artemisia — the ritual that would claim every living human in the Mediterranean into her lineage, that would make every person’s blood her blood, every person’s branches her branches, every person’s death her fuel. The ritual that would turn humanity into a hematic farm. The ritual that would make death just weather.

She was preparing, and the preparation was the blood of the island, centuries of it, being drawn from the earth and fed into the mountain, and the mountain was accepting it, and the mountain was growing, and the sky was red, and the ground was shaking, and the Doubled City was — Archimedes could feel it through the resonance, through the connection to Artemisia who was in the city — the Doubled City was destabilising.

The duplicates were dissolving.

Myrto. The fish seller. The woman who had been dead for three hundred years and had not known it. She was dissolving. Her blood — the blood that had held her shape, that had sustained her consciousness, that had anchored the doubling of the city — was being pulled from her, drawn toward the Cauldron, and without the blood, the shape was failing, and the consciousness was fragmenting, and the woman who had sold fish and remembered pomegranates and had a cat that caught more fish than she did was coming apart.

All of them were coming apart. The hundred duplicates in the Doubled City, the thousands in the Catacomb, the tens of thousands in the earth — all of them, every consciousness, every whisper, every fragment of a person who had been collapsed and manifested and sustained for four centuries — all of them were being drawn into the Cauldron.

Lachesis was consuming the island to fuel her working.

Archimedes ran.

He ran through the camp, past soldiers who were staring at the sky with the paralysed horror of men who had seen the Red Rain and thought they understood it and were now discovering that their understanding had been a child’s understanding, a fraction of the truth, a single drop in an ocean of blood. He ran past Flaminius, who was shouting orders that no one could hear over the sound — because there was a sound now, a sound that was not a sound but a vibration, a frequency, the collective voice of every consciousness being drawn from the earth, a chorus of the dissolved, the consumed, the sacrificed, all of them screaming not with mouths but with blood, the scream carried through the medium of the island itself, through the rock and the soil and the water and the air.

He ran toward the Doubled City.

He ran because Artemisia was there. He ran because the Blood Wedding had linked her to the Roman lineage, and the Roman lineage was linked to Clotho’s lineage through the wine, and Clotho’s lineage was linked to Lachesis’s lineage because they were the same lineage, they were all Danaë, they were all iterations of the same woman, and the working that Lachesis was performing was drawing on every connected lineage, every linked blood, every adopted and bonded and ritually joined consciousness in the Archipelago.

Including Artemisia.

Including the Romans.

Including him.

He felt it as he ran — the pull. The draw. The specific, physical sensation of his blood being — summoned. Called. Commanded to flow not through his veins but outward, toward the Cauldron, toward the mountain, toward the working that was consuming the island. His blood resisted — his blood was his, was anchored in his body, was held in place by the specific, biological architecture of a living human being — but the resistance was costing him. He could feel the effort. The strain. The specific, muscular tension of a body that was fighting to keep its blood inside while a force that was older and stronger and more desperate than anything he had ever encountered was trying to pull it out.

He reached the Doubled City.

The city was — collapsing. Not physically — the buildings were still standing, the walls were still intact, the doubled architecture was still present. But the doubling was resolving. The two versions of each building were merging — not into one, not into the singular beauty that Lachesis had shown them in the square, but into something else. Something wrong. The two versions were collapsing into each other like two bodies falling into the same grave, the merger violent, uncontrolled, the architectural equivalent of a scream.

The duplicates were gone. The streets were empty. The wrong-shadowed woman, the time-shifted children, the hundred souls who had held the doubling in place for four centuries — all gone. Drawn into the Cauldron. Consumed.

Artemisia was in the square.

She was on her knees beside the doubled fountain, and she was — Archimedes saw it and the seeing was a blade through his chest — dissolving. Not fully. Not yet. But the edges of her were — softening. Blurring. The sharp lines of her face, the precise architecture of her features, the specific, hard-won solidity that she had spent thirty years building — all of it was softening, the way a sandcastle softens when the tide comes in, the edges rounding, the details fading, the shape becoming less a shape and more a suggestion of a shape.

Her blood was being pulled. The Cauldron was drawing on her lineage — the Roman lineage, the lineage she had adopted through the Blood Wedding — and the draw was stronger than her will, stronger than her mathematics, stronger than the thirty years of practice that had taught her to hold a shape in a medium that wanted to be shapeless.

“Archimedes,” she said. Her voice was — wrong. Thick. Wet. The voice of a person speaking through a medium that was not air but blood, the words forming in the liquid of her throat and emerging distorted, attenuated, the sound of a consciousness that was losing its grip on the body that contained it.

He knelt beside her. He took her hands. The hands were — warm. Too warm. The warmth of blood that was being activated, energised, called to service by a working that did not care about the person it was consuming, that cared only about the fuel, the power, the raw material of consciousness that the Cauldron needed to prepare for the mass adoption.

“Hold on,” he said. “Hold your shape. Focus. The working is drawing on the lineage, but the lineage is yours — you adopted it, you claimed it, you are the root. If you hold, the lineage holds. If you hold, the Romans hold. If you hold —”

“I can’t.” The words were barely words. The voice was barely a voice. Artemisia’s face was — he watched, and the watching was the hardest thing he had ever done, harder than the partial collapse, harder than the working on the beach, harder than listening to his duplicate scream for three hours — her face was losing definition. The features blurring. The jaw softening. The cheekbones rounding. The individual, irreplaceable architecture of a person becoming the generic, undifferentiated medium of blood.

“You can,” he said. “You’re me. You’re the version of me that learned to hold a shape when the shape was impossible. You’re the version of me that refused to dissolve. You’re the strongest version of me that exists. Hold on.”

She looked at him. Through the blurring, through the softening, through the dissolution that was taking her features and her voice and her self — she looked at him, and the look was — clear. Focused. The look of a person who has been told something they needed to hear and has heard it and is using it, the way a drowning person uses a rope, not gently, not gratefully, but with the desperate, total, life-or-death grip of a consciousness that will not let go.

She held.

The dissolution slowed. The blurring stopped. The features — still soft, still imprecise, still carrying the evidence of how close she had come to losing them — stabilised. She was not solid. She was not the sharp, precise, hard-won Artemisia who had sat on the rock at the beach and held very still. She was — softer. Rounder. Less defined. But she was there. She was present. She was holding.

The Cauldron roared.

The sound came from the mountain — a sound that was not geological, not any sound the earth was supposed to make. It was the sound of blood. The sound of four hundred years of accumulated consciousness being compressed, concentrated, converted into the raw power that Lachesis needed for her working. The sound was — Archimedes felt it in his bones, in his teeth, in the spaces between his cells — the sound of thousands of people screaming simultaneously, their individual voices merged into a single, continuous, unbearable note that was not a note but a frequency, a vibration, a resonance that shook the island to its foundations.

The sky pulsed. Red. Brighter. The blood in the air — the blood that had risen from the earth and flowed toward the Cauldron — was visible now, a river of red arcing across the sky, connecting the island to the mountain, the earth to the fire, the dead to the power that would consume them.

And then — it stopped.

The roaring stopped. The pulsing stopped. The river of blood in the sky — froze. Hung there, suspended, a crimson arc against the red sky, motionless, silent, the specific, terrible stillness of a system that has been interrupted mid-process.

Clotho.

Archimedes felt her before he saw her. Felt her in his blood — the oldest signature, the deepest resonance, the four-hundred-year-old pattern that was the root of every lineage on the island, the source from which every iteration of Danaë had been derived. Clotho was — intervening. Interrupting. Using her authority as the original, the first, the woman who had invented métronurgy and who understood it at a level that neither Lachesis nor Atropos could match, to halt the working.

The river of blood in the sky began to fall. Not toward the Cauldron — downward. Toward the earth. Toward the places it had been drawn from. The blood was returning. The consciousnesses were returning. The four hundred years of accumulated death were flowing back into the ground, back into the stone, back into the walls of the Catacomb and the foundations of the Doubled City and the earth beneath the Roman camp.

The sky faded. Red to pink to grey. The ordinary, exhausted grey of the Archipelago’s permanent overcast.

Artemisia solidified. Not fully — not the sharp, precise solidity of before. But enough. Enough to stand. Enough to speak. Enough to be, in the diminished but real sense that was all she had ever had, a person.

“She stopped it,” Artemisia said. “Clotho stopped it.”

“For now,” Archimedes said. “Lachesis will try again. The Cauldron is — primed. The blood is — ready. She’s been preparing this for decades. Maybe centuries. The mass adoption. The hematic farm. The infinite branching. She’s not going to stop because Clotho interrupted one working.”

“Then what do we do?”

Archimedes looked at the sky. The grey sky. The singular sky. The sky that was, for this moment, not red, not doubled, not carrying the blood of the dead.

“We solve the Proof,” he said. “Before she tries again. We solve it, and we use it, and we end this. All of it. The branching. The collapsing. The blood. The rain. The four hundred years of horror that started with a woman cutting her palm to save her son.”

“And us? What happens to us?”

He looked at her. He looked at the softened features, the blurred edges, the woman who was his duplicate and his other self and the person he loved in a way that had no name and no precedent and no resolution.

“I don’t know,” he said. “But I know the Proof isn’t about collapse. It’s about integration. And integration means — us. Together. Not one surviving and one dissolving. Both. Together. The way we should have been from the beginning.”

She looked at him. The look was — hope. Not the bright, confident hope of a person who believes things will work out. The fragile, desperate, almost-extinguished hope of a person who has been told that the thing they want most — the thing they have wanted since the moment they became aware, since the first moment of consciousness in a pool of blood on a floor — might be possible.

Might be.

“Together,” she said.

“Together,” he said.

The word was not a promise. It was a hypothesis. A theory. A mathematical proposition that had not yet been proven.

But it was the best he had, and on this island the best was always insufficient, and the insufficient was always all there was.
