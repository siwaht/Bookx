Chapter Twenty-Three

The Last Meal Before the Mountain

Between Chapters Twenty-One and Twenty-Two (The Braiding and the Epilogue)

The night before the braiding, Macro cooked.

He cooked the way he always cooked — with the focused, unhurried attention of a man who understood that the act of cooking was its own argument against the end of the world. The world was ending. The sky had been red three days ago. The ground was warm. The Doubled City was fracturing into versions of itself that no longer made sense. The Cauldron was primed with four hundred years of compressed consciousness and a woman who wanted to turn humanity into a farm.

Macro cooked lentils.

He cooked them with garlic and rosemary and a splash of the local wine — which was not wine, exactly, but something fermented from a fruit that grew on the doubled trees of the island’s interior, something that tasted like wine the way the island tasted like an island: almost right, slightly wrong, carrying a persistent quality of a thing that existed in two states at once. He cooked them slowly, the way lentils required, the way everything that mattered required — with patience, with attention, with the willingness to let the process take the time it needed.

The smell spread through the camp.

It was, Archimedes thought, the most ordinary smell he had encountered since arriving on the island. Not the copper of blood, not the sulphur of the Cauldron, not the organic warmth of the blood-saturated earth. Garlic. Rosemary. The domestic smell of a meal being prepared by someone who cared about the meal.

He followed the smell.

They gathered without planning to. That was the thing about Macro’s cooking — it was a kind of gravity. People moved toward it the way water moved toward the sea: not because they decided to but because the direction was simply correct, the path of least resistance, the natural consequence of being a person who was hungry and cold and afraid and could smell garlic.

Archimedes arrived first. Then Artemisia, who had been in the Doubled City and had felt the smell through the blood — through the resonance, through the connection that carried not just emotions but sensations, the smell of garlic arriving in her blood the way a message arrives in a letter, specific and unmistakable. Then Flaminius, who had been walking the perimeter and had stopped when the smell reached him and had stood for a moment with his eyes closed, and the closing of his eyes was not weariness but something else, something that looked, in the firelight, like gratitude.

Then Decimus, who came with his hand in his pocket, holding the dice.

They sat around the fire. Macro handed out bowls without asking who wanted them, because Macro understood that the question was unnecessary — everyone wanted them, everyone needed them, the wanting and the needing were the same thing in the cold, blood-soaked context of this island on this night.

The lentils were good. They were, Archimedes thought, the best lentils he had ever eaten, and the best-ness was not about the lentils. The lentils were ordinary. The best-ness was about the eating of them, here, now, in this company, on this night, with the Cauldron glowing on the horizon and the blood whispering beneath their feet and the morning bringing something that none of them could fully predict.

“What happens tomorrow?” Flaminius asked. He was not asking for information — he had the information. He was asking the way soldiers asked questions before a battle: not to learn the answer but to hear it spoken aloud, to make it real, to convert the abstract into the concrete by the simple act of saying it.

“We go to the Cauldron,” Archimedes said. “We make the offering. The braiding begins.”

“And if it doesn’t work?”

“It will work.”

“You don’t know that.”

“No. But the mathematics are sound, and the mathematics are all I have, and I’ve been trusting them for twenty-three years, and they haven’t killed me yet.”

Flaminius looked at him. “They killed a version of you.”

“That’s different.”

“Is it?”

Archimedes considered this. “Yes,” he said. “The version that died understood why. The understanding made it — not acceptable. But comprehensible. There’s a difference between dying for a reason you understand and dying for a reason you don’t.”

“Is there? The dying is the same.”

“The dying is the same. The meaning is different. And meaning is — it’s not nothing. It’s not everything. But it’s not nothing.”

Flaminius was quiet for a moment. He ate his lentils. The eating was deliberate — the eating of a man who was paying attention to the food, who was not eating mechanically but consciously, with the full attention of a person who understands that this meal might be one of the last ordinary things he does.

“My daughters,” he said. “The older one — Livia — she’s eight. She collects beetles. Not dead ones. Live ones. She keeps them in a box with holes in the lid and she names them and she talks to them and she is absolutely certain that they understand her.” He paused. “The younger one — Fausta — she’s five. She doesn’t collect anything. She just follows Livia around and does whatever Livia does, including talking to the beetles, which she does with complete seriousness, as if the beetles are important people who deserve to be addressed with respect.”

No one spoke. The fire crackled. The lentils steamed.

“I want to go home,” Flaminius said. The words were simple. The weight behind them was not. “I want to go home and sit in the courtyard with the fig tree and watch my daughters talk to beetles. That’s all. That’s the whole of what I want. I don’t want glory. I don’t want Rome to remember my name. I want to sit in the courtyard and watch my daughters talk to beetles and eat dinner and go to sleep in a bed that doesn’t hum.”

“You’ll go home,” Archimedes said.

“You don’t know that either.”

“No. But I’m going to try very hard to make it true.”

Flaminius looked at him. The look was long, and in it was something that Archimedes had not seen in the praetor’s face before — not trust, not the military respect of a man acknowledging a superior. Something simpler. The look of a man who has been afraid for a long time and has found, in the company of other afraid people, a fragile comfort.

“The beetles,” Decimus said. “What are their names?”

Flaminius blinked. “What?”

“The beetles. The ones your daughter collects. What does she name them?”

A pause. Then, slowly, the compressed energy in Flaminius’s face shifted. Not relaxed — shifted. The way a fist shifts when the fingers uncurl, not open but less closed.

“She names them after Roman generals,” he said. “She has a Scipio and a Marius and a Sulla. She had a Caesar but he escaped.”

Decimus made a sound that was not quite a laugh. “Of course he did.”

“She was furious. She spent three days looking for him. She found him under the kitchen table, eating a crumb of bread, and she put him back in the box and told him that his behaviour was unacceptable and that she expected better from a man of his reputation.”

The laugh that followed was — Archimedes felt it, and the feeling was a gift — genuine. Not the polite laugh of people performing normalcy. The real laugh, the kind that comes from somewhere below the chest, the kind that arrives before you can decide whether to let it, the kind that is its own argument for the continuation of the world.

Macro laughed. Decimus laughed. Flaminius laughed — a short, surprised sound, as if the laugh had caught him off guard, as if he had not expected to laugh tonight and was not entirely sure he was allowed to.

Artemisia did not laugh. She sat beside Archimedes with her bowl of lentils and she watched the others laugh and her face held the specific, complicated expression of a person who is witnessing something they have always wanted and are not sure they are permitted to want. The expression of a person who is almost part of something and knows the almost.

Archimedes looked at her. She looked at him. The resonance between them carried the expression — he felt it in his blood, the almost, the specific, tender ache of a consciousness that had been almost-human for thirty years and was, in this moment, as close to human as it had ever been.

He handed her his bowl.

She looked at it. She looked at him.

“You haven’t finished,” she said.

“I’m not hungry.”

“You’re always hungry. You just don’t admit it.”

“Eat the lentils, Artemisia.”

She ate the lentils. She ate them the way she ate everything — with the focused attention of a person who was not eating for sustenance but for the experience of eating, for the sensory reality of food in a mouth, warmth in a throat, the ordinary miracle of a body that could receive nourishment and be nourished.

She ate, and the eating was real, and the reality was — enough.

After the meal, they sat for a while without speaking. The fire burned down. The stars appeared — the wrong stars, the doubled stars, the stars that flickered through two layers of atmosphere and arrived at the eye slightly out of phase. But they were stars. They were the same stars that hung over Syracuse and Rome and every other place where people sat around fires and ate lentils and talked about their daughters and their beetles and the ordinary things that made the world worth saving.

“Tell me about Elara,” Artemisia said.

The words were quiet. They were not a demand — they were an offering. The offering of a person who understood that the thing being asked for was not information but permission. Permission to speak the name. Permission to make the person real, specific, present, in the way that speaking a name makes a person present.

Archimedes was quiet for a moment.

“She ate olives one at a time,” he said. “She held each one between her thumb and forefinger and examined it before she ate it. As if each olive were a small decision that deserved consideration.”

“Why?”

“I don’t know. I never asked. I should have asked.” He paused. “She laughed with her whole body. Not just her mouth — her shoulders, her hands, her stomach. The laugh moved through her like a wave through water. You could feel it if you were standing close enough. The vibration of it.”

“What did she laugh at?”

“Everything. Bad jokes. Good jokes. The specific, absurd things that happen when you live in a city — a dog stealing a fish from a market stall, a man arguing with a cart that had lost a wheel, a child chasing a chicken through the agora. She found the world funny. Not cruelly funny. Genuinely funny. She thought the world was a comedy and she was grateful to be in the audience.”

Artemisia was quiet. The fire crackled. The stars flickered.

“She sounds like someone worth grieving,” she said.

“She was.”

“Is.”

“Is,” he agreed. “She’s alive. In a branch. In a garden. Sewing and humming.”

“You saw her.”

“In the Cauldron. Yes.”

“And you chose not to —”

“Yes.”

Artemisia was quiet for a long time. Then: “Why?”

“Because she was whole. Because the life she had built was complete. Because the gap I would have left when I was pruned from her timeline had healed, and the healing was — it was the most loving thing I’ve ever seen. The healing was the proof that she was alive. That she had continued. That the loss had not ended her.”

“And you?”

“I’m not healed. I don’t think I will be. But I’m — different. The grief is the same size. The loss is the same weight. But I’ve been carrying it for seven years and I know the shape of it now, and knowing the shape of something is not the same as being free of it, but it’s better than not knowing.”

Artemisia looked at him. The look was long, and in it was everything — the love that was also guilt, the tenderness that was also horror, the specific, impossible bond between a man and the version of himself that had died so he could live and had refused to stay dead.

“I’m sorry,” she said. “About Elara. About what I am. About the fact that my existence is the cost of yours.”

“Don’t be.”

“I am.”

“I know. But don’t be. The cost was paid before either of us had a choice. The only question is what we do with the life that the cost bought. And I think —” He paused. “I think we’re about to find out.”

She nodded. She handed him back his bowl. He looked at it — empty, the lentils gone, the bowl clean in the way that bowls are clean when someone has eaten everything in them and left nothing behind.

“Good lentils,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Macro is wasted on the army.”

“I’ve told him that.”

“What did he say?”

“He said a man who can cook has no business killing people. He said the skills are incompatible.”

Artemisia almost smiled. The expression was brief — a flicker, a ghost, the memory of a smile on a face that had been almost-smiling for thirty years. But it was there, and Archimedes saw it, and the seeing was a gift.

“He’s right,” she said.

“He usually is.”

They sat by the dying fire, and the stars flickered overhead, and the blood whispered beneath their feet, and the Cauldron glowed on the horizon, and the morning was coming, and the morning would bring what it brought, and the bringing would cost what it cost, and the cost would be paid in blood, because the cost was always paid in blood, and the blood always remembered.

But for now, the fire was warm. The lentils were good. The company was worth the danger.

And somewhere in the Doubled City, Myrto was selling fish to no one and did not seem to mind.

And somewhere in the ground beneath the camp, Nerva was cold and confused and waiting, and Decimus had his hand in his pocket, holding the dice, and the holding was its own kind of promise.

And somewhere in a garden in a branch so thin it was almost invisible, Elara was sewing and humming, and the humming was the sound of a life that had continued, and the continuation was the most beautiful thing Archimedes had ever witnessed.

The fire burned down to coals.

They slept.

In the morning, they went to the Cauldron.
