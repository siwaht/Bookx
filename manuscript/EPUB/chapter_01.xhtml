Chapter One

The Bloodless Man

The corpse had no blood in it, and Archimedes found this personally offensive.

Not morally offensive — he had long since exhausted his capacity for moral offense, the way a man who lives beside the sea eventually stops hearing the waves. Professionally offensive. He had been a coroner for twenty-three years, and in that time he had opened four thousand, six hundred and twelve bodies, and every single one of them had contained blood. Blood in expected places and unexpected places. Blood pooled in the dependent tissues where gravity had dragged it after the heart stopped. Blood clotted in the chambers of the heart like dark jelly. Blood dried to a crust in wounds, or still liquid in the abdominal cavity where a ruptured spleen had emptied itself, or crusted under fingernails where the dying had clawed at their own skin.

Blood was the constant. Blood was the signature of a life that had been lived and a death that had occurred. Blood was, in Archimedes’ professional opinion, the single most reliable witness to the circumstances of a person’s ending, because blood did not lie, did not forget, did not rearrange its testimony to flatter the living.

This body had none.

He stood over the Roman soldier — young, perhaps twenty-five, the muscles of his arms still defined beneath skin that had gone the colour of old parchment — and pressed his thumb into the tissue of the man’s forearm. The flesh yielded like dough. No lividity. No discolouration. No subcutaneous pooling. He lifted the arm and let it fall; it dropped with a dry, papery sound, like a sleeve stuffed with sand.

“When was he found?” Archimedes asked, not looking up.

“Dawn.” The voice belonged to a legionary who stood at the entrance to the tent, his hand on the pommel of his gladius, his face arranged in the particular expression of a man who has been ordered to assist a civilian and resents every moment of it. “The watch found him in his bunk. Thought he was sleeping.”

“He wasn’t sleeping.”

“We gathered that.”

Archimedes leaned closer. The soldier’s eyes were open — not the half-lidded droop of a body whose muscles had relaxed in death, but wide, fixed, the pupils blown to the edges of the iris. The expression was not peaceful. It was surprised, the way a man looks when he opens a door and finds not the room he expected but a void, a nothing, an absence so complete it registers not as darkness but as the removal of the concept of light.

He had seen that expression before. Once. On his wife’s face, the morning he found her.

Archimedes closed his eyes. Opened them. Continued.

“No wounds,” he said, more to himself than to the legionary. He ran his hands along the body with the practised efficiency of a man who touches the dead the way a baker touches dough — without sentiment, without revulsion, with the focused attention of someone looking for information in a medium most people found repulsive. “No contusions. No ligature marks. No signs of poisoning — the nails are clean, the lips aren’t discoloured, the conjunctivae are clear.” He paused. “Except for the blood.”

“Except for the blood,” the legionary agreed.

“There isn’t any.”

“We noticed.”

Archimedes straightened. His back protested — fifty-three years of bending over tables and bodies and the small, precise work of opening the dead had compressed his spine into something that creaked like a ship’s hull in a crosswind. He pressed his knuckles into the small of his back and looked at the legionary properly for the first time.

The man was afraid. Not of the corpse — Romans were practical about death, the way people who manufacture it in bulk tend to be. He was afraid of something else. Something he had seen, or heard, or been told, that had settled into the space behind his eyes like a stone in a shoe.

“What aren’t you telling me?” Archimedes asked.

The legionary’s jaw tightened. “The praetor wants to speak with you.”

“The praetor can wait. What aren’t you telling me?”

A pause. The legionary glanced at the corpse, then at the tent flap, then at a point somewhere above Archimedes’ left shoulder, the way people look when they’re deciding how much truth they can afford.

“He was screaming,” the legionary said. “Before. The watch heard him screaming in his bunk, but when they got there, he was — like this. Silent. Empty. And the screaming didn’t stop.”

Archimedes felt something cold move through his chest. “The screaming didn’t stop.”

“It moved. Into the ground. Under the tent. Like something was screaming underneath him, in the earth. It went on for — I don’t know. An hour. Two. Then it stopped, and the ground was wet.”

“Wet.”

“Red.”

Archimedes looked at the floor of the tent. Packed earth, the kind the Romans laid for their camps — levelled, tamped, covered with a thin layer of gravel for drainage. Ordinary. Except that in the corner, where the gravel met the canvas wall, there was a stain. Dark. Irregular. The shape of something that had tried to be a shape and failed.

He crouched beside it. The stain was dry now, but he could see the texture — not the flat, even absorption of spilled liquid, but something more complex. Ridges. Whorls. Patterns that looked, if you tilted your head and let your eyes unfocus, like the impression of fingers. Of a palm. Of a hand that had pressed itself against the earth from below, reaching upward, trying to break through.

He touched it. The stain was warm.

That was wrong. The stain should have been cold — dried blood reaches ambient temperature within hours, and the morning air in the Archipelago was cool enough to raise gooseflesh. But this stain was warm, body-warm, the temperature of living skin, and when Archimedes pressed his fingertip into it, he felt — or imagined he felt — a pulse.

He pulled his hand back.

“Get the praetor,” he said.

The Roman camp occupied the eastern beach of the largest island in the Archipelago of Ash — a crescent of black sand bracketed by volcanic rock, the kind of beach that looked like it had been designed by someone who wanted to remind visitors that the earth was, at its core, a machine for producing fire. The sand was not sand in any meaningful sense; it was pulverised basalt — fine and sharp — and it got into everything — boots, food, wounds, the spaces between teeth — with the persistence of a debt collector.

Archimedes walked through the camp with the legionary two paces behind him, and he catalogued what he saw the way he catalogued everything: automatically, compulsively, the habit of a man whose profession required him to notice what others preferred to ignore.

The camp was regulation — ditches, palisade, leather tents in ordered rows, the whole apparatus of Roman military occupation deployed with the mechanical precision of an empire that had been doing this for centuries. But the precision was fraying. He could see it in the details. A tent rope improperly tensioned. A latrine ditch that hadn’t been limed. A stack of pila leaning against a supply wagon at an angle that would have earned a flogging in any camp within a hundred miles of Rome.

The soldiers were worse. They moved through the camp with the careful, deliberate gait of men walking on ice — not the brisk efficiency of a legion on campaign but the cautious shuffle of people who had learned that the ground beneath them could not be trusted. Their eyes moved constantly, scanning not the perimeter (where enemies might approach) but the sky (where something else might fall).

He had been told about the Red Rain. He had not believed it.

He was beginning to reconsider.

The praetor’s tent was at the centre of the camp, larger than the others, marked by the eagle standard that stood outside it like a declaration of intent. The standard was tarnished. No one had polished it. In a functioning legion, that would have been unthinkable. Here, it was just another symptom.

The legionary stopped at the entrance. “He’s inside.”

“You’re not coming in?”

The legionary looked at him with an expression that was almost pitying. “No.”

Archimedes pushed through the tent flap.

Praetor Gaius Flaminius was not what Archimedes had expected.

He had expected a Roman officer — which was to say, he had expected a specific kind of man: broad, blunt, armoured in certainty the way his soldiers were armoured in iron, the kind of man who solved problems by applying force and measured success by the silence that followed. He had known men like this in Syracuse, before Syracuse fell. They were predictable. They were useful. They were, in their way, comforting, because a man who believes the world is simple will act simply, and simple actions are easy to anticipate.

Flaminius was not simple.

He sat behind a campaign desk that was covered not with maps or dispatches but with drawings — dozens of them, charcoal on linen, each one depicting the same thing from a different angle. A shape. A pattern. Something that looked like a hand, or a face, or a flower, or all three at once, depending on how the light caught the charcoal. The drawings were precise, obsessive, the work of a man who had seen something he couldn’t understand and was trying to trap it on cloth the way a naturalist pins a butterfly.

Flaminius looked up. He was younger than Archimedes had expected — mid-thirties, perhaps, with the kind of face that had been handsome before exhaustion had hollowed it out. His eyes were the colour of the sea on a day when the sea is trying to decide whether to be green or grey, and they held the particular quality of attention that Archimedes associated with people who had stopped sleeping and started seeing.

“You’re the coroner,” Flaminius said. Not a question.

“I’m the coroner.”

“Sit.”

There was one chair. Archimedes sat. The chair was Roman — folding, leather-seated, designed for a man in armour. It was uncomfortable in the specific way that Roman furniture was always uncomfortable: functional, durable, and utterly indifferent to the human body’s preference for curves.

“The soldier,” Flaminius said. “Lucius Nerva. Twenty-four. From Brundisium. Joined the legion to pay his father’s debts. Good with a pilum. Bad at dice. Owed money to half the century and favours to the other half.” He paused. “He was a person.”

“I know.”

“Do you? Because the women who run this island — the ones who summoned you — they don’t seem to think so. They talk about him like he was a — a component. A piece of a calculation that didn’t balance.”

Archimedes said nothing. He had learned, over twenty-three years of speaking with the bereaved, that silence was more useful than words in the first minutes of a conversation about the dead. People needed to say the thing they needed to say, and they needed to say it to someone who would not immediately try to fix it.

Flaminius looked at his drawings. He picked one up — the one that looked most like a hand — and held it toward Archimedes.

“This is what came out of the ground,” he said. “After Nerva died. After the screaming stopped. The earth — opened. Not cracked. Opened, the way a mouth opens. And this came out.” He tapped the drawing. “Made of blood. Nerva’s blood, I think, though I don’t know how blood can be someone’s when it’s not inside them anymore. It reached up. It tried to grab the nearest man — Decimus, one of the watch. Decimus says it was warm. He says it had fingernails.”

Archimedes took the drawing. The charcoal lines were steady — Flaminius had a draughtsman’s hand, precise and controlled. The shape was unmistakable: a human hand, rendered in dark strokes, the fingers slightly curled, the palm upturned, the wrist tapering into something formless. It was reaching. Not grasping — reaching. The gesture of a person who wants to be pulled up, pulled out, pulled free.

“It dissolved,” Flaminius continued. “After about an hour. Just — sank back into the earth. But the stain is still there. Still warm. And Decimus says —” He stopped. Rubbed his face with both hands, the gesture of a man scrubbing away something that wouldn’t come off. “Decimus says he can hear it. Under the ground. Whispering. He says it’s saying Nerva’s name.”

“Is Decimus reliable?”

“Decimus is the most boring man in the legion. He has never had an original thought. He collects rocks. He is not the kind of man who hears things.”

Archimedes set the drawing down. “How long have you been on this island, Praetor?”

“Forty-seven days.”

“And how many men have you lost?”

Flaminius was quiet for a moment. Then: “To combat, none. To disease, two — a fever, nothing unusual. To —” He gestured at the drawings, at the tent, at the island beyond. “To this. Seven.”

“Seven men. Like Nerva.”

“Not all like Nerva. Nerva was the worst. The others — some just disappeared. Walked into the interior and didn’t come back. One was found in the shallows, face down, no marks, no blood. One was found in his tent with his eyes open and his mouth full of —” Flaminius stopped again. “Ash. His mouth was full of ash. But the ash was falling upward, out of his mouth, into the air. It fell upward for three days before it stopped.”

Archimedes absorbed this. He filed it alongside the bloodless corpse, the warm stain, the screaming earth, the hand made of blood. He did not yet attempt to connect these things. Connection was a luxury that came after observation, and observation required patience, and patience required the willingness to sit with confusion without reaching for the nearest explanation.

“Why did you send for me?” he asked. “Specifically. You could have sent for any physician, any scholar. Why a coroner from Syracuse?”

Flaminius looked at him. The look was long, measuring, the look of a man deciding whether to trust.

“Because the women told me to,” he said. “The three who rule this place. They said you would understand. They said you had — experience. With blood that behaves in ways blood shouldn’t.”

The cold thing in Archimedes’ chest moved again. Deeper this time. Settling.

“They told you my name.”

“They told me your name. They told me about your wife.”

The silence that followed was the kind that has weight — not the absence of sound but the presence of something unsaid, pressing against the walls of the tent like water against a dam.

“What did they tell you,” Archimedes said, and his voice was level, and his hands were still, and the only sign that anything had changed was a slight tightening around his eyes, the involuntary contraction of the orbicularis oculi that no amount of professional composure could fully suppress.

“That she died,” Flaminius said. “That you spent years trying to prove she was alive somewhere — in some other branch, some other version of events. That you failed. That the failure is why you’ll help us.”

“The failure.”

“They said you proved she was dead in every branch. Every possible version of events. Someone had — pruned her. Cut away every timeline where she survived. They said that’s not supposed to be possible. They said whoever did it was very old and very powerful and very desperate.”

Archimedes stood. The chair scraped against the packed earth. He walked to the tent flap and looked out at the camp — the tarnished eagle, the nervous soldiers, the black sand beach where the waves left lines of foam that looked, in the morning light, like the edges of old scars.

“The women who told you this,” he said. “How many of them are there?”

“Three.”

“And they look —”

“One looks sixteen. One looks forty. One looks ancient. They are not what they look.”

“No,” Archimedes said. “They are not.”

He let the tent flap fall. He turned back to Flaminius.

“I’ll examine the body properly,” he said. “I’ll need a table, good light, and a basin of clean water. I’ll need to see the place where the blood emerged from the ground. And I’ll need to speak with Decimus.”

“And then?”

“And then I’ll speak with the women.”

Flaminius nodded. Then, as Archimedes moved toward the exit: “Coroner.”

Archimedes stopped.

“My men are afraid. They are Roman soldiers, and they are afraid of the ground they’re standing on. They are afraid of the sky. They are afraid of the rain, because the last time it rained, the rain was red, and it tasted of salt, and it carried — voices. Memories. Things that happened to people who don’t exist.” Flaminius paused. “I need to know what’s happening. Not the mythology. Not the philosophy. The mechanism. How does a man lose all his blood without a wound? How does blood come out of the earth with fingernails? How does rain carry memories?”

Archimedes looked at the praetor — this young, exhausted, precise man who drew pictures of impossible things and demanded mechanical explanations — and felt something he had not felt in years. Something closer to recognition — the recognition of a man who, when confronted with the incomprehensible, did not pray or flee or go mad but sat down and started drawing.

“The mechanism,” Archimedes said, “is called métronurgy. It is the mathematics of blood. And the women who rule this island invented it four hundred years ago to save a city that should have died.”

“And?”

“And the city lived. And the cost of its living is still being paid. And your soldier — Lucius Nerva, twenty-four, from Brundisium, good with a pilum, bad at dice — was part of the payment.”

Flaminius stared at him. “Payment to whom?”

“To himself,” Archimedes said. “To the version of himself that died in a branch that was collapsed to keep this one standing. The blood that came out of the ground — that was Nerva. A different Nerva, from a different version of events, who died so that a calculation could balance. And the blood remembered being him. And it wanted to live.”

The praetor was silent for a long time.

“That,” he said finally, “is the worst thing I have ever heard.”

“Yes,” Archimedes said. “It is.”

He pushed through the tent flap and walked into the morning, and the black sand crunched under his feet like the sound of small bones breaking, and the sky above the Archipelago of Ash was the colour of a bruise that would never heal, and somewhere beneath the earth, something that had once been a Roman soldier was whispering a name that no one alive could hear.

Archimedes heard it anyway.

He had been hearing it for years.

Different name. Same whisper. Same warm, copper-scented grief, rising from the ground like memory from a wound, like blood from a body that refused to forget it had once been alive.

He walked toward the examination tent, and he did not look at the sky, and he did not look at the ground, and he did not think about his wife, and the not-thinking was its own kind of calculation — a branch he chose, moment by moment, where the grief was manageable and the work was possible and the man who carried both could still put one foot in front of the other.

The bloodless man was waiting.

Archimedes had work to do.
