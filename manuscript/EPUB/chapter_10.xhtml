Chapter Ten

The First Working

The Red Rain came on the seventh day.

Archimedes was in the examination tent, working through the mathematics of Nerva’s suspension — filling sheets of linen paper with equations that looked, to anyone who was not Archimedes, like the fever-dreams of a man who had confused arithmetic with theology — when the light changed.

The grey light that filtered through the canvas shifted toward red — gradually, the way a sunset shifts, except that it was midmorning and the sun was high and the shift was not coming from the horizon but from directly above, from the sky itself, which was turning the colour of a wound.

He set down his pen. He stood. He pushed through the tent flap.

The camp was already in motion. Soldiers were emerging from their tents, looking up, their faces lit by the reddish light that was intensifying by the second. Centurions were shouting orders — shields up, formation, defensive positions — the automatic response of men trained to react to threats with structure, with discipline, with the comforting geometry of the Roman military formation, even when the threat was the sky.

Flaminius was at the centre of the camp, his gladius drawn, his face tilted upward. He was not giving orders. He was watching.

The sky was red.

Not the red of sunset or the red of fire or the red of any natural phenomenon Archimedes had ever witnessed. This was the red of blood — arterial blood, bright, oxygenated, the specific crimson of blood that has just left the heart and is still carrying the full charge of a living body’s chemistry. The sky was this colour from horizon to horizon, a dome of red that pressed down on the island like a lid on a pot, and the red was not static — it moved. It churned. It roiled with the slow, viscous motion of a liquid that was too thick to flow freely but too thin to hold still.

The first drop fell.

It hit the sand three paces from Archimedes’ feet. Not a raindrop — larger, heavier, the size of a grape. It struck the black sand and splashed, and the splash was red, and the red was warm, and the warmth was the warmth of a body, and the drop — Archimedes crouched beside it, his professional instincts overriding his horror — the drop was blood.

Human blood. He could see the cells — not with his naked eye, but with his blood, which recognised the drop the way a speaker of Greek recognises Greek, through the fundamental architecture of the thing, the pattern, the signature. Human blood. Fresh. Alive, in the specific sense that the cells were intact, the proteins were functional, the information was present.

And the blood was screaming.

Not audibly — not yet. But Archimedes could feel it, through the contact point where his knee touched the sand beside the drop, through the thin layer of fabric and skin and subcutaneous tissue that separated his blood from the blood on the ground. The drop was screaming. The drop contained a consciousness — fragmentary, compressed, the residual awareness of a person who had existed in a parallel branch for exactly as long as it took for the branch to collapse — and the consciousness was experiencing the specific, terrible moment of its own dissolution, and the experience was being broadcast through the blood like a signal through a wire.

More drops fell.

Ten. A hundred. A thousand. The sky opened, and the Red Rain came down in sheets — the heavy, driving deluge of a storm, except that the storm was blood, and the blood was warm, and the blood was screaming, and the screaming was not one voice but thousands, tens of thousands, a chorus of the collapsed, the pruned, the sacrificed, all of them experiencing their own endings simultaneously, all of them broadcasting their terror and their grief and their rage through the medium of the blood that was all that remained of them.

The camp dissolved into chaos.

Soldiers ran for cover — under tents, under wagons, under shields held overhead like umbrellas. The blood hit the canvas and soaked through. It hit the shields and ran down the curved surfaces in rivulets that pooled at the soldiers’ feet. It hit exposed skin and — Archimedes watched a legionary stumble, his face contorted, his hands pressed to his temples — it carried its cargo of consciousness into the living, the memories of the dead flooding into the minds of the living through the medium of shared blood, shared biology, shared humanity.

The legionary screamed. Not his scream — someone else’s. A woman’s scream, high and thin and carrying the specific timbre of a voice that had been speaking a language the legionary did not know, in a life the legionary had never lived, in a branch that had been collapsed to fuel a working the legionary had never consented to.

Other soldiers were screaming too. The Red Rain was hitting them, soaking through their tunics, running into their eyes and mouths, and with every drop, a fragment of a dead person’s consciousness entered them — a memory, an emotion, a moment of terror or joy or the mundane, specific experience of a life that had been real and was now blood. A soldier dropped his pilum and began to weep, not from pain but from the sudden, overwhelming memory of a child’s birthday — not his child, not his birthday, but the memory was so vivid, so specific, so saturated with the love and the cake and the small, sticky hands, that the soldier’s own emotions were displaced, overwritten, replaced by the grief of a parent who had lost a child in a branch that no longer existed.

Archimedes stood in the rain.

He stood in it because he could not move. Not from paralysis — from information. The blood was hitting him, soaking into his tunic, running down his face, and with every drop, a consciousness touched his, and the touching was not gentle. It was a flood. A deluge of other people’s lives pouring into him through his skin, through his blood, through the fundamental architecture of his biology that recognised the blood as kin — not because it was his blood but because it was human blood, and human blood spoke a common language, and the language was screaming.

He saw a fisherman mending a net. He saw a woman nursing an infant. He saw an old man sitting in a doorway, watching the sun set over a harbour that did not exist in this timeline. He saw a boy running through a market, stealing a pomegranate, laughing. He saw a priest praying to a god whose name he did not recognise. He saw a soldier — not a Roman soldier, an Atalántean soldier, armoured in bronze, carrying a spear — standing on a wall, watching an army approach, knowing he would die, knowing the wall would fall, knowing that the city behind him would burn and the people in it would scream and the screaming would last for four hundred years.

He saw all of them. He saw thousands of them. Each one a complete person, a complete life, compressed into a drop of blood and falling from a sky that was the colour of their collective death.

And then he heard Lachesis.

Her voice cut through the chorus of the dead like a blade through cloth — clear, strong, carrying the specific authority of a woman who had been speaking for two hundred years and had learned to make her voice heard over any noise, any chaos, any amount of screaming.

“This is mercy,” she said.

Archimedes turned. She was standing at the edge of the camp — not in the camp, not among the soldiers, but at the perimeter, where the black sand met the volcanic rock of the island’s interior. She was untouched by the rain. The blood fell around her but not on her — a circle of dry ground, perhaps ten feet in diameter, where the rain simply did not fall, as if the sky itself recognised her authority and deferred to it.

She was smiling.

“Watch,” she said.

She raised her hands. The gesture was simple — palms up, fingers spread, the gesture of a woman offering something or receiving something or both at once. And the rain — the Red Rain, the blood of thousands, the screaming deluge — responded.

It changed direction.

Not all of it. A portion — perhaps a tenth of the total rainfall — shifted. The drops that had been falling vertically began to curve, to arc, to flow toward the camp not from above but from the sides, converging on the soldiers who were huddled under their shields and their tents and their terror. The blood moved with purpose now — not the random fall of rain but the directed flow of a river, a current, a force that knew where it was going and what it intended to do when it got there.

It hit the wounded.

Archimedes had not noticed the wounded — in the chaos of the rain, in the flood of other people’s memories, he had not registered that several soldiers were injured. A man with a gash on his forearm from a training accident that morning. A man with a fever that had been building for days. A man with a broken rib from a fall, the bone pressing against his lung, his breathing shallow and wet.

The blood found them. It flowed to them — across the sand, up their legs, along their arms — and where it touched their wounds, the wounds closed. The gash sealed. The fever broke. The broken rib shifted, realigned, knitted. The blood entered the soldiers’ bodies through their injuries and healed them, and the healing was not gentle — it was violent, fast, the biological equivalent of a door being kicked open — and the soldiers screamed, not from pain but from the sudden, overwhelming sensation of their bodies being repaired by a force that was not their own, a force that carried the consciousness of the dead, a force that healed by pouring other people’s lives into the gaps where injury had been.

The wounded soldiers stood. They were healed. They were whole. They were also — Archimedes could see it in their eyes, in the way they moved, in a haunted quality in their expressions — carrying passengers. The blood that had healed them had not left. It was inside them now, integrated into their biology, and the consciousnesses it carried were inside them too — fragments of other people’s lives, lodged in their blood like shrapnel in flesh, present and persistent and impossible to remove.

“This is mercy,” Lachesis said again. “This is what métronurgy can be. Not horror. Not cost. Healing. Life. The blood of the dead, used to save the living. The branches that collapse, used to fuel the branches that survive. A system. A cycle. Death feeding life feeding death feeding life, forever, infinitely, the way the sea feeds the rain feeds the rivers feeds the sea.”

“Those men are carrying the dead inside them,” Archimedes said. His voice was flat. The flatness was not calm — it was the dangerous flatness of a man who was containing something that, if released, would be very loud. “You healed their bodies and filled them with the consciousness of people who died to fuel the healing. They will carry those consciousnesses for the rest of their lives. They will hear voices. They will have memories that aren’t theirs. They will dream other people’s dreams.”

“They will live,” Lachesis said. “The alternative was death. The man with the broken rib — his lung was collapsing. He had hours. Now he has years. Decades. A full life, enriched by the memories of others, expanded by the experiences of people he never met. Is that not a gift?”

“It’s a violation.”

“It’s survival. And survival is always a violation — of entropy, of probability, of the universe’s preference for stillness. Every breath you take is a violation. Every heartbeat is a theft — energy stolen from the environment, converted into motion, into thought, into the specific, temporary miracle of being alive. I am simply — scaling the process.”

The rain was slowing. The sky was fading from red to pink to the ordinary grey of the Archipelago’s permanent haze. The blood on the ground was cooling, losing its warmth, losing its voices. The screaming was fading — not stopping, not ending, but diminishing, the way a crowd’s noise diminishes as the crowd disperses, each voice going its separate way, each consciousness losing coherence, each drop of blood becoming just blood.

Flaminius appeared at Archimedes’ side. The praetor was soaked — his tunic dark with blood, his hair plastered to his skull, his face streaked with red. He was shaking. Not from cold. From the memories. Archimedes could see them moving behind his eyes — other people’s lives, other people’s deaths, the cargo of the Red Rain lodged in his mind like splinters.

“My men,” Flaminius said. His voice was controlled. The control was costing him everything. “The ones she healed. What’s happening to them?”

“They’re carrying branch-consciousnesses. Fragments of the people whose blood was used to heal them. The fragments will fade — over days, weeks — but they won’t disappear entirely. Your men will have — echoes. Memories that aren’t theirs. Dreams. Moments of confusion where they’re not sure which life they’re remembering.”

“Can you fix it?”

“I don’t know.”

“Can you try?”

Archimedes looked at the praetor. He looked at the camp — the blood-soaked tents, the shaking soldiers, the healed men who stood in the middle of the chaos with glazed expressions — people who had just been saved by something they did not understand and did not want. He looked at Lachesis, who stood at the perimeter in her circle of dry ground, smiling the smile of a woman who had just demonstrated a product and was waiting for the customer to acknowledge its value.

He looked at all of this, and he made a decision.

Not the decision to solve the Deathless Proof. A smaller decision. The decision to do something — right now, in this moment, with the tools he had — to protect the people in front of him from the thing that had just been done to them.

“I need blood,” he said.

Flaminius stared at him. “You need —”

“My blood. I need to perform a working. A small one. A calculation. I need to create a — a barrier. A filter. Something that will prevent the branch-consciousnesses from fully integrating with your men’s biology. It won’t remove them, but it will — contain them. Quarantine them. Keep them separate from your men’s own consciousness so the echoes don’t become permanent.”

“You said métronurgy kills people. You said every working creates a duplicate who dies.”

“It does.”

“So you’re going to kill a version of yourself to save my men.”

“Yes.”

The word was simple. The weight behind it was not. Archimedes had never performed a major working. He had studied métronurgy for seven years — had dissected its products, had documented its costs, had mapped its mechanisms with the thoroughness of a man who believed that understanding a thing was the first step toward controlling it. But he had never done it. He had never cut his palm and reached into the probability map and pulled a branch into reality. He had never created a duplicate of himself and watched it die.

He was about to.

“Give me your knife,” he said to Flaminius.

The praetor hesitated. Then he drew his gladius and reversed it, offering the handle. Archimedes took it. The blade was heavy — heavier than his surgical knife, heavier than any instrument he had used in twenty-three years of cutting the dead. This was a weapon. This was a tool designed for the opposite of his profession — designed not to open the dead but to make the living dead.

He drew the blade across his left palm.

The pain was — he had expected it to be clean, the way Danaë’s pain had been clean in the stories, the bright line of fire that clarified everything it touched. It was not clean. It was ragged, ugly, the pain of a dull blade on soft skin, and the blood that welled from the cut was ordinary — red, warm, smelling of copper, behaving the way blood behaved when it left the body: spreading, thinning, following gravity.

Then it changed.

The blood on his palm stopped spreading. It gathered. It concentrated. It formed a bead — iridescent, shimmering, carrying the same signature he had seen on Flaminius’s palm, the same signature he had seen in Nerva’s brain. The bead sat on his wound like a jewel, and it was not ordinary blood anymore. It was a medium. A channel. A doorway into the probability map that contained every version of his life, every branch, every possible Archimedes who had ever existed or could ever exist.

He looked into it.

The map opened.

It was not a visual experience — not a thing he saw with his eyes. It was a thing he felt with his blood, a thing he knew with the part of himself that was not mind and not body but something between, something that lived in the medium where information and matter were the same thing. The probability map was — vast. Infinite. A tree of branches extending in every direction, each branch a timeline, each timeline a complete version of his life, each version containing a complete Archimedes who had made different choices, lived different days, loved different things, died different deaths.

He could see them. Thousands of himself. Millions. An Archimedes who had never left Syracuse. An Archimedes who had died in the siege. An Archimedes who had never married Elara. An Archimedes who had married someone else. An Archimedes who had become a soldier instead of a coroner. An Archimedes who had become a priest. An Archimedes who had become nothing — who had died in infancy, in childhood, in the specific, unremarkable way that most people died in a world where survival was not guaranteed and death was not dramatic but simply the default state of a universe that did not care.

He found the branch he needed. A thin one — a timeline where the difference was small, where the Archimedes who lived there had made one different choice on one different day, a choice so minor that the two timelines were almost identical, almost indistinguishable, separated by a single moment of divergence that had produced a version of himself who was, in every meaningful sense, him.

He reached for it.

The room split.

He felt it — the dislocation, the wrongness, the sense of the world becoming two things pretending to be one. The air thickened. The light doubled. And then — the other Archimedes was there. Not visible. Not physical. But present. A consciousness, a self, a complete person occupying the same space, sharing the same blood, experiencing the same moment from a slightly different angle.

The other Archimedes was confused. The other Archimedes did not understand what was happening. The other Archimedes was — Archimedes could feel it, through the blood, through the shared medium of their existence — afraid.

I’m sorry, Archimedes thought. Not said — thought. The thought carried through the blood, from one consciousness to the other, and the other consciousness received it, and the reception was — understanding. Not acceptance. Understanding. The other Archimedes understood what was about to happen, because the other Archimedes was him, and he understood, and the understanding was not comfort but clarity, and clarity was the only gift he could offer.

He collapsed the branch.

The other Archimedes died.

The death was not instant. It was not painless. It was — Archimedes felt it, through the blood, through the connection that existed for exactly as long as the collapse took to complete — a process. A dissolution. The other consciousness coming apart, the memories fragmenting, the self disintegrating into its component parts, each part becoming blood, each drop of blood carrying a fragment of the person it had been.

The blood manifested.

It came from Archimedes’ wound — not the slow seep of an ordinary cut but a sudden, violent exhalation of red that splashed onto the ground at his feet and pooled there, warm and thick and iridescent. The blood was — he could feel it — him. A version of him. A complete consciousness, compressed into liquid, experiencing the moment of its own dissolution.

The blood tried to form a hand.

The hand reached for him.

Archimedes looked at the hand — his hand, made of his blood, carrying his consciousness, reaching for him with the desperate, instinctive gesture of a person who is drowning and sees a solid thing and grabs for it — and he did not take it.

He could not take it. If he took it, the working would fail. The blood needed to be used — directed, channelled, applied to the purpose for which the duplicate had been created and killed. The blood needed to become a barrier, a filter, a calculation that would protect the Romans from the branch-consciousnesses that Lachesis had poured into them.

He let the hand reach. He let it grasp at air. He let it fall.

The blood collapsed. The hand dissolved. The consciousness — his consciousness, his duplicate, his other self — screamed. Not with a mouth, because it no longer had a mouth. With the blood itself, the scream carried through the medium, a vibration that Archimedes felt in his bones, in his teeth, in the spaces between his cells where the blood flowed and the information lived.

The scream lasted three hours.

Archimedes stood in the camp, surrounded by blood-soaked soldiers and the fading Red Rain and the cooling puddles of other people’s deaths, and he listened to himself scream. He listened to the version of himself that he had created and killed — the version that had been alive for exactly as long as it took for the mathematics to resolve, the version that had understood what was happening and had been afraid and had died anyway because the mathematics demanded it.

He did not look away. He had examined four thousand bodies and spoken for every one of them and he would not look away from this one, even though this one was him, even though the screaming was his screaming, even though the blood on the ground was his blood and the grief in the blood was his grief and the dying was his dying.

He stood in it. He let it be what it was.

The scream faded. The blood cooled. The consciousness dissolved. The puddle on the ground became inert — just blood, just red, just the residue of a choice that could never be unmade.

But the working had succeeded. Archimedes could feel it — the barrier, the filter, the calculation he had woven into the blood as it manifested, using the energy of his duplicate’s death to create a structure that would protect the Romans from full integration with the branch-consciousnesses Lachesis had implanted. The barrier was thin — fragile, temporary, a bandage rather than a cure — but it was there, and it was working, and the soldiers who had been healed were already showing signs of relief, the glazed expressions clearing, the foreign memories receding, the echoes fading from shouts to whispers.

Flaminius was staring at him.

“What did you just do?” the praetor asked.

“I killed myself,” Archimedes said. “A version of myself. To protect your men.”

“The blood — the hand — it was —”

“Me. Yes. It was me. A different me, from a different branch, who died so that the calculation could work. He was alive. He was aware. He was afraid. And I killed him.”

The words were flat. The flatness was not composure. The flatness was the sound of a man who had just crossed a line he could not uncross, who had done a thing he could not undo, who had become — in the space of three hours, in the time it took for a scream to fade — a practitioner. A métronurgist. A man who had created a person and killed them to fuel a calculation.

He was, now, exactly what he had spent seven years studying. He was the thing he had documented and analysed and condemned. He was a survivor whose duplicate had died for him, and the blood on the ground was his own guilt made manifest, and the guilt was warm, and the guilt was fading, and the fading was the worst part, because it meant the guilt would become bearable, and bearable guilt was the most dangerous kind, because bearable guilt could be repeated.

“I need to sit down,” he said.

He sat. On the ground. In the blood. His blood. The blood of the version of himself that had died screaming so that thirty Roman soldiers could keep their minds.

He sat, and he did not cry, and the not-crying was not strength but exhaustion, and the exhaustion was not physical but moral, and the moral exhaustion was the irreversible consequence of having done a thing that was necessary and terrible and that he would do again if he had to, and the knowledge that he would do it again was the thing that frightened him most.

Not the doing. The willingness.

The willingness was the real cost. The willingness was the thing that métronurgy took from you — not your blood, not your life, but your ability to say I would never do this. Because you would. You had. And the having changed you, and the change was permanent, and the permanence was the point.

The blood on the ground cooled.

The camp was quiet.

The sky was grey again — ordinary grey, singular grey, the grey of a world that had finished bleeding and was pretending nothing had happened.

Archimedes sat in the blood and stared at nothing and thought about the Deathless Proof and knew — with the certainty of a man who had just felt his own duplicate die in his blood — that the Proof was not a mathematics problem.

It was a question.

The question was: How many versions of yourself are you willing to kill?

And the answer — the answer that the Proof demanded, the answer that would collapse every branch and end every duplication and let the dead be dead — was: All of them.

He was not ready for that answer.

Not yet.

But the yet was shrinking.
