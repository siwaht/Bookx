Chapter Thirteen

The Cook's Philosophy

The best meal Archimedes had eaten in forty-seven days was prepared by a man named Titus Macro, who was the worst soldier in the century and the best cook in the legion and who considered these facts to be related.

“A man who can cook,” Macro said, stirring a pot that contained something that smelled, improbably, of rosemary and garlic and the specific, comforting aroma of lentils that had been simmered for exactly the right amount of time, “has no business killing people. Killing is the opposite of cooking. Cooking takes a collection of dead things and makes them into something that sustains life. Killing takes a living thing and makes it into a collection of dead things. The skills are incompatible. You cannot be good at both.”

“You’re terrible at both,” said a legionary named Corvus, who was sitting on an overturned bucket beside the cook fire, mending a sandal with a piece of sinew and the focused attention of a man who had been mending the same sandal for three days and was beginning to suspect the sandal was winning.

“I am terrible at killing,” Macro agreed. “I am excellent at cooking. The lentils will prove it.”

They were in the camp’s common area — a space between the tents that served as mess hall, social club, and the specific, informal parliament where the enlisted men of the century conducted the real business of military life: complaining, gambling, gossiping, and the careful, continuous negotiation of status and favour that determined who got the best sleeping spot, who got the first serving, who owed whom what and for how long.

Archimedes had not intended to be here. He had been walking — the restless, purposeless walking of a man whose mind was full and whose body needed to move — and the smell had caught him. The smell of lentils. The smell of garlic. The smell of something being cooked with care, with attention, with the love that a good cook puts into food not because the food deserves it but because the cooking deserves it, because the act of transforming raw ingredients into something nourishing is, in itself, a kind of prayer.

He had followed the smell, and the smell had led him here, and Macro had handed him a bowl without asking who he was or why he was there, because a cook who asks questions before serving is a cook who has misunderstood the fundamental nature of hospitality.

The lentils were good. Better than good. They were — Archimedes searched for the word and found it in a language he did not usually use for food — transcendent. Not because the ingredients were special — lentils, garlic, rosemary, a splash of vinegar, a drizzle of oil that was probably olive but might have been something else, something local, pressed from a fruit that grew on the doubled trees of the island’s interior. The ingredients were ordinary. The cooking was not. The cooking was the work of a man who understood that the difference between a meal and a miracle was attention, and attention was the one resource that was always available, even on a volcanic island at the edge of the known world where the rain was red and the ground was warm and the sky was wrong.

“You’re the coroner,” Macro said. Not a question. He was a large man — broad, heavy, the kind of body that was built for lifting and carrying and the specific, physical labour of a kitchen rather than the physical labour of a battlefield. His hands were scarred — not from combat but from burns, cuts, the accumulated damage of twenty years of working with fire and blades and the unpredictable physics of boiling liquid. “The one who talks to the dead.”

“I examine the dead. I don’t talk to them.”

“Decimus says you talk to Nerva. Decimus says Nerva talks back.”

Archimedes looked at his lentils. The lentils looked back, in the way that food looks back when you’re eating it in a place where the boundary between the living and the dead is not as firm as you’d like.

“Nerva’s situation is — unusual,” he said.

“Everything on this island is unusual. The rain is unusual. The ground is unusual. The fact that I can grow rosemary in volcanic ash is unusual. I’ve stopped being surprised by unusual. I’ve started being surprised by ordinary.” Macro ladled more lentils into Archimedes’ bowl without being asked. “Eat. You’re too thin. A man who works with the dead should eat like the living, or the dead will think he’s one of them.”

Corvus snorted. “That’s not how it works.”

“How would you know? You’ve never been dead.”

“Neither have you.”

“I’ve eaten army rations. It’s close.”

The exchange was — Archimedes recognised it — ritual. The banter of men who had been together long enough to develop a shared language, a shared rhythm, a conversational pattern that was as much a part of their daily routine as the morning muster or the evening watch. The banter was not about the content — it was about the connection. The sound of two men confirming, through the medium of insults and jokes and the specific, affectionate contempt that soldiers reserved for their closest companions, that they were still here, still alive, still capable of the ordinary human act of talking nonsense over a meal.

Archimedes ate his lentils and listened and felt something he had not felt in — he calculated — years. Not happiness. Not comfort. Something simpler. The feeling of being in a place where people were being people, where the conversation was about food and sandals and the relative merits of killing versus cooking, where the horror of the island was, for a moment, held at bay by the powerful magic of a good meal shared with strangers.

“The blood,” Macro said, stirring. “The rain. The — whatever it is that’s happening here. You understand it?”

“I’m beginning to.”

“Can you explain it? In words a cook would understand?”

Archimedes considered. He had explained métronurgy to Flaminius in military terms — mechanisms, costs, strategic implications. He had explained it to himself in mathematical terms — equations, probabilities, the specific algebra of branching and collapsing. He had not explained it in cook’s terms.

“Imagine,” he said, “that every time you cook a meal, a version of you in another kitchen cooks the same meal but burns it. And the burned meal — the smoke, the ash, the ruined food — appears in your kitchen. On your counter. Warm. Smelling of failure. And the burned meal remembers being cooked. It remembers the moment the oil got too hot, the moment the garlic turned black, the moment everything went wrong. And it’s angry. At you. Because you got it right and it didn’t, and the difference between your meal and its meal is not skill or luck but — choice. The choice of which kitchen gets to be real.”

Macro stopped stirring. He looked at Archimedes with the expression of a man who has just been told something that makes perfect sense and is therefore terrifying.

“That’s métronurgy,” Archimedes said. “Except instead of meals, it’s lives. And instead of burned food, it’s blood. And instead of appearing on your counter, it falls from the sky.”

“And the blood remembers.”

“The blood remembers everything.”

Macro was quiet for a moment. Then he picked up the ladle and resumed stirring, and the stirring was — Archimedes noticed — slightly different. Slower. More deliberate. The stirring of a man who was thinking about something while his hands did the work his mind was too occupied to direct.

“My grandmother,” Macro said, “used to say that every meal you cook is a conversation with the dead. The recipe came from someone. The technique came from someone. The ingredients grew in soil that was fed by — by the dead. Everything we eat is the dead, transformed. And the cooking is the transformation. The cooking is the act of taking what was and making it into what is. And the eating is the act of taking what is and making it into what will be.”

“Your grandmother was a philosopher.”

“My grandmother was a cook. Cooks are philosophers. We just use better ingredients.”

Corvus looked up from his sandal. “Your grandmother also said that a man who doesn’t finish his lentils will be haunted by the ghost of the lentil that died for nothing.”

“My grandmother was also superstitious. The two are not incompatible.”

Archimedes finished his lentils. He set the bowl down. He looked at Macro — this large, scarred, gentle man who cooked lentils in a war zone and philosophised about the dead and fed strangers without asking questions — and he felt the feeling again. The simple feeling. The feeling of being in a place where people were being people.

“Thank you,” he said. “For the meal.”

“Don’t thank me. Thank the lentils. They did the hard part. I just provided the heat.”

Archimedes stood. His knees protested. He ignored them. He walked away from the cook fire, back into the camp, back into the grey light and the copper smell and the whispers from the earth, and the feeling faded — not disappeared, not ended, but faded, the way warmth fades when you step away from a fire, gradually, reluctantly, the heat leaving the skin in increments that you feel but cannot stop.

He carried the feeling with him. He carried it the way he carried everything — in his blood, in his bones, in the durable architecture of a man who had learned that the things worth carrying were not the things that were easy to hold but the things that were necessary to hold, and the necessity was its own kind of strength.

The lentils sat in his stomach, warm and solid and real.

The dead whispered beneath his feet.

The living cooked above them.

And the cooking was, in its way, a kind of answer — not to the Deathless Proof, not to the question of who lived and who died and who decided, but to the smaller, more immediate question of how you continued. How you put one foot in front of the other in a world where the ground was full of the dead and the sky was full of their blood and the only thing between you and the void was a bowl of lentils and the man who made them.

You continued by eating. By cooking. By the daily, unremarkable act of transforming the dead into the living, one meal at a time.

It was not enough. It was never enough.

But the lentils were good, and the cook was kind, and the kindness was real, and the real was — in a world of doubles and branches and the infinite multiplication of selves — the rarest thing there was.
