

Chapter Nine: The Informants


They did not go straight back to the Unwritten’s cavern.


Sarai led him through a different route — longer, more circuitous, through corridors that doubled back on themselves and passages so narrow that Naram had to turn sideways to pass. She was being careful. More careful than before. The tomb had changed something — not just what they knew but how they moved, the way a soldier moves differently after the first arrow flies. Before the tomb, they had been investigators. After the tomb, they were targets.


“He knows,” Sarai said as they walked. Her voice was low, barely above a whisper, though there was no one to hear them in these forgotten corridors. “Gilgamesh knows we were there. The books in the tomb — they’re his eyes. His ears. Every biography he’s writing is a window into the world. He sees through the text. He hears through the words. And we were in his chamber, reading his books, standing in his presence.”


“But he can’t see you. You’re Unwritten.”


“He can’t see me. But he can see you. Your biography is in the Catalog. Your Blank Book is being written right now, this moment, documenting every step you take, every word you speak. Gilgamesh has access to the Blank Books — he’s part of the system that writes them. Which means he knows where you are. He knows what you’ve seen. He knows what you know.”


The thought was like swallowing ice. Naram had been so focused on the discovery — the empty tomb, the writing books, the revelation that Gilgamesh had become text — that he hadn’t considered the implications for his own visibility. He was a documented person in a documented world, and the documentation was the enemy’s weapon.


“Then why hasn’t he acted?” Naram asked. “If he knows I was in the tomb, if he knows what I’ve learned — why wait? Why not move the execution up? Why give me two more days?”


“Because the execution is written. ‘On the third day, Naram will be executed for heresy.’ The third day. Not the second. Not the first. The third. Gilgamesh wrote it in your biography in Ink of First Blood, and First Blood writing is absolute. It cannot be changed, not even by the author. The words are fixed. The timeline is fixed. Gilgamesh bound himself to the schedule when he wrote the sentence. He cannot kill you before the third day any more than you can die before the third day. The writing holds.”


“The writing protects me?”


“For two more days. After that, the writing kills you. It’s a double-edged blade. The same power that makes the death sentence real also makes the timeline real. You are, until sunset on the third day, unkillable. Not invulnerable — you can be hurt, imprisoned, restrained. But you cannot die. The Archive won’t allow it. The biography says the third day, and the biography is law.”


Naram absorbed this. The idea that the death sentence was also a shield — that the same words that condemned him also protected him — was the kind of paradox that the Archive seemed to specialize in. A system where truth was power and power was truth and both could save you and destroy you depending on which direction you were facing.


“So I have two days of immunity.”


“Two days of immunity from death. Not from everything else. Gilgamesh can still act against you. He can have the masters arrest you. He can have Tabni confine you. He can close the corridors, seal the passages, cut off your access to the Restricted Section and the Catalog and everything else you need to investigate. He can make your last two days a prison. He just can’t make them your last.”


“Then we need to move fast.”


“We need to move smart. Fast and smart are not the same thing, and in the Archive, smart usually wins.”




They stopped in a chamber Naram had never seen — a small room, barely larger than a closet, carved into the rock at a junction of three corridors. The room had been used for storage once — empty shelves lined the walls, and the floor was covered in a fine layer of dust that showed no footprints except theirs. Sarai sat on the floor, cross-legged, and gestured for Naram to do the same.


“Before we go back,” she said, “I need to tell you something. About what happened in the tomb.”


“I was there. I saw it.”


“You saw the books. You saw the writing. You didn’t see what happened to the walls.”


Naram waited.


“When we entered the tomb, the text on the corridor walls — the living text, the Archive’s immune system — it parted for me. Because I’m Unwritten. It can’t see me. But it didn’t just part. It reached. Toward you. I saw it. The characters stretched, elongated, like fingers trying to touch you. The Archive wasn’t just aware of you in that corridor. It was trying to communicate with you.”


“Communicate what?”


“I don’t know. But I’ve been in those corridors dozens of times. The text has never done that before. Not for anyone. The Archive treats intruders like infections — it walls them off, pushes them out. It doesn’t reach for them.” She paused. “Enheduanna told you the Archive chose you. I think she’s right. And I think the choosing is more active than she realizes.”


The thought should have been comforting — the idea that the vast, ancient consciousness of the Archive was on his side. But it wasn’t comforting. It was terrifying. Because the Archive was also the system that Gilgamesh controlled. The Archive was the body, and Gilgamesh was the parasite inside the body, and if the body was reaching for Naram, the parasite would notice.


A sound. Distant. The scrape of stone on stone — the sound of a corridor rearranging itself, of the Archive shifting its architecture in response to a command. Sarai went still. Not the stillness of fear — the stillness of a predator hearing a branch snap. Her clear eyes moved to the corridor entrance. Her hand went to her belt, where she kept a small knife — not a weapon, a tool, the kind of knife you use to cut thread or scrape clay, but held now with the grip of someone who had learned that any edge could be a weapon if the need was sharp enough.


The sound came again. Closer. The walls of their small chamber vibrated — a fine tremor, barely perceptible, the kind of vibration you feel in your teeth before you feel it in your hands.


“The corridors are moving,” Sarai whispered. “He’s rearranging the deep Archive. Closing passages. Opening new ones. He’s looking for you.”


“Can he find us here?”


“He can’t see me. He can see you. But these corridors are old — older than Gilgamesh, older than the Archive’s awareness. They were here when the cave was just a cave. The Archive’s consciousness doesn’t extend fully into the oldest passages. It’s like — peripheral vision. He can sense movement but not detail.”


The tremor stopped. The silence that followed was not the Archive’s usual breathing silence. It was the silence of something listening. Something waiting. Something that had heard a noise and was deciding whether to investigate.


They sat in the dusty room, in the lamplight, and did not move. Did not speak. Did not breathe louder than necessary. Two people hiding in the margins of a living library while a dead god searched the corridors for a scribe who had seen too much.


A minute passed. Two. The silence held. Then the breathing resumed — the Archive’s normal rhythm, the slow, patient respiration of a system that had decided the noise was nothing. Or had decided to wait.


“We need to go,” Sarai said. “Different route. The passage we came through may not exist anymore.”


She stood. She moved to the corridor entrance and pressed her hand against the wall — not reading it, feeling it. The way a sailor feels the wind. The way a blind person feels a face. Her fingers traced the stone, reading the vibrations, the temperature, the subtle variations in texture that told her which passages were open and which had been sealed.


“This way,” she said. “Stay close. Don’t touch the walls — if the text is reaching for you, touching it might tell Gilgamesh exactly where you are.”


They moved. Through corridors that were narrower than before, darker, the stone rougher. Sarai navigated by touch and instinct, her bare feet silent on the stone, her body moving through the dark with the fluid certainty of someone who had been navigating these passages for years. Naram followed, his hand hovering near the wall but not touching, his breath controlled, his heart hammering with the specific rhythm of a man who was being hunted by a building.


They emerged into a wider passage. The air changed — cooler, fresher, the smell of the deep springs replacing the mineral smell of old stone. They were near the Unwritten’s territory. Safe. Or as safe as anyone could be in a library that was alive and hungry and controlled by a consciousness that wanted them dead.


“Tomorrow,” Sarai said. “Tomorrow we go to the tomb again. But not through the corridors. Through the ventilation. Enki’s system. The shafts are too narrow for the text to follow, too physical for the Archive’s consciousness to monitor. It’s the one path Gilgamesh can’t close.”


“And when we get there?”


“We find the truth. The truth about what Gilgamesh is building. The truth about why the Archive chose you. The truth that will either save us or kill us.” She paused. The faintest flicker of humor in her clear eyes — the same flicker Naram had seen before, the flower growing through the crack. “Probably both. In my experience, truth usually does both.”


They sat in the dusty room, in the lamplight, in the breathing dark, and for a moment — just a moment — the weight of the conspiracy and the death sentence and the three-hundred-year-old enemy lifted, and they were just two people sitting on a floor, sharing a thought that made them both feel slightly less doomed.


Then the moment passed. The weight returned. The clock resumed.


“Let’s go,” Sarai said. “The others need to know what we found.”


They went. Through the corridors. Through the dark. Through the margins of a living library that was, even now, writing the next line of Naram’s biography, documenting his movements, recording his words, feeding the information to a consciousness that had been dead for three hundred years and was very much alive.


Two days. The clock was running.


But the immunity held. The death sentence was also a shield. And Naram, walking through the dark with a woman who had no past and a plan that had no guarantee, felt something that was not quite hope and not quite courage but was, perhaps, the raw material from which both could be built.


Determination. The decision to act. The choice to fight, not because victory was certain but because surrender was unacceptable.


It would have to be enough.




End of Chapter Nine
