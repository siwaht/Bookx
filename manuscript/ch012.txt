

Chapter Eleven: The Other Victims


Naram spent the rest of the morning in the Catalog, moving shelf to shelf with the methodical urgency of a man defusing a bomb. He checked every book in the Archive section first — the scribes, the people he knew, the people who ate lentils beside him and argued about desk assignments and complained about the ventilation. Then the Damascus section. Then Baghdad. Then Nineveh, Mosul, Aleppo, Basra.


The Catalog was different at this hour. The morning shift hadn’t started yet — the duty master wouldn’t ring the bells for another two hours — and the vast room was empty except for Naram and the books and the particular quality of silence that a library produces when it thinks no one is listening. Not true silence. The Archive never achieved true silence. There was always the breathing — the low, rhythmic pulse of air through the ventilation shafts, the expansion and contraction of stone corridors that behaved less like architecture and more like a ribcage. But layered beneath the breathing, in the Catalog specifically, there was something else. A whisper. The sound of books being written.


He’d never noticed it before. Or rather, he’d noticed it the way you notice the hum of blood in your own ears — as background, as the ambient noise of existence, as something so constant it becomes invisible. But now, standing in the Catalog with his new understanding of what the Archive was and what it did, the whisper was deafening. Every book on every shelf was producing it — a faint, continuous murmur of text being generated, of lives being documented, of the Archive’s vast and tireless machinery grinding forward through the biographies of everyone it had ever claimed.


Most of the whispers were slow. Lazy, almost. The sound of routine documentation — a meal eaten, a conversation had, a night of dreamless sleep. The Archive recording the ordinary moments of ordinary lives with the thoroughness of a bureaucracy that had confused comprehensiveness with importance.


But some of the whispers were fast. Urgent. The sound of a pen racing across paper, of a story accelerating toward its conclusion, of a biography being completed with the focused intensity of a predator closing on its prey.


Those were the ones Naram was looking for.


He learned to identify them by sound alone. He would stand at the end of a shelf, close his eyes, and listen. The slow whispers he ignored. The fast ones he followed — tracking the sound to its source, pulling the book from the shelf, opening it to the final pages. And there, in the Archive’s own handwriting — that precise, mechanical script that no human hand could produce — he would find the completion. The final chapter. The death.


The first one he found was a weaver in Aleppo. A woman named Fatima, sixty-three years old, who had spent her life making carpets with patterns so intricate that buyers sometimes wept when they saw them. Her biography was thick — four hundred pages of a life lived in color and texture and the particular satisfaction of work done well. The final page was being written as Naram watched. The ink appeared on the paper like blood seeping from a wound — slow, steady, inevitable. 
Fatima will develop a cough. The cough will worsen. On the fourteenth day, the cough will become pneumonia. On the seventeenth day, Fatima will die in her bed, surrounded by her carpets, which will be sold by her son-in-law to pay for the funeral.


Naram’s hands shook. He put the book back. He moved to the next shelf.


The second was a soldier in Baghdad. A young man — twenty-two, barely older than Naram — named Tariq, who had joined the army because his father had been a soldier and his grandfather had been a soldier and the weight of expectation was heavier than the weight of a rifle. His death was scheduled for a border skirmish. Not a battle — a skirmish. A nothing engagement, a footnote in a war that was itself a footnote, the kind of death that merited three lines in a newspaper and a flag on a coffin and nothing else. The Archive was writing it with the same care it gave to everything — every detail of the skirmish documented, every bullet trajectory calculated, the exact angle at which the shrapnel would enter Tariq’s neck specified to the degree.


The precision was the worst part. Not the deaths themselves — death was common, death was expected, death was the one biographical event that every life shared. But the precision. The specificity. The way the Archive documented each death with the loving attention of an artist signing a masterpiece. As if the deaths were not tragedies but achievements. As if the Archive was proud of them.


By midday, he had found twenty-three books being completed.


Twenty-three lives being written to their ends. Twenty-three people who would die in the next days or weeks, their deaths scheduled with the bureaucratic precision of a tax collection — dates, causes, circumstances, all predetermined, all written in the Archive’s own hand but guided by something that was not the Archive. By Gilgamesh. By the dead man who was not dead, who had become the writing itself, who sat at the center of the Archive like a spider at the center of a web and pulled the threads of lives he had never met.


The victims were varied. Weavers, soldiers, merchants, farmers. A child of seven in Mosul — a child, for God’s sake, whose biography was being completed with the same relentless thoroughness as the others, every detail of her short life documented with perfect accuracy, every scraped knee and bedtime story and moment of wonder at a butterfly recorded as if it mattered, as if the Archive cared about the beauty of a child’s life even as it prepared to end it.


The child was scheduled to die of a fever. Like Utu. Fever was the preferred method, it seemed — natural enough to avoid suspicion, common enough to avoid investigation. A fever that would come from nowhere, burn for three days, and end in a death that the family would mourn and the physicians would shrug at and no one would ever connect to a library buried under the Syrian desert.


Naram sat on the floor of the Catalog between the Mosul shelves and pressed his back against the stone and tried to breathe. The Archive breathed with him — or he breathed with it, he could no longer tell which — the rhythm of his lungs syncing with the rhythm of the ventilation shafts until he felt less like a person sitting in a library and more like a cell sitting in a body. The Archive’s body. The body that was eating people.


He thought about the child. He didn’t want to think about the child. He wanted to think about systems and conspiracies and the mechanics of Onomancy, because systems were abstract and abstractions were manageable and manageable things didn’t make you want to vomit on the floor of a three-thousand-year-old library. But the child kept coming back. Seven years old. A girl — the biography specified, with its usual thoroughness — named Kidu. She liked butterflies. She was afraid of dogs. She had a gap in her front teeth that she was proud of because her older brother had told her it meant she was brave, and she had believed him, because she was seven and seven-year-olds believe their older brothers the way scribes believe the Archive: completely, unquestioningly, with the absolute faith of someone who has not yet learned that the things they trust can lie.


Kidu would die on a Tuesday. The biography specified this too. A Tuesday in late autumn, when the air in Mosul smelled of woodsmoke and the river ran low and the fever would seem, to everyone who witnessed it, like the most ordinary thing in the world. A child’s fever. A tragedy, yes, but a common tragedy. The kind of thing that happened. The kind of thing that had always happened. The kind of thing that no one questioned because questioning it would mean questioning the fundamental fairness of a universe that allowed seven-year-olds to die of fevers, and most people preferred not to question that, because the answer was too large and too dark and too close to the truth that Naram was now sitting with on the floor of the Catalog.


The truth that the universe was not unfair. The universe was indifferent. But the Archive was not indifferent. The Archive was deliberate. The Archive — Gilgamesh — chose these deaths. Selected them. Scheduled them with the care of a gardener pruning roses, cutting away the living to feed the growth of something else. Something that Gilgamesh was building. Something that required the specific, documented, thoroughly narrated deaths of specific people.


Not random people. Naram was beginning to see the pattern. The twenty-three victims were not random. They were — connected. Not to each other, not in any obvious way, but to the Archive. Each of them had appeared in the biographies of other people. Each of them was a node in a network of narratives, a character who showed up in multiple stories, whose life touched other lives in ways that the Archive had documented and Gilgamesh had noticed. The weaver whose carpets were bought by a merchant whose biography mentioned a diplomat whose biography mentioned a general. The soldier whose death in a skirmish would appear in the biographies of his commanding officer, his fellow soldiers, his family, his enemies. The child whose fever would be mentioned in the biographies of her parents, her brother, her doctor, her neighbors.


Each death was not one death. Each death was a narrative event that rippled through dozens of biographies, touching them, changing them, adding weight and truth and power to the Archive’s documentation of the world. Each death was a stone dropped in a pond, and the ripples were the real harvest — not the life consumed but the narrative energy generated, the truth-power that flowed from the documentation of a death into every biography that mentioned it.


Gilgamesh was not just eating lives. He was farming stories.


Naram stood up. His legs were stiff. His eyes were dry from the Catalog’s dusty air, and his throat was tight with something that was not dust. He put the child’s book back on the shelf. He did it gently, the way you put a sleeping child back in a crib, and he hated himself for the gentleness because the book was not a child, the book was a weapon, the book was a death sentence written in the handwriting of a library that had confused documentation with divinity.


He moved on. More shelves. More books. More deaths.


He found more. A merchant in Basra, a farmer outside Nineveh, a midwife in Damascus. Each death unique in its details and identical in its purpose. Each life being ended not because it had reached its natural conclusion but because its conclusion served the narrative needs of something larger, something hungrier.


Naram made a list. He wrote it on a scrap of papyrus he’d taken from the scriptorium’s waste bin, using a stub of charcoal instead of ink — because ink was the Archive’s medium, and anything written in ink was visible to the Archive, and Naram was learning to be careful about what the Archive could see.


Twenty-three names. Twenty-three death dates. Five scribes marked for heresy. Eighteen civilians marked for fever.


He folded the list and put it in his pocket. He left the Catalog. He went to find Sarai.




The walk from the Catalog to the Unwritten’s cavern took twenty minutes through the service corridors. Naram used every minute to think about Gilgamesh farming stories — planting deaths, harvesting narratives, feeding the crop back into the soil to grow more. An agricultural model of divinity. A god grown from the ground up, fertilized with human lives.


He was nineteen. He was a junior scribe. Three days ago, his biggest concern had been a desk assignment. Now he was carrying twenty-three lives in his pocket and the only tool he had was a stub of charcoal and the ability to write lies that a sentient library might believe.


The Unwritten’s cavern was busier than before. More people — or the same people, more active. Naram was learning to read the Unwritten’s rhythms, the way you learn to read the weather or the mood of a room. When they were calm, they moved slowly, drifting through their tasks with the unhurried patience of people who had nowhere to be and no past to return to. When they were agitated, they moved faster, their clear eyes sharper, their conversations more urgent.


They were agitated now.


Sarai met him at the entrance. She looked — not frightened. The Unwritten didn’t do frightened, not in the way that people with full memories did frightened. Fear required context, required the memory of past dangers to give present dangers their weight. The Unwritten feared in the present tense only — a pure, contextless anxiety, like the fear of a child who doesn’t understand what’s happening but knows it’s bad.


“Something’s changed,” she said. “The Archive is — moving. Growing. New corridors appeared overnight. Three of them. Leading down. Toward the tomb.”


“The Archive is making room,” Naram said. He thought of Adapa’s words: 
It’s looking for something. Or making room for something.


“Making room for what?”


“For the completions. Twenty-three of them. Maybe more — I only checked a fraction of the Catalog. Gilgamesh is completing biographies on a scale I don’t think he’s attempted before. He needs space. He needs — infrastructure. The Archive is building it for him.”


Sarai absorbed this. Her face did the thing it did when she was processing information — a brief stillness, like a pond settling after a stone, and then clarity. “The executions. The scribes. When?”


“Sunset, third day. Tomorrow.”


“And the civilians?”


“Spread out. Over the next two weeks. But the scribes are first. We’re the priority.”


“Because you know.”


“Because we know. Or because we might know. Or because Gilgamesh can’t take the risk that we’ll figure it out and do something about it.” Naram pulled out his list. “Five scribes. Me, Utu, and three others I don’t know personally. All junior or mid-level. All assigned to biographies that touch on the Damascus trade network — the same network where I found the falsified files.”


Sarai took the list. She read it with the careful attention of someone for whom reading was a skill she’d had to relearn after the erasure — not the effortless scanning of a trained scribe but the deliberate, word-by-word processing of someone who had lost the shortcuts and had to take the long way around.


“The Damascus network,” she said. “Khalid al-Rashid. The honest merchant who isn’t honest.”


“The thread I pulled. The thread that unraveled everything.”


“Not everything. Not yet.” She handed the list back. “You need to talk to the other scribes. The ones on this list. They need to know what’s happening. They need to know they’re targets.”


“And then what?”


“And then we need a plan. A real plan. Not just investigation — action. We need to stop the completions. All of them. Not just yours. Not just Utu’s. All twenty-three. And we need to do it in less than two days.”


“How?”


Sarai looked at him. Her clear eyes held his, and in them Naram saw something he hadn’t seen before — not the emptiness of erasure but the fullness of purpose. A woman who had spent years — decades — living in the margins, invisible, purposeless, surviving but not living. And now, for the first time since she’d erased herself, she had a reason to act. A reason to fight. A reason to step out of the margins and into the text.


“I don’t know yet,” she said. “But I know where to start. The forgery.”


“What forgery?”


“The one I’m going to teach you. The one skill that the Archive fears more than any other. The ability to write a lie so convincing that the Archive accepts it as truth.”


The cavern was quiet. The other Unwritten had stopped their tasks and were watching — the same attentive stillness Naram had seen before, the stillness of people who recognized that something important was happening and wanted to be present for it.


“The cost,” Naram said. “You said there’s always a cost.”


“There is. Forgery costs more than honest writing. Much more. To fool the Archive — to write a lie that a sentient, three-thousand-year-old library accepts as truth — you have to believe the lie yourself. Completely. Absolutely. You have to rewrite your own understanding of reality to match the forgery. And the Archive takes the difference as payment.”


“The difference between what I know is true and what I write.”


“Yes. If you write ‘Naram is ill’ when you know you’re healthy, the Archive takes the knowledge of your health. You forget what it feels like to be well. If you write ‘Naram is in his chambers’ when you’re standing in the Catalog, the Archive takes your memory of the Catalog. The bigger the lie, the bigger the cost. And the cost is always memory. Always.”


“My childhood,” Naram said. He was thinking of Enheduanna’s warning. 
If you had written ten pages of lies, you might have lost her face. Her name.


“Your childhood. Your mother. Your training. Everything that makes you who you are. The forgery eats backward — it takes the oldest memories first, the foundations, the things you built yourself on. By the time you’ve written enough forgeries to fool the Archive consistently, you’re — well. You’re like us.”


She gestured at the cavern. At the clear-eyed, empty-memoried people who lived in the margins. At Adapa, trembling in his corner. At the old woman by the fire, stirring soup she couldn’t remember learning to make.


“That’s the price,” Sarai said. “That’s always been the price. The question is whether you’re willing to pay it.”


Naram thought of Utu. Of the child in Mosul. Of twenty-three names on a scrap of papyrus.


“Teach me,” he said.




End of Chapter Eleven
