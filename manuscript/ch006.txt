

Chapter Five: The Unwritten


Sarai took him to meet the others.


They went deeper — always deeper, in the Archive, as if truth and secrecy both lived at the bottom of things — through corridors that Naram was certain had not existed the last time he’d been in this part of the complex. The walls were rough, unfinished, the chisel marks still visible in the stone. Fresh corridors. New growth. The Archive expanding in the dark, reaching downward like roots seeking water.


“How many of you are there?” Naram asked.


“Enough. Not many. The erasure is — not something most people survive intact. The ones who do are the ones who had the least to lose. Or the ones who were desperate enough that losing everything felt like a fair trade.” She walked ahead of him, her bare feet silent on the stone. She moved like smoke — present but insubstantial, as if the air couldn’t quite decide whether she was there. “We live in the spaces the Archive doesn’t use. The abandoned corridors. The unfinished chambers. The places where the Archive grew and then forgot. It forgets, you know. Not the way people forget — not through loss, but through abundance. It has so much to remember that the empty spaces fall out of its attention. We live in its blind spots.”


“And it can’t find you.”


“It can’t see us. There’s a difference. Finding implies looking. The Archive doesn’t look for us because it doesn’t know we exist. We are — how to explain it. You know how a book has margins? The white space around the text? The margins are part of the book, but they’re not part of the story. We are the margins. Present but unwritten. Part of the Archive but not part of its narrative.”


They passed through a section where the corridor widened into something that might once have been a storage chamber. The walls here were covered in a thin film of moisture — condensation from the deep springs that fed the Archive’s water supply, the same springs that Enki tapped for the ventilation system. Mushrooms grew in the cracks between the stones, pale and luminous, giving off a faint blue-green glow that made the corridor look like the inside of a tide pool. Sarai plucked one as she passed, examined it, put it in a pouch at her belt.


“Edible?” Naram asked.


“Medicinal. We have a woman — Ninlil — who was a healer before she erased herself. She doesn’t remember her training, but her hands remember. She can set a bone, stitch a wound, brew a fever-tea. She does it all by feel, by instinct, by the muscle memory that the erasure can’t reach. The mushrooms are for pain. We have a lot of pain down here.”


“Physical pain?”


“All kinds. The erasure doesn’t take pain. It takes the context for pain — the memory of what caused it, the understanding of why it hurts. But the pain itself stays. Phantom pain, you might call it. Pain without a source. Grief without a loss. Fear without a threat. The body remembers what the mind forgets, and the body’s memories are not gentle.”


She said this matter-of-factly, the way you describe the weather or the price of grain. Not complaining. Not seeking sympathy. Just stating the terms of existence for people who had traded their pasts for their freedom and were still paying the interest.


They descended another staircase — rougher than the ones in the inhabited levels, the steps uneven, carved by hands that were either unskilled or in a hurry. The air grew warmer. The mushroom-glow faded, replaced by the orange flicker of oil lamps set into niches in the wall — lamps that burned with a steady, smokeless flame.


“Enki’s design?” Naram asked, nodding at a lamp.


“Enki doesn’t know we exist. These are ours. Copied from his design, yes — one of our people was an engineer’s apprentice, before. She doesn’t remember the theory, but she remembers the shapes. The proportions. The way the wick needs to sit in the oil to burn clean. She builds them from scraps — clay from the Archive’s waste bins, oil skimmed from the kitchen stores, wicks braided from linen threads pulled from old robes.” Sarai paused at a junction where three corridors met. She chose the left one without hesitation, navigating by a knowledge that was not quite memory and not quite instinct but something in between — the body’s map of a place the mind had forgotten learning. “We are scavengers, Naram. We live on what the Archive discards. Its waste is our sustenance. Its forgotten spaces are our home. Its blind spots are our safety. We are the Archive’s shadow — shaped by it, dependent on it, invisible to it.”


“That sounds like a prison.”


“It sounds like a life. All lives are shaped by the systems they exist within. The difference is that we chose this system. We chose the margins. We chose the shadow. Most people don’t get to choose.”


Naram thought about that. He thought about his own life — the life he hadn’t chosen, the life that had been assigned to him by the accident of being born in the Archive, of being identified as a potential scribe at age seven, of being trained and shaped and molded into a tool for the institution’s purposes. He had never chosen to be a scribe. He had never chosen to live underground. He had never chosen lentils.


But he had chosen to investigate. He had chosen to follow the ash. He had chosen to come here, to the margins, to the people who lived in the spaces between the words.


Maybe that was enough. Maybe choice didn’t have to be grand to be real.


They turned a corner and entered a chamber that was larger than Naram expected — a natural cavern, it looked like, that the Archive had incorporated into its structure the way a tree incorporates a rock, growing around it rather than through it. The ceiling was high and rough, studded with mineral deposits that caught the lamplight and threw it back in fragments. The floor had been smoothed — by feet, not by tools — and covered with rugs that looked like they’d been woven from scraps of linen and wool salvaged from the Archive’s textile stores. Along one wall, someone had built shelves from scavenged wood — not the fine cedar of the Archive’s stacks but rough planks, probably from packing crates, nailed together with the pragmatic ugliness of furniture built for function rather than beauty. The shelves held jars, pouches, folded cloth, tools. The inventory of a community that survived on what it could steal and what it could make.


There were people here.


Not many. A dozen, maybe fifteen. They sat in small groups, or alone, or moved between tasks that Naram couldn’t immediately identify — mending clothes, preparing food over a small fire that vented through a crack in the ceiling, sorting through piles of objects that looked like they’d been scavenged from the Archive’s storage rooms. The scene was domestic, almost cozy, like a village market compressed into a cave.


But the people were wrong.


Not wrong in any way Naram could point to. They looked normal — men and women of various ages, dressed in the plain linen robes that everyone in the Archive wore. They moved normally. They spoke normally, in low voices, in conversations that sounded ordinary. But there was something missing. A quality. A — weight. They moved through the space like reflections in water, present but somehow less than present, as if the world had trouble holding onto them.


And their eyes. Every one of them had Sarai’s eyes. Clear. Empty. The eyes of people who had looked inward and found the shelves bare.


“This is everyone?” Naram asked.


“This is the ones who are well enough to be here. There are others. Deeper. The ones who lost too much.” Sarai’s voice was careful. The voice of someone describing a wound they’d learned to live with but not to forget. “The erasure takes differently from different people. Some lose their pasts but keep their — their selves. Their personalities. Their ability to function. Those are the ones you see here. Others lose more. They lose language. They lose the ability to recognize faces. They lose the understanding that they are people at all. Those ones — we care for them. We feed them. We keep them warm. But they are not — they are not here. Not in any way that matters.”


A man approached them. He was old — genuinely old, not Archive-old — with a face like crumpled paper and hands that shook with a fine, constant tremor. His eyes were the clearest Naram had seen. So clear they were almost transparent, like glass, like something you could see through to the empty space behind.


“New one?” the man said to Sarai.


“Visitor. Not Unwritten. He needs help.”


The old man looked at Naram. The clear eyes moved across his face the way a scribe’s eyes move across a text — reading, assessing, cataloging. “He’s in the books. I can feel it. The Archive knows him. He smells like ink.”


“Everyone in the Archive smells like ink,” Sarai said.


“Not like this. He smells like — attention. The Archive is watching him. Right now. Through the walls. Through the stone. It knows he’s here.” The old man’s trembling hands reached toward Naram, stopped, pulled back. “Dangerous. For us. If the Archive is watching him, it might see us through him. Like looking through a window.”


“It can’t see us, Adapa. You know that.”


“It couldn’t see us. Past tense. Things change. The Archive changes. It’s been — restless. Growing. Reaching. I feel it in the walls. New corridors. New chambers. It’s looking for something. Or making room for something.” The old man — Adapa — shook his head. The tremor in his hands spread to his shoulders, a full-body shiver that passed through him like a wave. “Be careful, Sarai. The margins are getting narrower.”


He shuffled away. Sarai watched him go with an expression that Naram couldn’t read — not because it was complex but because it was simple. Sadness. Plain, undecorated sadness. The sadness of someone watching a person they care about disappear by inches.


“Adapa was a Master,” she said quietly. “Before. One of the best. He erased himself forty years ago — or fifty, or sixty, he can’t remember and neither can I. He was the one who taught me how to do it. How to unwrite yourself. The technique. The cost.” She paused. “He used to be brilliant. Now he’s — you saw. The tremor. The paranoia. The erasure took his steadiness. His confidence. His ability to trust his own perceptions. He sees threats everywhere because he can’t remember what’s real and what’s fear.”


“And the others?”


“Various. Some are like me — functional, mostly. Missing our pasts but present in the present. Some are like Adapa — declining. Losing more every year, even though the erasure was a one-time event. As if the absence keeps growing. A hole that gets wider.” She turned to face him. “This is what I’m offering you, Naram. Not a solution. A tool. We can go where the Archive can’t see. We can enter the Restricted Section. We can find out who accessed your First Blood. But the tool comes with a warning: the people who use it pay a price that never stops being collected.”


“I’m not asking to become Unwritten.”


“No. You’re asking us to help you. Which means you’re asking us to risk the only safety we have — our invisibility — for a scribe we don’t know, against an enemy we can’t see, in a fight we didn’t choose.” She smiled. It was a small smile, crooked, the smile of someone who has forgotten most of the things worth smiling about and is making do with what’s left. “But I’ll do it. Because the handwriting in your book belongs to Gilgamesh. And Gilgamesh is the reason I’m here. The reason all of us are here. He built this place. He made the rules. He created the system that trapped us. And if he’s alive — if he’s somehow still writing, still controlling, still reaching out from whatever death or not-death he’s found — then he’s my enemy too.”


“Why?”


“Because I was his student. Before I was Unwritten. Before I erased myself. I was Gilgamesh’s last apprentice. And the thing I was running from — the cruel husband, the life I couldn’t escape — Gilgamesh arranged it. He chose my husband for me. He wrote it into my biography. He decided who I would marry and what my life would be, and when I objected, he told me it was for the good of the Archive, and when I tried to leave, he told me the Archive doesn’t let people leave, and when I erased myself to escape, he—”


She stopped. Her clear eyes were bright. Not with tears — the Unwritten didn’t cry, Naram would learn later, because crying required a depth of emotional memory that the erasure took — but with something harder. Anger. The kind of anger that survives the loss of everything else because it lives not in the mind but in the body, in the flinching hands, in the muscles that remember what the memory has forgotten.


“He let me go,” she said. “He could have stopped me. He had the power. But he let me go. And I’ve spent every year since then wondering why. What was his plan? What did he see in my future that made my erasure useful to him? Because Gilgamesh never did anything without a reason. And his reasons were never kind.”


The cavern was quiet. The other Unwritten had stopped their tasks and were watching — not with curiosity but with the particular attention of people who have heard this story before and are waiting to see if it ends differently this time.


It didn’t. Sarai straightened. The anger receded, not gone but controlled, banked like a fire that would burn for a long time.


“I’ll help you,” she said. “We’ll go to the Restricted Section tonight. We’ll find out who took your First Blood. And then we’ll go to Gilgamesh’s tomb. Because if his hand is writing in your book, then his tomb is either empty or it’s not a tomb at all. And either way, we need to know.”


Naram looked at the Unwritten — the clear-eyed, empty-memoried, invisible people who lived in the margins of a living library — and felt something he hadn’t expected to feel.


Hope. Small, irrational, fragile. The kind of hope that grows in dark places, in the spaces between words, in the margins where the story hasn’t been written yet.


“Tonight,” he said.


“Tonight,” Sarai agreed. “But first — more tea. And you should eat something. You look like you haven’t eaten since yesterday.”


“I had lentils.”


“Everyone has lentils. I meant real food.” She led him to the fire, where an old woman — Unwritten, clear-eyed, silent — was stirring a pot that smelled of lamb and onions and something spicy that made Naram’s eyes water. “We steal from the kitchens. Old Shamhat pretends not to notice. I think she leaves things out for us. She’s been doing it for years. She can’t see us, but she knows we’re here. Some people are like that. They don’t need the Archive to tell them what’s true.”


The old woman ladled stew into a clay bowl and handed it to Naram without looking at him. The stew was hot and rich and tasted like the memory of every meal Naram had ever eaten that wasn’t lentils. He ate. Sarai sat beside him and drank her tea and said nothing.


The weaving woman — the one who hummed without melody — came and sat near them. She didn’t speak to Naram. She didn’t look at him. She sat and wove, her fingers moving through the threads with the automatic precision of a skill that had outlived the person who learned it. But her presence was deliberate. She had chosen to sit near the stranger. She had chosen proximity. And in a community where choice was the only currency that hadn’t been devalued by the erasure, the choice to sit near someone was a statement. An offering. A small, wordless act of welcome.


Others followed. The carver, still working his piece of cedar. A tall man with a scar across his forehead who carried himself like a soldier — straight-backed, alert, his clear eyes scanning the room with the habitual vigilance of someone who had spent a life watching for threats and couldn’t stop even though he couldn’t remember what the threats had been. A woman with grey hair and ink-stained hands — a scribe, once, like Naram, her fingers still bearing the marks of a profession her mind had forgotten.


They gathered around the fire. Not in a circle — the Unwritten didn’t do circles, Naram noticed. They arranged themselves in loose clusters, each person maintaining a careful distance from the others, close enough for company but far enough for solitude. The geometry of people who craved connection but had lost the social memories that made connection easy — the shared histories, the inside jokes, the accumulated trust of years of knowing someone. Without those memories, every interaction was a first meeting. Every conversation started from zero. Every relationship had to be built from the ground up, every day, because the foundation kept disappearing.


“They’re curious about you,” Sarai said. “You’re the first outsider we’ve had in — I don’t know. A long time. The last one was a cook’s apprentice who stumbled into our corridors by accident. We fed him, blindfolded him, and led him back to the kitchens. He thought he’d dreamed it.”


“And me?”


“You’re different. You’re here on purpose. You’re here because the Archive sent you — or pointed you toward us, which amounts to the same thing. And you’re here because someone is trying to kill you, which makes you one of us in the way that matters most.”


“What way is that?”


“The way of people the Archive has decided to consume. The way of people who have looked at the system and said: 
No. Not me. Not today.
 The specific details vary — erasure, execution, the slow grinding of a life spent in service to an institution that doesn’t care whether you live or die as long as the biographies get written. But the shape is the same. The Archive takes. We refuse. And the refusal costs us everything.”


She poured more tea. The fire crackled. The mineral deposits in the ceiling caught the light and scattered it in fragments across the cavern, tiny stars in a tiny sky, and Naram thought of Enki’s painted ceiling in the refectory — the orderly constellations, the contained and manageable night — and thought that this sky was better. Messier. Realer. A sky that didn’t pretend to be anything other than what it was: light reflected off stone, beauty found in accident, stars that told no stories because the people beneath them had forgotten how to read them.


The Unwritten moved around them in their quiet, weightless way, and the Archive breathed above them, vast and hungry and blind to the people living in its margins.


Three days. Two and a half, now. The clock was running.


But the stew was good. And the company was strange and sad and real. And for a few minutes, in a cave beneath a library beneath a desert, Naram allowed himself to stop counting.




End of Chapter Five
