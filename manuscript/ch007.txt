

Chapter Six: The Margins


Naram could not sleep.


This was not unusual — he had not slept properly since the clay turned to ash, since the world rearranged itself around the discovery that the institution he served was alive and hungry and not particularly concerned with his welfare. But the inability to sleep in the Unwritten’s cavern was different from the inability to sleep in his cot in the apprentice quarters. In the quarters, the sleeplessness was anxious — the racing mind, the clenched jaw, the body wound tight as a bowstring. Here, in the margins, the sleeplessness was something else. Something closer to wonder.


The cavern at night was beautiful.


He hadn’t expected that. The mineral deposits in the ceiling glowed with a blue-green phosphorescence that turned the cavern into something that looked like the inside of a seashell. The fire had burned down to embers, and in the combined light, the sleeping Unwritten looked like figures in a painting — still, peaceful, their clear eyes closed.


They slept close together. Not touching — the Unwritten maintained their careful distances even in sleep — but close. The geometry of people who needed proximity but couldn’t remember why.


Sarai was not sleeping. She sat at the edge of the cavern, near the corridor entrance, her back against the stone wall, her knees drawn up, her clear eyes watching the dark.


Naram went to her quietly. The silence of the cavern felt sacred.


“Can’t sleep?” Sarai said. She didn’t look at him.


“Too much to think about.”


“Thinking is overrated.” She shifted, making room on the stone ledge beside her. “Sit. If you’re going to be awake, you might as well be comfortable. Relatively.”


He sat. The stone was cold through his robe. The air was warm — the deep Archive was always warm — but the stone held its own temperature, indifferent to the air around it.


They sat in silence for a while. Not the uncomfortable silence of strangers — something else. The silence of two people who had seen the same terrible thing and didn’t need to name it. Naram was aware of her beside him — the warmth of her, the stillness, the way she held herself like someone who had learned to take up exactly as much space as she needed and no more. Her shoulder was close to his. Not touching. Close.


“What was the last thing you remember?” he asked. “Before the erasure.”


The question surprised her. He could tell by the way her breathing changed — a small hitch, barely perceptible.


“No one’s ever asked me that,” she said.


“You don’t have to answer.”


“I know I don’t.” She was quiet for a moment. The blue-green glow played across her face, softening the sharp edges, making her look less guarded. “The last thing I remember is a sound. Not a word. Not a voice. A sound. Like — humming. Someone humming. I don’t know who. I don’t know the melody. But my body knows it. Sometimes, when I’m falling asleep, my chest vibrates with it. This low, warm hum. And I feel safe. For about three seconds, I feel safe. Then the feeling goes and I’m just a woman sitting in a cave with no past.”


She turned to look at him. The clear eyes, in the blue-green light, were less clear. More human.


“What about you?” she said. “What have you lost?”


“A song. My mother used to sing it. The Archive took it as payment when it rejected my biography of Khalid. I can’t remember the melody. I can’t remember the words. I just know there was a song, and it’s gone.”


“A song,” Sarai said. And something in her voice shifted — a recognition, a resonance, as if the word had struck a chord in whatever remained of her erased self. “We’ve both lost music, then.”


“We’ve both lost music.”


She reached out and touched his hand. Not holding it — just touching. Her fingers on the back of his hand, light, cold, brief. The touch lasted two seconds, maybe three. Then she withdrew. But the place where her fingers had been stayed warm, as if the touching had generated something that neither of them carried alone.


“Tell me about Adapa,” Naram said.


Sarai was quiet for a moment. In the blue-green glow, her face was all planes and shadows, the face of a woman carved from the same stone as the walls around her.


Sarai told him about Adapa — the greatest scribe the Archive ever produced. Not powerful like Gilgamesh, but perceptive. Adapa had studied the Blank Books and discovered that the scribes’ biographies weren’t just documenting lives — they were authoring them. Write a man as “brave” and the man becomes braver. The adjectives weren’t descriptions. They were instructions. The subjects didn’t know. They thought their choices were their own.


“Adapa saw that as slavery,” Sarai said. “So he went to the masters. Proposed reforms. They refused. Because the human layer — the conviction, the interpretation — was the point. It was what gave the biographies power.”


“So he erased himself,” Naram said.


“He erased himself. Not to escape cruelty, like me. Because he couldn’t live with what he knew. He couldn’t hold a stylus knowing that every word was a chain.” She paused. “But the guilt stayed. The body remembers. His hands shake — the tremor is guilt, Naram. The body’s memory of a thousand small enslavements.”


She fell silent. The corridor breathed — the Archive’s respiration, distant but present, the sound of the system that Adapa had tried to reform and that had, in the end, consumed him the way it consumed everything.


“Why are you telling me this?” Naram asked.


“Because you need to understand what you’re fighting. Not just Gilgamesh. Not just the death sentence. The system. The whole system. The Archive is not a library that went wrong. The Archive is a library that works exactly as designed — a machine for controlling lives through documentation, for shaping people through writing, for consuming truth and converting it into power. Gilgamesh didn’t corrupt the Archive. Gilgamesh is the Archive’s logical conclusion. The endpoint of a system that treats people as texts and texts as tools and tools as weapons.”


“And if I stop Gilgamesh?”


“Then you’ve cut off the head. But the body remains. The system remains. The scribes will still write biographies with conviction. The biographies will still shape lives. The subjects will still be enslaved without their knowledge. Stopping Gilgamesh is necessary. But it’s not sufficient.”


Naram looked at the sleeping Unwritten. At Kidu, the child who drew shapes that were almost letters. At the weaving woman, her fingers still even in sleep, curled around invisible threads. At Adapa, trembling even in his dreams, his hands twitching with the ghost of a stylus.


“One thing at a time,” he said. “First I survive. Then I fix the world.”


Sarai almost smiled. The crooked smile, the one that was made from whatever was left after the erasure took the rest. “That’s what Adapa said. Forty years ago. Or fifty. Or sixty.”


“And look how well it worked out for him.”


“Yes. Look.” She turned her clear eyes back to the corridor. The dark mouth. The passage to the world above. “Get some sleep, Naram. Tomorrow we go to the Restricted Section. Tomorrow we start fighting. And fighting is easier when you’re rested.”


“I can’t sleep.”


“Then close your eyes and pretend. The body doesn’t know the difference. And the body, as I keep telling you, is smarter than the mind.”


He closed his eyes. He didn’t sleep. But he rested — his body against the cold stone, his mind against the warm dark, his thoughts against the silence of a place where people who had lost everything had found, if not peace, then at least each other.




He woke to the smell of tea.


He had slept after all. The body, as Sarai had promised, was smarter than the mind. He had slept without dreams — or without remembering dreams, which amounted to the same thing — and he woke feeling not rested but less exhausted, which was an improvement he was willing to accept.


The cavern was awake. The Unwritten moved through their morning routines with the quiet efficiency of people who had been doing this for years — tending the fire, heating water, preparing food from their scavenged stores. The weaving woman was already at her work, her fingers moving through the threads, her mouth humming its tuneless rhythm. The carver was carving. Kidu was drawing. The soldier-man was doing exercises in a corner — push-ups, stretches, the physical maintenance of a body that remembered being strong even though the mind had forgotten why strength mattered.


Sarai handed him a cup of tea. The same wild-herb blend — bitter, warm, tasting of earth and something faintly floral that he couldn’t identify.


“We have a problem,” she said.


“Just one?”


“A new one. Adapa had an episode last night. After you fell asleep. He woke up screaming — not words, just sound, the kind of sound a person makes when they’re afraid of something they can’t name. Ninlil — the healer — gave him the mushroom tea. It calmed him. But before it calmed him, he said something.”


“What?”


“He said: ‘The walls are listening. The walls have ears. The margins are closing.’ And then he said a name. A name I haven’t heard in forty years.”


“Whose name?”


“Gilgamesh.”


Naram set down his tea. The cup was warm in his hands. The cavern was warm around him. But the warmth felt thin, suddenly. Fragile. The warmth of a fire that could be extinguished.


“Adapa doesn’t remember Gilgamesh,” he said. “You told me. The erasure took everything.”


“The erasure took his memories. But the body remembers. And last night, in his sleep, in his terror, the body spoke a name that the mind has forgotten. Which means one of two things. Either Adapa is remembering — which would be unprecedented, which would mean the erasure is reversible, which would change everything we know about the Unwritten. Or—”


“Or the Archive is reaching into the margins. Into the blind spots. Into the places it’s not supposed to see.”


“Or that. Yes.”


They looked at each other. The cavern hummed with the quiet activity of the Unwritten’s morning. The fire crackled. The tea steamed. And beneath it all, beneath the stone and the silence and the careful, constructed safety of the margins, the Archive breathed. And the breathing was — was it louder? Was it closer? Was it the breathing of something that was reaching, stretching, extending its awareness into spaces it had previously ignored?


Or was that paranoia? Was that the same fear that made Adapa tremble, the fear of a threat that might not exist, the fear that was itself a kind of prison?


Naram didn’t know. He couldn’t know. And the not-knowing was, in its own way, the most frightening thing of all.


“Tonight,” he said. “The Restricted Section. We can’t wait.”


“Tonight,” Sarai agreed. “But carefully. More carefully than I planned. If the Archive is reaching into the margins — if the blind spots are shrinking — then we’re not as safe as we thought. And safety, for us, is not a luxury. It’s the only thing we have.”


She took his empty cup. She went to the fire. She poured more tea. And the morning continued, and the Unwritten moved through their routines, and the Archive breathed, and the margins held — for now, for today, for the hours that remained before the night came and the real work began.


Two and a half days. The clock was running.


And the walls, Adapa had said, were listening.




End of Chapter Six
