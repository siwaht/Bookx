

Chapter Thirteen: The Conspiracy


The margins were darker than Naram remembered.


Not the comfortable dark of the apprentice quarters at night, where the glow of Enki’s crystal shafts leaked through the ventilation slits and painted the ceiling in faint amber stripes. This was the dark of absence — the dark of corridors the Archive had grown and then forgotten, the way a body forgets the shape of a scar once the wound has healed. The stone here was rough, unfinished, the chisel marks still visible under his fingertips when he trailed his hand along the wall for guidance. Fresh corridors. Or old corridors. In the margins, the distinction didn’t hold. Time moved differently in spaces that no one was documenting.


He counted his steps. Sarai had told him: forty paces past the junction with the luminous mushrooms, then left at the crack that smelled of copper, then down — always down, in the Archive, as if secrecy and depth were the same thing — until the air changed from cool to warm and the stone under his feet went from rough to smooth, worn by the passage of bare feet that the Archive couldn’t see.


The Archive breathed around him. He could feel it — the slow, tidal rhythm of the ventilation system, the expansion and contraction of air through miles of stone corridors, the respiration of something vast and alive and, in these forgotten passages, asleep. Or not asleep. Inattentive. The breathing of a creature whose attention was elsewhere, whose awareness had contracted to the inhabited levels, the scriptorium, the Catalog, the places where scribes worked and biographies grew and the machinery of documentation hummed along in its ancient, hungry rhythm.


Here, in the margins, the breathing was fainter. A whisper instead of a sigh. The sound of the Archive’s unconscious — the autonomic processes that kept the air moving and the temperature stable even in corridors that no one was supposed to use. Naram found it almost peaceful. Almost. The way you find the silence of a sleeping predator almost peaceful, provided you don’t think too hard about what happens when it wakes.


Forty paces. Left at the copper-smell. Down.


He found Sarai’s chamber by smell. The wild-herb tea — bitter, earthy, with that faint floral note he still couldn’t identify — drifting through the dark like a thread he could follow. The smell meant warmth. The smell meant a door, or what passed for a door in the margins: a gap in the stone, narrow enough that you had to turn sideways to pass through, hung with a curtain of salvaged linen that had been washed so many times it was nearly transparent.


He pushed through the curtain and stopped.


Sarai’s room was small. Smaller than he’d expected, though he wasn’t sure what he’d expected — the Unwritten’s main cavern was large enough for a community, and some part of his mind had assumed that Sarai, as their leader, would have claimed a proportional space. She hadn’t. The room was barely ten paces across, carved from the same rough stone as the corridors, with a ceiling low enough that Naram could touch it if he raised his hand. The walls were bare. Not decorated-bare, not minimalist-bare, but bare the way a cell is bare — the bareness of a person who owns nothing because ownership requires memory, and memory is the thing she traded for her freedom.


A low table, scavenged wood, the surface scarred with knife marks and tea stains. Two cushions. A shelf cut into the wall holding three clay jars — tea, the medicinal mushrooms, and something else he couldn’t identify. And the lamp.


The lamp was the only beautiful thing in the room. Blue-white, steady, smokeless — one of the copies of Enki’s design that the Unwritten’s engineer built from scraps. But this one burned with a particular clarity, a blue-white light that was almost cold, almost clinical, the light of a place where illusions were not welcome. It cast no shadows. Or rather, it cast shadows so faint they were barely there — ghost-shadows, margin-shadows, the shadows of things that existed but chose not to be seen.


The room felt like a held breath. The stillness of a space that existed in the pause between the Archive’s inhalations — a pocket of silence inside the vast respiratory rhythm, a place where the air didn’t move because the Archive didn’t know the air was there. Sarai lived in a held breath. She had made her home in the space between one heartbeat and the next, and it was, Naram thought, the loneliest room he had ever entered.


Sarai was sitting at the table. The tea was already poured — two cups, steaming, as if she’d known exactly when he would arrive. She probably had. The Unwritten couldn’t be seen by the Archive, but they could feel it, the way a fish feels the current — not through sight but through the pressure of the water against its skin. She knew where the Archive was looking. She knew when it blinked. And she knew, therefore, when someone was moving through its blind spots toward her door.


“You’re late,” she said.


“I got lost. The corridor with the low ceiling — it wasn’t low yesterday.”


“The margins shift. The Archive grows in its sleep.” She gestured to the cushion across from her. “Sit. Drink. We have work to do and not much time to do it in.”


He sat. He drank. The tea was the same — bitter, warm, grounding. The taste of the margins.


“Less than two days,” she said, before he could ask. “Thirty-some hours, if you want to be precise. And you should want to be precise, because precision is the only advantage you have.” She set down her cup. Her clear eyes — the empty eyes of the Unwritten, the eyes that had looked inward and found the shelves bare — met his. “Are you ready to learn?”


“I’m ready to try.”


“Good enough. Trying is the first step. Failing is the second. Succeeding is — optional, but preferred.” The ghost of a smile. The crooked one, assembled from whatever materials the erasure had left behind. “The lesson begins with a cup of tea and a lie.”




“Tell me something true,” Sarai said. “Something you know absolutely. Something the Archive knows you know.”


“My name is Naram.”


“Good. Now write it.”


She handed him a stylus — not an Archive stylus, not adamant, but a simple reed stylus, the kind apprentices used for practice. And a small clay tablet, fresh, still damp. The clay was cool under his fingers, yielding, alive in the way that all Archive clay was alive — responsive to the touch of a scribe, waiting to receive words the way soil waits to receive seeds.


He wrote: 
My name is Naram.


The clay held. The words sat on the surface, clean and clear. No ash. No rejection. Truth, accepted. He could feel it — the faint hum of the Archive’s approval, distant but present even here in the margins, the vibration of a system recognizing a statement that matched its records. The hum was almost pleasant. The hum was the feeling of being known.


“Now,” Sarai said. “Write a lie.”


“What lie?”


“Any lie. Something small. Something the Archive can check.”


He thought for a moment. Then he wrote: 
My name is Khalid.


The clay turned to ash. Instantly. The words dissolved, the tablet crumbled, and the fine white powder settled on the table like snow. The hum vanished, replaced by something else — not a sound but a sensation, a brief, sharp wrongness, the feeling of a door slamming in a room you didn’t know had doors. The Archive’s rejection. He’d felt it before, in the scriptorium, when the Khalid al-Rashid biography had crumbled. But here, in the margins, in the silence of Sarai’s held-breath room, the rejection felt more intimate. More personal. As if the Archive had leaned close and whispered: 
I know who you are. Don’t test me.


“The Archive knows your name,” Sarai said. She brushed the ash from the table with the back of her hand — a practiced gesture, casual, the gesture of someone who had watched a lot of clay turn to powder. “It has your biography. It has your birth record. It knows you are Naram, not Khalid. The lie is obvious, and the rejection is immediate.”


“So how do I forge?”


Sarai leaned back on her cushion. The blue-white lamp cast its shadowless light across her face, and Naram noticed, not for the first time, how the light made her look less like a person and more like a drawing of a person — all clean lines and flat planes, the dimensionality stripped away, the depth removed. A woman without shadows. A woman who had erased her own depth and was left with only surface.


“You don’t lie to the Archive,” she said. “You lie to yourself. And then you write the truth — your truth, the truth you’ve made yourself believe. The Archive reads the writer, not just the writing. If the writer believes what they’re writing, the Archive accepts it. Because the Archive’s definition of truth is not objective. It’s subjective. It’s 
your
 truth. The truth as you understand it.”


“That’s — that’s insane.”


“It’s Onomancy. The power of the written word depends on the writer’s conviction. A biography written by a scribe who believes every word is more powerful than a biography written by a scribe who’s just going through the motions. Gilgamesh understood this. It’s why he built the Archive around human scribes instead of — I don’t know, some kind of automatic system. Because human belief gives the writing power. And human belief can be manipulated.”


She poured more tea. Her hands were steady — steadier than Naram’s, which were trembling slightly, though whether from fear or exhaustion or the residual cold of the deep Archive, he couldn’t tell. The trembling annoyed him. He was a scribe. His hands were his instruments. A scribe with trembling hands was like a singer with a sore throat — technically functional, practically useless.


“Close your eyes,” she said. “Think of your name. Naram. Feel it. The sound of it. The weight of it. The history of it — your mother choosing it, your teachers using it, your friends calling it across the refectory. Feel how true it is. How certain.”


He closed his eyes. He felt it. Naram. His name. The first word anyone had ever written about him. The word that meant 
him,
 in every language, in every context, in every moment of his nineteen years. The word Utu used when he wanted Naram’s attention across the refectory, always too loud, always with that particular rising inflection that turned the name into a question and a greeting and a mild accusation all at once.


Naram. The name was a room he’d lived in his whole life. The walls were familiar. The furniture was arranged just so. He knew where everything was. He could navigate it in the dark.


“Now let it go.”


“What?”


“Let it go. Not the name — the certainty. The feeling that the name is 
yours.
 The feeling that it defines you. Let that go. Hold the name at arm’s length. Look at it like a word on a page — just a word, just letters, just ink on clay. Not you. Just a label. A convenience. Something someone chose for you before you could choose for yourself.”


He tried. It was like trying to let go of his own hand — the name was so deeply part of him that separating from it felt like separating from his body. The room he’d lived in his whole life, and she was asking him to step outside it, to look at it from the corridor, to see it as just a room. Four walls. A ceiling. A door. Not home. Just architecture.


He tried. He held the name at a distance. He looked at it. 
Naram.
 Just a word. Just five letters. Just a sound. A sound his mother had made. A sound that could have been any other sound — she could have called him Khalid, or Dagan, or Shamash, and the sound would have stuck just the same, and he would have felt just as certain that 
that
 name was his, because certainty was not about the name. Certainty was about the habit of wearing it.


The room wavered. The walls thinned. For a moment — half a moment, a fraction — he saw the name from outside. Just a word. Just a label. Just a sound that someone had attached to him before he had any say in the matter.


“Now,” Sarai said. “While you’re holding it at a distance — while the certainty is loose — pick up the stylus. And write: 
My name is Khalid.
”


He opened his eyes. He picked up the stylus. He looked at the fresh tablet Sarai had placed before him — she’d prepared it while his eyes were closed, he realized, the way a surgeon prepares instruments while the patient is counting backward from ten. And he wrote, with the name 
Naram
 held at arm’s length in his mind, with the certainty loosened like a knot half-untied:


My name is Khalid.


The clay held.


For one second. Two. Three. The words sat on the surface, clear and clean, accepted. The Archive tasted the lie and found — not truth, exactly, but something close enough. A writer who was not certain of his own name. A writer whose conviction was loose enough that the lie slipped through the gap between belief and doubt. The hum returned — fainter than before, uncertain, the hum of a system that was accepting the statement provisionally, the way a customs official accepts a passport that looks almost right but not quite.


Then the ash came. Slower this time — not the instant rejection of before but a gradual dissolution, the words fading like ink in rain, the clay softening, the ash forming in patches rather than all at once. The Archive had accepted the lie for three seconds before its deeper systems caught up, checked the records, found the discrepancy, and rejected it.


Three seconds. Not long. But longer than zero. And the difference between zero and three was the difference between impossible and difficult, and difficult was something Naram could work with.


“That’s the beginning,” Sarai said. “Three seconds of accepted forgery. With practice, you can extend it. Minutes. Hours. Days. The better you are at loosening your own certainty — at holding your beliefs at a distance, at making yourself genuinely unsure of what’s true — the longer the forgery holds.”


“And the cost?”


“Check.”


Naram checked. He thought of his name. 
Naram.
 It was there. Clear. Certain. He thought of his mother. There. His childhood. There. The refectory. The scriptorium. Utu. Sarai. All there.


But something was missing. Something small. A — a color. The color of the robe he’d worn on his first day as an apprentice. It had been — blue? Green? He couldn’t remember. He knew he’d worn a robe. He knew it was his first day. But the color was gone. A tiny detail. A speck of memory. Like a single tile missing from a mosaic — the picture was still recognizable, still complete in all the ways that mattered, but there was a gap. A small, square absence where a color used to be.


“The color of my apprentice robe,” he said.


“Small cost for a small lie. The costs scale. A bigger lie — ‘Naram is ill,’ ‘Naram is in his chambers,’ ‘Naram did not visit the Catalog’ — would cost more. A childhood memory. A face. A name.” Sarai’s voice was gentle. Not soft — the Unwritten didn’t do soft — but gentle, the way a surgeon is gentle when they tell you the operation will hurt. “This is the tool, Naram. This is what I can teach you. The ability to fool the Archive, at the cost of yourself. The question is how much of yourself you’re willing to spend.”


Naram looked at the ash on the table. The remains of his three-second lie. The white powder that had been clay, that had been words, that had been a small act of rebellion against a system that demanded truth and punished deviation. The cost of a color he would never see again — not in memory, not in dreams, not in the idle moments when the mind wanders backward and finds, in the past, the small bright details that make a life feel lived.


Gone. Spent. Traded for three seconds of accepted forgery.


He thought about what else he might trade. The taste of his mother’s bread. Utu’s laugh — the real one, the one that shook his whole body. The feeling of holding a stylus for the first time, the understanding arriving all at once that this small tool could change the world. Would he trade those? For minutes of forgery? For hours?


“Enough,” he said. “I’m willing to spend enough.”


Sarai watched him. The clear eyes, the shadowless face. She was assessing him — not judging, but assessing, the way a carpenter assesses a piece of wood. Looking for the grain. Looking for the strength. Looking for the places where the wood would bend and the places where it would break.


“Good,” she said. “Then we practice.”




He practiced for the rest of the afternoon.


Small lies first. 
The tea is cold
 when it was hot. 
The lamp is dim
 when it was bright. 
Sarai is standing
 when she was sitting. Each lie required the same process — the loosening, the distancing, the deliberate unfastening of certainty — and each time the process got a little easier and a little harder simultaneously. Easier because the technique was becoming familiar, the mental gesture of stepping outside his own convictions growing smoother with repetition, like a dancer learning a step. Harder because each loosening left him a little less anchored, a little less sure of the ground beneath his beliefs, and the cumulative effect was a kind of vertigo — the dizziness of a person who has looked down from a great height and realized that the height is inside them.


Each lie held for a few seconds longer than the last — four seconds, six, ten — and each cost him something. The taste of a specific meal — lamb and rosemary, eaten on a feast day he could no longer place. The sound of rain on the Archive’s ventilation shafts — a sound he hadn’t known he treasured until it was gone, until he reached for the memory and found only the knowledge that rain made a sound and the sound had been beautiful and the beauty was now a blank space in his mind, a silence where music used to be. The feeling of his mother’s hand on his forehead when he was sick — the specific temperature of her palm, the specific pressure of her fingers, the specific combination of touch and warmth and presence that meant 
you are not alone, you are cared for, you will be well.


Small losses. Bearable losses. The kind of losses you don’t notice until you reach for the memory and find it gone, like reaching for a cup that someone has moved.


But the losses accumulated. By the time the afternoon light faded in the crystal shafts far above — by the time the second day was half gone — Naram had lost a constellation of small memories. Dozens of them. The specific shade of blue in Enki’s painted sky. The name of the apprentice who had sat next to him during his first year. The smell of the ink he’d used for his first biography. Tiny, specific, irreplaceable pieces of his past, traded for seconds of accepted forgery. His personal history was becoming a night sky with stars going out — still recognizable, still navigable, but darker. Emptier.


He wondered if this was how the Unwritten felt. Not the full erasure — the catastrophic loss that Sarai and Adapa had undergone — but the early stages. The first thinning. He looked at Sarai, sitting across the table, watching him with her clear, empty eyes, and he thought: 
This is what you looked like, once. Before the erasure.


And he was getting better. His last forgery — 
Naram is sleeping in his quarters
 — had held for six hours before the Archive’s deeper systems caught up and rejected it. Six hours of the Archive believing he was asleep while he sat in Sarai’s chamber, practicing lies. Six hours of invisibility, purchased with the memory of — what? He checked. He couldn’t find what he’d lost. That was the cruelest part of the cost: the big losses were identifiable, nameable, grievable. But the small losses — the losses that accumulated during sustained forgery — were anonymous. You couldn’t mourn what you couldn’t remember having.


“That’s enough for today,” Sarai said. She was watching him with an expression he was learning to read — concern, filtered through the Unwritten’s emotional flatness, coming out as a kind of careful attention. “You’re losing too much too fast. The memories need time to — settle. To find their new shape. If you keep going, you’ll start losing structural memories. Not details. Foundations.”


“I need to be ready by tomorrow.”


“You need to be 
you
 by tomorrow. A forger who’s lost too much is useless — they can’t remember what they’re forging or why. The skill requires a balance. Enough loss to loosen your certainty. Not so much that you lose your purpose.” She paused. “I’ve seen it happen. People who practiced too hard, too fast. They got very good at forgery. They could fool the Archive for days. But they couldn’t remember why they wanted to fool it. They’d lost the motivation along with the memories. They’d become — tools. Instruments. Capable of the technique but empty of the reason.”


She was right. He knew she was right. But the clock was running, and every hour that passed was an hour closer to sunset on the third day, and the seven words in his biography were not getting any less true.


“One more,” he said. “One more, and then I’ll stop.”


Sarai looked at him. The clear eyes. The assessment. The carpenter examining the wood.


“One more,” she agreed. “But not a lie about yourself. A lie about the world. Something external. The identity-lies are the expensive ones. The world-lies are cheaper — they cost peripheral memories, not the structural ones. Not the ones that hold you together.”


He thought. He picked up the stylus. He held the world at arm’s length — not himself, not his name, but the world around him. The temperature of the room. The color of the lamp. The sound of the Archive’s breathing, faint and distant. He loosened his certainty about these things — not much, just enough, just the slight unfastening of conviction that created the gap between belief and doubt.


He wrote: 
The Archive is silent.


The clay held. Five seconds. Ten. Fifteen. The lie sat on the surface, accepted, because Naram had made himself genuinely uncertain about whether the Archive was breathing or not — and in the margins, where the breathing was faint, where the sound was more felt than heard, the uncertainty was almost honest. Almost true. And the almost was enough.


Thirty seconds. Forty. The ash began to form at the edges, the letters softening. Fifty seconds. The tablet crumbled.


Fifty seconds of accepted forgery, and the cost was the taste of pomegranate. A specific pomegranate, eaten on a specific day, in a specific place he could no longer recall. The fruit was gone. The sweetness was gone. But the structural memories held. His name. His purpose. His rage. His plan.


“Fifty seconds,” Sarai said. “Not bad. Not enough, but not bad.” She collected the ash into a small clay jar she kept on the shelf. “Tomorrow. Early. We’ll work on sustained forgery — lies that hold not for seconds but for hours. You’ll need that for what comes next.”


“What comes next?”


“The plan. The one you haven’t figured out yet but that I can see forming behind your eyes like a storm behind a mountain. You’re going to do something reckless and brilliant and probably fatal, and you’re going to need the Archive to believe a very large lie for a very long time while you do it.”


“You can see all that?”


“I can see that you’re nineteen and angry and running out of time. The reckless and brilliant part is an educated guess. The fatal part is statistics.” The crooked smile. “Get some rest, Naram. Tomorrow we make you into a liar. A proper one. The kind the Archive can’t catch.”




He went back to the inhabited levels. The transition was always jarring — the shift from the margins’ held-breath silence to the Archive’s full respiration, the sound swelling around him as he climbed from the forgotten corridors into the known ones, the breathing growing louder with each step until it was not just audible but physical, a pressure against his skin, the weight of being observed by something that knew he was there and was, in its vast and impersonal way, paying attention.


He ate dinner — lentils, cumin, the cook had found her supply — and sat with Utu and said nothing about the biography he’d found in the Catalog, the one with Utu’s name on the spine, the one that said 
fever in seven days.
 He said nothing because saying it would not help. Because the only way to save Utu was to save everyone, and the only way to save everyone was to stop Gilgamesh, and the only way to stop Gilgamesh was to do something that Naram hadn’t figured out yet.


Utu talked about his day — a biography he was working on, a potter in Mosul whose hands knew things her mind didn’t. Muscle memory. The body’s knowledge. Naram listened and thought about Sarai’s hands, steady on the teapot. About Adapa’s hands, trembling with the ghost of a stylus. About his own hands, which had spent the afternoon writing lies and were now holding a spoon and pretending that the world was normal.


“You’re quiet,” Utu said.


“Thinking.”


“You’re always thinking. It’s your worst quality. Well — second worst. Your worst quality is that you never finish your lentils.”


“Nobody finishes their lentils. The lentils are a test of endurance, not a meal.”


Utu laughed. The real laugh — the one from somewhere deep, the one that shook his whole body. Naram listened to it and held it and memorized it, because he had learned today that memories could be taken, and this was a memory he was not willing to lose.


But he was getting closer. He could feel it — the shape of a plan, forming in the spaces between what he knew and what he didn’t know, the way a sculpture forms in the spaces between chisel strikes. Not complete. Not clear. But present. Taking shape. The plan was made of forgery and rage and the specific, desperate creativity of a nineteen-year-old who had less than two days to live and was not, despite the statistics, planning to die.


He went to bed. He did not sleep. He lay in the dark and listened to the Archive breathe and thought about forgery and truth and the cost of lies and the price of freedom and the specific, irreplaceable color of a robe he would never remember wearing. He thought about the taste of pomegranate, gone now, a sweetness he could name but not recall, a ghost-flavor haunting the place where a memory used to live.


And somewhere in the deep Archive, in an empty tomb full of open books, Gilgamesh wrote. And the pages turned. And the biographies grew longer. And the deaths grew closer. And the Archive breathed, and the breathing was patient, and the patience was the patience of something that had been alive for three thousand years and intended to be alive for three thousand more.


One day left.




End of Chapter Thirteen
