

Chapter Fourteen: The Masters


Enheduanna found him.


Not in the corridors. Not in the stacks. In the refectory, at lunch, sitting at his usual table with a bowl of lentils he wasn’t eating and a mind that was racing so fast it had lapped itself twice and was now running on fumes and desperation.


She sat down across from him. She did not have a bowl. She did not have tea. She had the expression of a woman who had been carrying a secret for two hundred years and was about to set it down.


“You went to the tomb,” she said.


It was not a question. Naram didn’t bother denying it. Enheduanna knew everything. She was two hundred years old and had not survived that long by being uninformed.


“I went to the tomb,” he said.


“And you found it empty.”


“I found it full. Full of books. Full of writing. Full of Gilgamesh.”


Enheduanna nodded. The nod of a physician confirming a diagnosis they already knew. “Yes. I know.”


“You know.”


“I have known for — a long time. Not always. Not from the beginning. But for long enough that the knowing has become a weight I carry the way you carry your ink stains. Permanently. Invisibly. Part of me.”


The refectory hummed around them. Scribes eating. Apprentices running. Old Shamhat banging pots. The ordinary sounds of an ordinary institution, and beneath the sounds, the slow tidal rhythm of the ventilation, and beneath that, the writing of Gilgamesh, and beneath the writing, the slow consumption of twenty-three lives.


“How long?” Naram asked.


“Eighty years. Give or take. I discovered it the way you discovered it — through an anomaly. Not a rejected biography. A sealed one. A biography that completed itself without a scribe’s involvement. A merchant in Aleppo — a man I had been documenting for years, a man whose life I knew intimately, whose virtues and flaws I had cataloged with the care of a portraitist. He died. Suddenly. Of a fever that came from nowhere and burned for three days and left nothing behind. And when I checked his biography — the official biography, the one I had been writing — I found that the final pages had been written in a hand that was not mine.”


“Gilgamesh’s hand.”


“Gilgamesh’s hand. The same hand you found in the falsified files. The same hand that wrote your death sentence. Beautiful. Precise. Unmistakable. The hand of the founder. The hand of a man who had been dead for two hundred and twenty years.”


She paused. A scribe walked past their table — a senior, carrying a stack of tablets, moving with the distracted urgency of someone who had too much work and not enough time. The scribe didn’t look at them. In the refectory, a Master sitting with a junior was not unusual. Masters mentored juniors. It was part of the system. Part of the hierarchy. Part of the machine.


“I investigated,” Enheduanna continued. “Quietly. Carefully. Over years. I traced the completions — the biographies that finished themselves, the deaths that came from nowhere. I found the pattern. I found the tomb. I found the books. I found — him.”


“And you did nothing.”


The words came out harder than Naram intended. Harder and sharper and carrying a weight of accusation that he hadn’t planned but couldn’t retract. Enheduanna looked at him. The dark eyes. The reading-eyes. The eyes that had been reading people for two hundred years and had seen, in that time, every variety of human anger and human disappointment and human betrayal.


“I did nothing,” she said. “That is accurate. And the reason I did nothing is the reason I am sitting here now, telling you this, instead of sitting in my chambers pretending that the world is not on fire.”


“What reason?”


“The Archive.”


She said the word the way a priest says the name of God — not with reverence but with the particular weight of someone who has spent their life in service to something larger than themselves and has learned that the something is both worthy of service and capable of terrible things.


“The Archive is not Gilgamesh,” she said. “I need you to understand that. The Archive existed before Gilgamesh merged with it. The Archive will exist after Gilgamesh is stopped — if Gilgamesh can be stopped. The Archive is — it is the most important thing in the world, Naram. Not because it is powerful. Not because it is alive. Because it is the repository of human knowledge. Three thousand years of truth. Three thousand years of history, science, literature, law, medicine, philosophy. Every text that Ashurbanipal’s scribes gathered. Every text that the Archive has produced since. The sum total of human civilization, preserved in stone and clay and papyrus, protected from fire and flood and war and the slow erosion of time.”


“And from Gilgamesh?”


“And from Gilgamesh. That is the paradox. Gilgamesh is destroying the Archive from within — consuming lives, corrupting biographies, using the system for his own purposes. But Gilgamesh is also sustaining the Archive. His continuous writing feeds the Archive’s awareness. His consumed biographies provide the energy that keeps the Archive alive. Without Gilgamesh, the Archive might weaken. Might sleep. Might die. And if the Archive dies, three thousand years of human knowledge dies with it.”


“So you chose the knowledge over the people.”


“I chose the knowledge over the people. Yes. I made that choice eighty years ago, and I have made it every day since, and every day the choice has cost me something, and the cost has accumulated, and the weight of it is — considerable.” She folded her hands on the table. The hands of a scribe. Ink-stained. Strong. Precise. The hands of a woman who had written more biographies than anyone alive and who knew, better than anyone, what the writing cost. “I am not asking for forgiveness. I am not asking for understanding. I am telling you the truth, because you deserve the truth, and because the truth is the only currency that holds its value in this place.”


Naram looked at her. The ageless face. The dark eyes. The grey at the temples that seemed deliberate, chosen, a display of age that was also a display of control. She was two hundred years old. She had watched Gilgamesh consume lives for eighty of those years. She had known and done nothing. She had weighed the lives of individuals against the preservation of civilization and had chosen civilization, and the choice had been rational and defensible and monstrous.


“What changed?” he asked. “Why are you telling me now?”


“Because you changed the equation. You proved that the Archive can reject a lie. You proved that the system is not perfect. You proved that Gilgamesh’s control is not absolute. And that proof — that small, accidental, beautiful proof — means that there might be a way to stop Gilgamesh without destroying the Archive. A way to separate the parasite from the host. A way to save the knowledge and the people.”


“Might.”


“Might. I deal in probabilities, Naram. I have lived too long for certainties. But the probability has shifted. Before you, the probability of stopping Gilgamesh without destroying the Archive was zero. Now it is — not zero. Small. But not zero. And not-zero is enough. Not-zero is the difference between despair and action.”


She stood. She was tall — taller than Naram, taller than most people in the Archive, a woman whose physical presence matched her institutional presence, a woman who filled a room the way a pillar fills a room, by being the thing that holds the ceiling up.


“I will help you,” she said. “Not openly. Not yet. The other masters — Tabni, the others — they know about Gilgamesh. They have always known. They accept it. They believe, as I believed, that the cost is justified. That the consumption is the price of preservation. If they learn that I am working against Gilgamesh, they will act. They will accelerate the executions. They will seal the corridors. They will do whatever Gilgamesh tells them to do, because Gilgamesh is the system and they are the system’s servants and servants do not question the system.”


“Tabni.”


“Tabni is the most dangerous. Not because he is cruel — Tabni is not cruel. Tabni is faithful. Tabni believes in the Archive the way the devout believe in God — absolutely, unquestioningly, with a certainty that no evidence can shake. He has spent his life enforcing the Archive’s rules because he believes the rules are good. He has spent his life guarding the Restricted Section because he believes the secrets are sacred. He has spent his life conducting disciplinary reviews because he believes that discipline is love. And he will spend his life defending Gilgamesh because he believes that Gilgamesh is the Archive and the Archive is Gilgamesh and the two cannot be separated.”


She paused. Something moved across her face — not pity, exactly. Recognition. The recognition of one true believer looking at another, even if the beliefs had diverged.


“Tabni had a daughter,” she said. “Did you know that?”


Naram shook his head.


“Thirty years ago. A girl named Nisaba — named after the goddess of writing, because of course Tabni would name his child after the goddess of writing. She was bright. Quick. She had his precision and her mother’s warmth, and she was going to be a scribe. Tabni was — I had never seen him like that. The man you know, the man with the tax-audit conversations and the eleven-hour interviews — that man did not exist when Nisaba was in the room. He was gentle. He was patient. He told her stories about the Archive’s history, about the texts they preserved, about the knowledge they were protecting for the future. He believed every word. He believed it the way you believe the sun will rise — not as an opinion but as a fact of the universe.”


“What happened to her?”


“She got sick. A fever. The kind that comes from nowhere and burns for three days and—” Enheduanna stopped. The sentence hung in the air between them, unfinished, and Naram finished it in his mind. The kind of fever that comes from nowhere. The kind that burns for three days. The kind that is not a fever at all but a biography completing itself, a life being consumed, a death being written in beautiful, precise handwriting by a consciousness that had been dead for three hundred years.


“Gilgamesh,” Naram said.


“Gilgamesh. Nisaba’s biography completed itself six months before her death. I found the entry later — the final pages, written in that hand. The hand you’ve seen. The hand I’ve seen. Tabni’s daughter, consumed to fuel the Archive’s awareness. One of the twenty-three. One of the names on the list that I keep in my mind and that I will carry until I die.”


“And Tabni knows?”


“Tabni knows his daughter died of a fever. Tabni does not know — or does not allow himself to know — that the fever was written. That the death was authored. That the institution he serves killed the person he loved most.” Enheduanna’s voice was very quiet. “I have watched him, Naram. For thirty years. I have watched him tend her grave — a small marker in the Archive’s memorial garden, with her name and the dates and a line from a hymn to Nisaba the goddess. He visits it every seventh day. He brings flowers — desert flowers, the small, stubborn kind that grow in the cracks of the surface rock. He arranges them with the same precision he applies to everything. And then he goes back to his office and he enforces the rules and he guards the Restricted Section and he conducts his interviews and he serves the system that killed his daughter, and he does it with absolute faith, because the alternative — the alternative is a world where his daughter’s death was not a tragedy but a murder, and the murderer is the thing he has given his life to, and his life’s work is complicity, and the flowers on the grave are an apology he doesn’t know he’s making.”


The refectory noise seemed very far away. The lentils. The scraping spoons. The ordinary sounds of an ordinary institution.


“That’s why he’s dangerous,” Enheduanna said. “Not because he’s cruel. Because he’s broken. Because the faith is the only thing holding the broken pieces together. If the faith cracks — if Tabni is forced to see what Gilgamesh did to Nisaba — the pieces will fall, and what’s underneath the faith is not reason or doubt or the capacity for change. What’s underneath is grief. Thirty years of unfelt grief. And grief that large, released that suddenly, is not a force you can predict or control.”


“Can they be separated?”


Enheduanna looked at him. The dark eyes held something that Naram had not seen in them before — not the reading-look, not the assessing-look, but something softer. Something that might have been hope, if hope were a thing that a two-hundred-year-old woman who had watched eighty years of consumption could still feel.


“I don’t know,” she said. “But I think you might find out.”


She left. She walked through the refectory with the measured stride of a Master — unhurried, authoritative, the walk of a woman who owned the room and everyone in it. Scribes moved aside. Apprentices straightened. Old Shamhat, in the kitchen, paused her pot-banging long enough to nod — a nod of respect, or recognition, or the particular acknowledgment that passes between two old women who have seen too much and said too little.


Naram sat at his table. His lentils were cold. The refectory hummed. The ventilation sighed through the crystal shafts above.


And somewhere in the deep stacks, in the sealed chambers where the masters met to discuss the business of running a three-thousand-year-old institution, Tabni was reading a report. A report about unauthorized access to the Restricted Section. A report about ash in the First Blood Registry. A report about a junior scribe named Naram who had been asking questions and visiting places and doing things that junior scribes were not supposed to do.


Tabni read the report. Tabni made a note. Tabni filed the note in the appropriate folder, in the appropriate cabinet, in the appropriate section of his meticulously organized office.


And then Tabni went to the tomb.


Not to investigate. Not to question. To report. To tell Gilgamesh — the system, the founder, the god-in-progress — that the junior scribe was becoming a problem. That the problem needed to be addressed. That the execution, scheduled for sunset on the third day, might need to be — supplemented. Preceded by measures that would ensure the scribe could not cause further disruption in the time remaining.


Tabni did not think of this as cruelty. Tabni thought of this as maintenance. The Archive was a machine. Machines required maintenance. And maintenance, sometimes, meant removing the parts that didn’t work.


He descended the staircase to the deep Archive. He passed through the corridor of living text — the text parting for him, recognizing him, accepting him as authorized.


But he did not go directly to the tomb.


He stopped, first, at the memorial garden. A small chamber off the main corridor — not a garden in any real sense, because nothing grew sixty feet underground, but a room with stone markers and the smell of dust and the particular silence of a place where the living came to speak to the dead. Tabni had petitioned for this room forty years ago, when the Archive’s first generation of native-born scribes began to die and there was nowhere to mark their passing. The masters had approved it. Gilgamesh — through the masters, through the system — had approved it. A small concession. A room for grief. The kind of thing an institution provides when it wants to appear humane without actually being humane.


Tabni knelt before the third marker on the left wall. A small stone, carved with precise letters — his own carving, his own hand, the letters as clean and measured as everything Tabni did. 
Nisaba, daughter of Tabni. Born in the year of the Archive’s 2,970th cycle. Died in the year of the Archive’s 2,977th cycle. She was seven. She loved the stories.


He had brought flowers. Desert flowers — small, yellow, the kind that grew in the cracks of the surface rock where the ventilation shafts opened to the sky. Enki brought them down for him, once a week, without being asked. Enki, who expressed care through engineering and small acts of procurement and who had never once mentioned the flowers to anyone, because Enki understood that some kindnesses are diminished by acknowledgment.


Tabni arranged the flowers with care. He removed the old ones — dried now, brittle, the color faded to the pale brown of things that have been underground too long. He placed the new ones in the small clay vase he had made for this purpose. He adjusted them. He adjusted them again. The arrangement was never quite right. It was the one thing in Tabni’s life that was never quite right — the one task he performed with something other than his usual mechanical precision. The flowers were always slightly crooked, slightly uneven, slightly imperfect. As if the hands that could organize an archive and conduct an eleven-hour interview and file a report with flawless accuracy could not, when it came to this, when it came to flowers for a dead child, achieve the control they applied to everything else.


He spoke to her. He always spoke to her. Not aloud — Tabni was not a man who spoke aloud to empty rooms. But in his mind, in the private, unwritten space where even the Archive’s awareness could not reach, he told his daughter about his day. About the junior scribe who had been asking questions. About the report on his desk. About the lentils, which were garlic tonight, and which Nisaba had hated because she said garlic made her breath smell like a merchant’s donkey, and the memory of her saying that — the specific words, the specific face she made, the specific giggle that followed — was so vivid and so present that for a moment, just a moment, the thirty years between then and now collapsed, and she was here, and she was seven, and she was alive, and the world was a place where children did not die of fevers that came from nowhere.


The moment passed. The thirty years returned. The stone marker was just a stone marker. The flowers were just flowers. And Tabni was just a man kneeling in a room underground, speaking to someone who could not hear him, arranging flowers that would die in a week, performing a ritual that changed nothing and meant everything.


He stood. He straightened his robe. He composed his face — the institutional face, the enforcement face, the face of a man who believed in the system and served the system and did not, could not, would not question the system, because the system was the only thing that gave his daughter’s death meaning. She had died in the Archive. She had died in service to the Archive. She had died because the world was imperfect and fevers were cruel and children were fragile. That was the story. That was the only story Tabni could afford to believe. The alternative — the alternative was a room with no floor, a fall with no bottom, a grief with no container.


He entered the tomb. He stood before the empty coffin and the open books and the ceaseless, tireless writing of a consciousness that had been dead for three hundred years.


“Master Gilgamesh,” Tabni said. His voice was formal. Respectful. The voice of a servant addressing a master. “There is a matter that requires your attention.”


The books rustled. The pages turned. And from the writing — from the text itself, from the words on the pages and the ink on the paper and the three hundred years of accumulated narrative — a voice emerged. Not a human voice. A text-voice. The sound of language being spoken by something that had no mouth.


“Tell me,” Gilgamesh said.


Tabni told him. He told him about the junior scribe, about the questions, about the unauthorized access. He told him with the precision and completeness of a man delivering a report, because that is what this was — a report, a piece of maintenance, a necessary communication between a servant and the system he served.


He did not mention the flowers. He did not mention the memorial garden. He did not mention the small stone marker with the precise letters and the name of a goddess of writing and the dates that spanned seven years — seven years that were, in the vast timeline of the Archive, less than a breath, less than a heartbeat, less than the time it took for a page to turn.


He did not mention Nisaba. He never mentioned Nisaba. Not here. Not to Gilgamesh. Not to the consciousness that wrote the biographies and completed the lives and consumed the futures of people who were, to it, not people but fuel.


Because if he mentioned Nisaba here, in this room, in the presence of the books that wrote and the consciousness that consumed — if he said her name in this place — he might hear something in the text-voice’s response. A recognition. A confirmation. A small, terrible detail that would crack the faith and release the grief and bring the ceiling down on everything he had built his life upon.


Tabni did not mention Nisaba. Tabni delivered his report. Tabni received his instructions. Tabni left the tomb and climbed the staircase and returned to his office and filed his notes and did not think about the flowers, which were already beginning to wilt in the underground air, and did not think about the stone marker, which would outlast him, and did not think about the seven years, which were gone, and did not think about the fever, which had come from nowhere.


He did not think about these things. He was very good at not thinking about these things. He had been practicing for thirty years.


And in the refectory, sixty feet above, Naram ate his cold lentils and did not know that the clock had just gotten faster.




End of Chapter Fourteen
