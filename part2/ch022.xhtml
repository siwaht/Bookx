Chapter 19: Blood and Sisters

The truth broke Daliya in the morning.

She had been feeding the children — the seventeen orphans in the cave system, the ones she had cared for, the ones she had told stories to, the ones whose hair she had braided and whose games she had taught and whose names she had learned because learning names was what you did when you cared for people, even if the people were small and the caring was temporary and the reason they were there was something that nobody would explain.

Samira had explained.

Not willingly. Not gently. The explanation had been forced — extracted by the convergence of events that neither sister had anticipated. The alchemists had arrived at Iram. The formula had been completed. The proof had been read. And the proof said: the Stone does not require the blood of children.

The proof said that everything Samira had done — the gathering, the preparation, the five years of methodical, clinical, emotionless collection of orphans and street children — had been unnecessary. The proof said that the children in the cave were not materials. They were not reagents. They were not the raw substance that the formula required. They were children. Just children. Gathered for nothing. Prepared for nothing. Cared for by Daliya and cataloged by Samira and held in a cave system in the mountains for two years because a three-hundred-year-old woman had been wrong about the cost of the Stone and had passed her wrongness to her apprentice and the apprentice had built her entire identity on the wrongness and the identity was now crumbling.

Samira told Daliya in the cave. In the dark. Among the sleeping children. The words came out flat — the controlled, deliberately emotionless delivery of a woman who was reporting facts and refusing to feel them.

“The formula is wrong. The blood was never necessary. The children were never necessary. Everything I’ve done — everything we’ve done, was based on incomplete knowledge.”

Daliya stared at her sister. The wide eyes, the warm eyes, the eyes that had never learned to be cold — processed the information with the visible, heartbreaking slowness of a mind that was trying to understand something that could not be understood because the understanding would require the destruction of everything the mind believed about the person delivering the information.

“The children,” Daliya said. “You said they were orphans. You said Nasreen rescued them.”

“I lied.”

“You said they were being cared for. You said they would be returned to the cities when Nasreen’s work was done.”

“I lied.”

“You said—”

“I lied about everything, Daliya. The children were gathered for the Stone. Their blood — their unlived potential — was supposed to be the catalyst. The fuel. The price of creating the Philosopher’s Stone. I gathered them. I selected them. I chose the ones nobody would miss — the orphans, the street children, the ones whose absence would not be noticed. I chose them the way I choose reagents for a transmutation — by quality, by availability, by the measurable, quantifiable properties that made them suitable for the purpose.”

The cave was quiet. The children slept. Seventeen small bodies, breathing in the dark, the sound of their breathing the sound of lives that had been gathered for consumption and were now, by the accident of a completed formula, reprieved.

“You were going to kill them,” Daliya said. The words were not a question. The words were the devastating, irreversible moment when a person understood something about someone they loved and the understanding changed everything and the everything could not be unchanged.

“The formula required their blood. The blood had to be harvested. The harvesting would have been—” Samira stopped. The flat delivery faltered. The control cracked — not dramatically, not visibly, but in the subtle way that a wall cracked when the pressure behind it exceeded the structure’s capacity. A hairline fracture. A line running through the composure. “Fatal. The harvesting would have been fatal.”

“You were going to murder seventeen children.”

“I was going to complete the formula. The formula required—”

“You were going to murder seventeen children and you let me care for them. You let me feed them and tell them stories and braid their hair and learn their names. You let me love them, Samira. You let me love them knowing that you were going to kill them.”

The accusation was not loud. The accusation was quiet — the devastating quiet of a person who was too hurt to shout, too broken to rage, too fundamentally altered by the revelation to produce any sound louder than a whisper. The whisper was worse than a shout. The whisper was the sound of a heart breaking in a way that could not be repaired.

Samira said nothing. The sharp face was still. The sharp eyes were fixed on a point in the darkness that was not a point but an absence — the visible, physically present absence of the thing that Samira had transmuted out of herself five years ago. The warmth. The compassion. The capacity to feel what Daliya was feeling and to be destroyed by the feeling.

Samira could not feel it. Samira had transmuted the feeling into purpose. The transmutation had been successful. The transmutation had been necessary. And the transmutation had left Samira standing in a cave in the dark, facing her sister’s devastation, and feeling — nothing. The nothing was the cost. The nothing was the permanent, irreversible consequence of a woman who had converted her humanity into utility and was now, in the moment when humanity was most needed, bankrupt.

“I’m going to take them,” Daliya said. “The children. I’m going to take them out of the cave and I’m going to bring them to the alchemists — to Khalil, to Zahra, to the people who know the truth about the formula. And I’m going to make sure they’re safe.”

“Daliya—”

“Don’t. Don’t say my name like that. Don’t say my name like you’re my sister. My sister wouldn’t have done this. My sister, the girl in the cellar in Aleppo, the girl who drew circles on the walls and dreamed of changing the world, that girl wouldn’t have gathered children for slaughter. That girl is gone. You transmuted her. The way you transmute everything. You turned her into — this.” She gestured at Samira. At the sharp face and the sharp eyes and the scarred hands and the missing fingers. “This thing that looks like my sister and sounds like my sister and has my sister’s face and my sister’s voice and none of my sister’s heart.”

The words hit Samira the way the forge had hit Khalil — not with force but with truth. The truth was worse than force. Force could be resisted. Truth could only be received.

Samira received it. The truth entered her the way light entered glass — passing through, illuminating, revealing the internal, previously hidden architecture of a person who had been living in darkness and had not known it because the darkness was comfortable and the comfort was the darkness.

She saw herself. For the first time in five years, she saw herself — not the alchemist, not the apprentice, not the gatherer, not the purpose-driven, emotion-free, clinically efficient instrument that Nasreen had shaped and that Samira had willingly become. She saw the woman beneath. The woman who had been a girl in a cellar in Aleppo. The woman who had drawn circles on walls and dreamed of changing the world. The woman who had loved her sister with the fierce, protective, all-consuming love of an older sibling who would do anything — anything — to keep the younger one safe.

The woman who had gathered seventeen children for slaughter while her sister braided their hair.

The seeing was the cost. The seeing was the devastating, irreversible consequence of a transmutation that had been performed five years ago and whose price was only now coming due — the price of converting compassion into purpose, the price of transmuting humanity into utility, the price of becoming the thing that the work required and losing the thing that the work was supposed to serve.

“Go,” Samira said. The word was small. The word was the smallest word she had ever spoken — smaller than the equations she recited, smaller than the formulas she drew, smaller than the precise, technically perfect language of alchemy that had been her primary mode of communication for five years. “Take the children. Take them to the alchemists. Keep them safe.”

“And you?”

“I’ll stay. I’ll — I don’t know. I’ll stay.”

Daliya looked at her sister. The warm eyes met the sharp eyes across the dark cave, across the sleeping children, across the five years of separation and the two years of lies and the irreversible, untransmutable distance between a woman who had kept her heart and a woman who had traded hers for purpose.

“I loved you,” Daliya said. “I followed you across the mountains because I loved you. I stayed in this canyon for two years because I loved you. I cared for these children because you asked me to and I loved you enough to do what you asked without questioning why.”

“I know.”

“The love was real, Samira. Even if everything else was a lie. The love was real.”

“I know.”

Daliya turned. Began waking the children. Gently — the practiced, tender efficiency of a woman who had been caring for seventeen children for two years and knew each one’s sleeping habits, each one’s fears, each one’s individual, irreplaceable way of transitioning from sleep to wakefulness.

The children woke. The children trusted Daliya. The children followed her because following Daliya was what they did — the way following a parent was what children did, the instinct deeper than thought, the trust deeper than understanding.

She gathered what food she could find—a sack of dried dates, a jar of honey, flatbread that was stale but edible. She divided it among the children with the practiced efficiency of a woman who had been rationing for seventeen mouths for two years. The smallest boy, Rami, clutched his portion of dates with both hands and looked up at her.

“Where are we going?” he asked.

“Somewhere safe.”

“Will there be more food?”

“Yes, habib. There will be more food.”

The promise was the easiest she had ever made and the most important. Food. Safety. The two things that children needed and that adults were supposed to provide and that the world, in its vast indifference, did not guarantee.

Daliya led them out of the cave. Into the dawn. Into the desert. Toward Iram. Toward the alchemists. Toward safety.

She did not look back.

Samira followed.

Not immediately. Not openly. She followed the way Ghazal followed — at a distance, in the shadows, the practiced, invisible pursuit of a person who did not want to be seen but could not bear to be absent. She followed because the alternative — staying in the empty cave, alone with the empty purpose and the empty heart and the devastating, newly visible emptiness that Daliya’s words had illuminated — was unbearable.

She followed because Daliya was walking toward Hasan.

The jealousy was still there. Beneath the guilt, beneath the shame, beneath the newly awakened horror of what she had done and what she had been prepared to do — the jealousy persisted. The irrational, unkillable jealousy of a woman who had transmuted her capacity for love into purpose and was now watching her sister walk toward a man with the warmth that Samira could no longer produce.

Daliya reached the alchemists’ camp at midday. The children — seventeen small figures, blinking in the sunlight, confused and frightened and trusting Daliya with the absolute, unquestioning trust of children who had no one else to trust — were received by Zahra and Reyhan. The scholar and the commander. The theorist and the soldier. Two women who understood, immediately and completely, what the children represented and what their presence meant.

As the children filed into the camp, Zahra knelt to check them — the scholar’s instinct, cataloging, assessing, her ink-stained hands moving with the careful gentleness of a woman who understood that these were not subjects but survivors. Two of the children — a boy of nine and a girl of seven — bore marks on their inner forearms that stopped Zahra cold. Not bruises. Not burns. Sigils. Small, precise, carved into the skin with something sharper than a blade and healed over with scar tissue that was too smooth, too deliberate to be accidental. The sigils were organic — wound-like, curving, the lines branching and rejoining like veins beneath the surface, nothing like the clean geometry of transmutation circles. Zahra had never seen their like, but something in the pattern made her stomach turn — the way a discordant note made the ear flinch, the wrongness registering before the mind could name it. The marks looked like they had been made by someone who understood the body the way an alchemist understood metal — as raw material, as substrate, as something to be inscribed upon and shaped and used. The boy flinched when she touched his arm, the small body contracting with the practiced reflex of a child who had learned that adult hands meant pain. The girl did not flinch. The girl had learned not to. Zahra looked at Daliya. “Where did these children come from? Before Nasreen’s camp?” Daliya shook her head. “I don’t know. Samira handled the gathering. Some of them were already — marked — when they arrived. I asked Samira about it once. She said they were old injuries.” They were not old injuries. They were Voidcraft sigils — the Bloodsworn’s signature, carved into children’s flesh during experiments that Daliya could not imagine and that Zahra was only beginning to understand. The cult had been here first. Before Nasreen. Before Samira. The Bloodsworn had found these children and used them and discarded them, and Nasreen’s network had picked them up like secondhand goods, not knowing — or not caring — where the damage had come from. The realization settled into Zahra’s chest like a stone dropped into still water, the implications rippling outward: the cult was not a rumor, not a historical footnote, not a dead religion buried with the Old World. The cult was active. The cult was carving sigils into children. And the cult had been operating in the same territory as Nasreen’s gathering, which meant the cult knew about the Stone, which meant the cult was watching, which meant nothing about this quest was as simple or as contained as any of them had believed.

“Nasreen’s gathering,” Zahra said. Her voice was quiet. The voice of a woman who had read the formula and understood the cost and was now looking at the cost in the form of seventeen children standing in the desert sun. “The blood of the young. The catalyst that was never necessary.”

“They’re safe now,” Daliya said. “My sister — Samira — she gathered them. For Nasreen. For the formula. But the formula is wrong. The blood was never needed. The children were never needed.”

“Your sister gathered them,” Reyhan said. The dark eyes were hard. The commander’s assessment — the professional, comprehensive, morally absolute evaluation of a woman who had spent ten years enforcing a law she knew was wrong and was now, for the first time, enforcing a law she knew was right. “Your sister gathered seventeen children for slaughter.”

“My sister was wrong. My sister believed what Nasreen taught her. My sister—”

“Your sister is an accomplice to the planned murder of seventeen children. The law—” Reyhan stopped. The word law caught in her throat — the physical, audible sensation of a woman who had spent ten years hiding behind the law and was now, in the presence of seventeen children who had been gathered for slaughter by the same kind of certainty that had driven the Muhtasib, unable to use the word without choking on it.

“The law,” Reyhan said again. Quieter. “The law is changing. Everything is changing. The children are safe. That’s what matters.”

Hasan was there. The young lieutenant, standing at the edge of the group, his healed wrist flexing unconsciously — the wrist that Reyhan had mended with Farid’s circle, the bone that had been broken and was now whole, the physical evidence that alchemy could heal as well as destroy.

Daliya saw him. Up close, for the first time. The face she had seen from the ridge — the young man on the horse, the figure that had produced the warmth, the stranger who had become, in the two months since that first distant sighting, the architecture of her daydreams and the subject of her stories and the irrational, fundamentally human focus of a heart that needed something to focus on because the alternative was focusing on the horror of what her sister had done.

He was kind. That was the first thing she noticed. Not handsome — though he was handsome, in the unremarkable, pleasant way that young soldiers were handsome. Kind. The kindness was visible in the way he looked at the children — not with the professional assessment of a soldier evaluating a situation but with the unguarded, instinctive compassion of a person who saw children in distress and responded with the desire to help.

He helped. He carried the smallest child — a boy of four, too tired to walk, too frightened to speak. He carried the boy on his hip with the easy, natural competence of a man who had younger siblings and knew how to hold a child and was not embarrassed by the holding.

Daliya watched him carry the child. Daliya felt the warmth — the same warmth she had felt on the ridge, amplified by proximity, intensified by the devastating, irresistible combination of kindness and competence and the way his hand supported the child’s back with a gentleness that was not weakness but strength, the strength of a man who was secure enough in his masculinity to be tender.

She loved him. The realization was not gradual. The realization was instantaneous — the sudden, complete, irreversible recognition of a heart that had found the thing it was looking for and would not be persuaded that the finding was premature or inappropriate or complicated by the fact that seventeen children had just been rescued from her sister’s slaughter and the world was ending and the barrier between worlds was failing and the timing was, by any rational assessment, catastrophic.

The heart did not care about timing. The heart did not care about rationality. The heart cared about the way Hasan held the child and the way Hasan smiled at the child and the way Hasan’s kindness was not performed but inherent, the inalienable, untransmutable quality of a person who was good not because goodness was useful but because goodness was what he was.

Samira watched from the shadows. Samira saw Daliya’s face. Samira saw the warmth — the visible, physically present warmth that Daliya directed at Hasan with the unconscious, uncontrollable, fundamentally honest expressiveness of a woman who could not hide what she felt because hiding was not in her nature.

The jealousy intensified. Not of Hasan. Of the warmth. Of the capacity. Of the human, untransmutable ability to feel love that Samira had traded for purpose and could not buy back because the exchange was permanent and the ledger did not permit refunds.

Samira watched. And the watching was its own kind of torture — the self-inflicted, precisely calibrated suffering of a woman who was seeing, in her sister’s face, the thing she had lost and could not recover and had not known she had lost until the loss was visible in someone else’s possession.

The confrontation came at dusk.

Daliya was with the children. Settling them. The cave system near the alchemists’ camp, a different cave, a safe cave, a cave that was shelter rather than prison. She was telling them a story. The story of the prince on the horse, the same story she had told after seeing Hasan from the ridge, the story that was not about Hasan but was about the feeling, the warmth, the irrational, beautiful hope that the world contained people who were kind and that kindness was worth walking toward.

Samira appeared at the cave entrance. The sharp face was sharper — the angles more pronounced, the eyes more intense, the visible, physically present evidence of a woman who had not slept and had not eaten and had spent the day watching her sister fall in love while the foundation of her own existence crumbled.

“You need to come with me,” Samira said.

“I’m with the children.”

“The children are safe. Reyhan’s soldiers are guarding them. You need to come with me. Now.”

Daliya looked at her sister. The warm eyes assessed the sharp eyes — the practiced, deeply familiar evaluation of a younger sister who had spent her entire life reading her older sister’s moods and knew, with the instinctive certainty of shared blood, that something was wrong. Not the wrongness of the gathering.

“What is it?”

“The formula. The complete formula. I’ve read it — I took Zahra’s notebooks while the camp was distracted by the children. The proof says the Stone requires a choice. But the proof also describes the containment geometry — the circle that prevents a second Iram. The containment requires — materials. Specific materials. Alchemically prepared. And the preparation requires—”

“Samira. What are you asking me to do?”

“The containment geometry requires a blood component. Not the Stone itself, the containment. The safety mechanism. The thing that prevents the transmutation from destroying everything. The blood of the young, a small amount, freely given, not harvested — is part of the containment circle. Not the catalyst. The shield.”

“You want to use the children.”

“I want to protect the children. The containment circle protects everyone — the alchemists, the soldiers, the pilgrims, the children themselves. Without the containment, the transmutation could produce another Iram. Another glass city. Another ten thousand people turned to crystal. The blood component is — minimal. A drop from each child. Freely given. No harm. No death. Just — a drop.”

The logic was compelling. The logic was precise.

Daliya wanted to believe. Daliya wanted to believe because believing would mean that Samira was not entirely lost — that the sister who had gathered children for slaughter still had, somewhere beneath the sharp face and the sharp eyes and the five years of transmuted compassion, a core of goodness that was trying to protect rather than destroy.

“Show me,” Daliya said. “Show me the formula. Show me the containment geometry. Show me the blood component.”

Samira showed her. The notebooks, Zahra’s notebooks, stolen, the ink-stained pages spread on the cave floor. The formula. The proof. The containment geometry that Samira had identified, a real component, a genuine element of the circle, a legitimate requirement that did, in fact, include a blood component.

But the component was not what Samira described. The blood component was symbolic — a drop of blood from the person making the choice, not from children. The containment geometry required the alchemist’s blood, not the blood of the young. Samira had read the formula correctly. Samira had interpreted it incorrectly — or had interpreted it correctly and was lying about the interpretation, the distinction irrelevant because the result was the same: Daliya was being asked to bring the children to the transmutation circle based on a misrepresentation of the formula.

Daliya did not know this. Daliya could not read the Djinn mathematics. Daliya could not verify the interpretation. Daliya could only trust her sister — the sister who had lied about the children, who had lied about the gathering, who had lied about everything for two years and was now, in a cave at dusk, asking to be trusted one more time.

“Okay,” Daliya said. “I’ll bring them. But I’m staying with them. Every moment. And if anything — anything — feels wrong, I’m taking them out.”

“Of course,” Samira said. “Of course.”

The agreement was the trap. The agreement was the freely made, uncoerced choice that sealed Daliya’s fate — not because Samira intended harm (Samira did not intend harm; Samira intended to prove her worth, to demonstrate that the gathering had not been entirely pointless, to salvage something from the wreckage of her purpose) but because the formula did not require what Samira believed it required, and the children did not need to be at the transmutation circle, and bringing them there would place them in proximity to the most powerful alchemical event in three hundred years, and proximity to power was proximity to danger, and danger did not care about intentions.

Free will. The trap that you set for yourself. The choice that you made freely, with full agency, with complete autonomy, that led you to the place where the cost came due.

Daliya chose to trust her sister. The choice was free. The choice was hers.

The choice would kill her.

Not immediately. Not that night. But the choice — the free, voluntary, uncoerced choice to trust Samira and bring the children to the transmutation circle — would place Daliya in the path of a rebound that she could not survive, and the rebound would come, and the cost would be paid, and the payment would be Daliya’s life.

The Djinn watched. From the spaces between. The ancient, patient, amoral watchers.

They did not intervene. They did not warn. They did not prevent.

They had never needed to.

Free will was enough.
