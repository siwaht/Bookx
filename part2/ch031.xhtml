Chapter 28: The Merchant Goes Home

Omar al-Tayyib arrived in Qasr Ramad on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were the days when the spice market opened early and the streets smelled of cinnamon and cardamom and the warm, complicated scent of a city that had been his home for forty-three years and had never, not once, felt like a place he belonged.

He had walked the last league. Not because his horse was tired — the animal was fine, the sturdy desert mare that Reyhan’s quartermaster had assigned him for the return journey. He walked because walking was how you arrived at a thing you were afraid of. Walking gave you time. Walking gave you the physical, undeniable experience of closing the distance between where you were and where you needed to be, and the closing was important, because the closing was the choice, and the choice was the only thing that had ever been his.

The streets of Qasr Ramad were the same. The same vendors in the same stalls selling the same goods to the same customers with the same arguments about price and quality and the eternal, never-resolved negotiation between what a thing was worth and what a person was willing to pay. The same children running through the same alleys. The same old men sitting in the same doorways, watching the same world pass by with the same expression of patient, amused, slightly disappointed familiarity.

The world had not changed. The overlap had not reached Qasr Ramad yet — the expanding zone of connection between worlds was still leagues to the south, advancing at its walking pace, one league per day, the steady, patient transformation of a world that was learning to be two worlds at once. Qasr Ramad was still Qasr Ramad. The spice market was still the spice market. The house on the Street of Weavers was still the house on the Street of Weavers.

And Fatima was still Fatima.

He knew she was home because the shutters were open. Fatima always opened the shutters in the morning — the wooden slats pulled back, the light let in, the house breathing the way a house breathed when the person inside it was alive and present and going about the business of being alive and present. The shutters were a signal. A declaration. A statement that said: I am here. I am not hiding. I am not ashamed.

Omar stood in the street and looked at the open shutters and felt the familiar, well-worn weight of the knowledge that he had been carrying for years — the knowledge that everyone in Qasr Ramad carried about his wife, the knowledge that was whispered in the market and laughed about in the coffee houses and discussed with the cruel, inexhaustible fascination that people reserved for the failures of others.

He had known. He had always known. The way you knew the sun would rise — not because you had evidence, not because you had proof, but because the alternative was a darkness so complete that the mind refused to contemplate it. He had known that Fatima was unfaithful the way he had known that the earth was solid — as a fact so fundamental that questioning it would require questioning everything else, and questioning everything else was a luxury that a merchant who needed to function, who needed to trade, who needed to maintain the fragile, essential stability of a life built on commerce and reputation and the careful management of what people believed about you — could not afford.

So he had chosen not to know. Actively. Deliberately. With the full, conscious, eyes-open awareness of a man who was making a choice and understood the cost of the choice and was willing to pay it. He had chosen to trust Fatima. Not because she deserved trust — trust was not a thing that was deserved or earned or merited. Trust was a thing that was given. Freely. Without conditions. Without evidence. Without the corrosive, relationship-destroying demand for proof that turned trust into surveillance and surveillance into suspicion and suspicion into the kind of jealousy that ate a man alive from the inside out.

He had seen that jealousy. In other men. In the husbands who followed their wives and checked their letters and interrogated their servants and built elaborate architectures of control that they called love and that were, in fact, the opposite of love — the desperate, suffocating need to possess another person so completely that the person ceased to be a person and became a thing. A possession. A piece of property whose value was determined by its exclusivity.

Omar had refused that. He had refused the jealousy and the surveillance and the control and the masculine, culturally sanctioned insanity of treating a woman as a thing to be owned rather than a person to be trusted. He had refused it not because he was noble or enlightened or morally superior — he was none of those things, he was a merchant, a man who bought and sold goods for a living and understood the economics of exchange better than he understood the economics of the heart. He had refused it because the alternative was worse. Because the jealousy would have consumed him. Because the surveillance would have destroyed him. Because the control would have turned him into the kind of man he despised — the kind of man who measured his worth by his wife’s fidelity and found himself bankrupt.

The cost of trust was humiliation. The whispers. The laughter. The social, public degradation of being the man whose wife slept with everyone. The cost was real. The cost was the look in the eyes of the spice vendor when Omar bought cinnamon, the look that said: I know. Everyone knows. And you know that everyone knows. And the knowing is the punishment.

The payment for trust was peace. The internal, unshakeable peace of a man who had made a choice and was living with the choice and was not, despite the whispers and the laughter and the looks, destroyed by the choice. The peace of a man who could function. Who could trade. Who could walk through the streets of Qasr Ramad with his head up and his back straight and his eyes clear, because the choice he had made was his choice, and the making was the only thing that was his, and the only thing that mattered.

The Balance. The cost of trust was humiliation. The payment was peace. The exchange was not fair. But it was equivalent. And equivalence, Omar had learned, was the only kind of fairness that the universe offered.

He had learned more on the journey. He had learned it in the desert, watching alchemists reshape matter with circles drawn in sand. He had learned it at Iram, watching a boy choose to become a bridge between worlds. He had learned it from Khalil — the soldier with the clay arm, the man who had paid the highest price Omar had ever witnessed and was still standing, still teaching, still speaking to a mad sister who could not hear him because the speaking was the only transmutation that cost nothing.

He had learned that trust was a transmutation. The most common transmutation in the world — more common than turning lead to gold, more common than healing wounds, more common than any circle drawn on any floor in any laboratory in the Cradle. Trust was the transmutation of uncertainty into stability. Of doubt into function. Of the raw, chaotic, unmanageable complexity of another person’s inner life into something that you could live with. Something that you could build on. Something that allowed you to wake up in the morning and go to the market and buy cinnamon and sell silk and be a person in the world.

Trust did not require a circle. Trust did not require geometry or symbols or the precise, mathematical framework of alchemical energy transfer. Trust required only the choice. The free, conscious, deliberate choice to believe in something that you could not prove and to accept the cost of that belief and to keep believing even when the cost was high and the evidence was against you and the whispers were loud and the laughter was cruel.

Trust was faith without religion. Trust was alchemy without circles. Trust was the most human transmutation — the transformation of fear into function, of doubt into daily life, of the devastating, universal uncertainty of loving another person into the fragile, essential stability of a life shared.

Omar climbed the steps. Three steps. Stone. Worn smooth by forty-three years of feet — his feet, Fatima’s feet, the feet of the children they had never had because Fatima had not wanted children and Omar had not insisted because insisting was a form of control and control was the thing he had refused.

He knocked. Not because the door was locked — the door was never locked, Fatima did not believe in locked doors, Fatima believed that a house should be open the way a heart should be open, and the openness was either a philosophy or an excuse and Omar had never decided which and had chosen not to decide because the choosing would have required the kind of examination that he had spent years avoiding.

He knocked because knocking was polite. Because knocking was the act of a man who respected the boundary between the street and the home, between the public and the private, between the world that knew everything about his wife and the world that existed inside the house where the knowing was different — more intimate, more complicated, more real.

The door opened.

Fatima was forty-one. She was beautiful. Not the beauty of youth — the beauty of a woman who had lived fully and unapologetically and had allowed the living to mark her face with the honest, irreversible evidence of experience. Lines around her eyes. Lines around her mouth. The lines of a woman who had laughed and cried and argued and loved and done all of it without the careful, self-protective restraint that most women learned to apply to their faces like cosmetics, the restraint that said: I am appropriate. I am contained. I am what you expect me to be.

Fatima was not what anyone expected her to be. Fatima was what she was. And what she was — what she had always been — was a woman who refused to be contained.

“Omar,” she said. Not surprised. Not guilty. Not the performative innocence of a woman who had been caught and was pretending she hadn’t been. Just — his name. Spoken the way she had always spoken it. With warmth. With familiarity. With the complicated, indefinable tone of a woman who had been married to a man for twenty-three years and had never, not once, been faithful to him and had never, not once, stopped loving him.

“Fatima,” he said.

She stepped aside. He entered. The house was the same — the same tiles on the floor, the same cushions on the divan, the same smell of jasmine and cooking oil and the domestic, irreplaceable scent of a home that was lived in. The same house he had left three months ago, when Reyhan’s column had needed a supply contractor and Omar had volunteered because the journey paid well and because the journey took him away from Qasr Ramad and because being away from Qasr Ramad was, sometimes, easier than being in Qasr Ramad with the whispers and the laughter and the knowing.

They sat. On the divan. The way they had sat a thousand times — facing each other, the cushions between them, the distance that was not distance but space. The space that married people maintained between them — the negotiated, carefully calibrated gap that allowed two people to share a life without sharing every thought, every feeling, every secret.

“I know,” Omar said.

Two words. The simplest words in any language. The words that every person feared hearing and every person needed to say. The words that were the beginning of every honest conversation and the end of every dishonest one.

Fatima did not flinch. Did not look away. Did not perform the expected, culturally scripted response of a woman who had been confronted with her infidelity — the tears, the denial, the desperate, humiliating plea for forgiveness that was not really a plea for forgiveness but a plea for the continuation of the lie.

She looked at him. The dark eyes — the eyes that had attracted him twenty-three years ago, the eyes that were still the most beautiful eyes he had ever seen — looked at him with the steady, unflinching directness of a woman who had been waiting for this conversation for years and was, at last, relieved that it was happening.

“I know you know,” she said. “I’ve always known you know.”

“Then why—”

“Because you chose not to know. And I chose to let you choose. And the choosing — yours and mine — was the only thing that kept us together. The only thing that made the marriage work. The only thing that made the love — and it is love, Omar, it has always been love — possible.”

The silence between them was not empty. The silence was full — full of twenty-three years of unspoken words, full of the weight of a truth that had been carried by both of them and acknowledged by neither, full of the particular, devastating intimacy of two people who had been lying to each other for so long that the lying had become a language of its own, a dialect spoken only by them, understood only by them, the private, shameful, necessary grammar of a marriage built on the agreement not to see what was visible.

“There was a man,” Fatima said. The words came out like stones dropped into still water — heavy, deliberate, each one creating ripples that spread outward and could not be recalled. “The first one. Before the others. Before the pattern. There was a man, and his name was Tariq al-Nassir, and he was your business partner, and he came to this house when you were traveling, and he sat on this divan, and he told me things.”

Omar’s hands went still. The merchant’s hands that were always moving — counting, calculating, the restless fingers of a man whose mind worked through his hands — went completely still.

“He told me you were a fool,” Fatima continued. Her voice was steady but her hands were not. Her hands were in her lap, the fingers interlaced, the knuckles white. “He told me you were a fool for leaving a woman like me alone. He told me I deserved better. He told me I was beautiful and wasted and that a woman of my intelligence should not be sitting in an empty house waiting for a merchant who loved his ledgers more than his wife. He told me these things every time you traveled. For two years. Every time you left, he came. Every time he came, he told me I was wasted. And the telling — the patient, persistent, two-year campaign of telling — worked. Because I was lonely, Omar. I was twenty years old and lonely and married to a man who was kind and good and absent, and the absence was the wound, and Tariq al-Nassir poured himself into the wound like salt.”

Omar was quiet. The stillness of his hands had spread to his entire body — the merchant who was always in motion, always calculating, always adjusting the terms of the exchange, was completely, absolutely still. The stillness of a man who was hearing something he had always known but had never allowed himself to know in detail, and the detail was worse than the knowing, because the detail had a name and the name was the name of a man Omar had trusted.

“Tariq al-Nassir,” Omar said. The name was flat. The name was the sound of a man processing a betrayal that was twenty-one years old and still fresh, still raw, still capable of producing the specific, devastating pain of discovering that the person who had destroyed your marriage was the person you had invited into your home. “My partner. The man I trusted with my accounts. The man I —” He stopped. His jaw worked. The muscles in his face moved in ways that faces moved when the person behind the face was fighting something — fighting the rage, fighting the grief, fighting the twenty-one-year-old betrayal that was suddenly not abstract but specific, not a general knowledge but a particular man with a particular name who had sat on this particular divan and seduced his particular wife.

“He laughed about it,” Fatima said. Quietly. “Afterward. When I — when it was done. When the thing that could not be undone was done. He laughed. Not cruelly. Triumphantly. The laugh of a man who had won a contest that the other contestant did not know was being played. He told me — he told me he had wanted me since the wedding. Since the day he stood beside you at the ceremony and watched me walk toward you in the white dress and thought: she should be walking toward me. He told me the partnership was the strategy. The business was the access. The friendship was the weapon. Everything — the accounts, the shared ventures, the dinners, the trust — was the architecture of a seduction that had been planned from the first day.”

Omar’s eyes were wet. The merchant’s eyes — the practical, unsentimental eyes of a man who calculated value and assessed risk and understood the economics of exchange — were wet. Not with tears. Not yet. With the precursor of tears — the burning, the pressure, the physical sensation of grief rising from the chest to the eyes like water rising in a well.

“And the servant,” Fatima said. “Your servant. Mahmoud. The old man who kept the house. The man you trusted to watch over me when you traveled. He knew. He knew from the beginning. Tariq paid him. Not much — a few coins, a bottle of wine, the small, contemptible currency of a betrayal that was not even expensive. Mahmoud opened the door for Tariq. Mahmoud left the house when Tariq arrived. Mahmoud stood in the street and watched the shutters close and counted the coins in his pocket and felt nothing — nothing at all — because the nothing was what the coins purchased. The nothing was the product. The nothing was what Tariq bought when he bought Mahmoud’s loyalty: the absence of conscience, the absence of duty, the absence of the basic, fundamental human obligation to protect the household you served.”

The tears came. Omar’s tears. The merchant who had never cried in Fatima’s presence — the man who had maintained his composure through twenty-three years of whispers and laughter and the daily, grinding humiliation of being the man whose wife slept with everyone — wept. The weeping was not dramatic — private. Internal. The tears running down the merchant’s face in silence, the jaw clenched, the body rigid, the only movement the tears themselves, tracking down the weathered cheeks and falling onto the calloused hands that lay motionless in his lap.

He wept for Mahmoud. That was the thing that broke him — not Tariq, not the seduction, not the twenty-one-year betrayal. Mahmoud. The old servant who had been with Omar’s family since Omar was a boy. The man who had taught Omar to tie his sandals. The man who had carried Omar on his shoulders through the spice market when Omar was four years old and the market was a forest of legs and the shoulders were the only safe place in the world. That man. That specific, irreplaceable, trusted man. Bought for a few coins and a bottle of wine.

“Mahmoud is dead,” Omar said. The words came through the tears. “He died three years ago. I sat beside his bed. I held his hand. I told him he was family. I told him —” The words stopped. The merchant’s voice, the voice that had negotiated a thousand deals and maintained its composure through a thousand crises, broke. The breaking was a sound — a small, involuntary sound, the sound of a man whose throat had closed around a grief that was too large to swallow and too sharp to hold.

Fatima moved. The woman who had been sitting across the divan, maintaining the negotiated distance, the carefully calibrated space between them — moved. She crossed the space. She sat beside him. She put her arms around him. The embrace was not gentle. The embrace was fierce — the fierce, desperate, all-consuming embrace of a woman who was watching the man she loved break and could not stop the breaking and could not undo the cause of the breaking and could only hold him while the breaking happened.

“I’m sorry,” she said. The words were muffled against his shoulder. “I’m sorry about Mahmoud. I’m sorry about Tariq. I’m sorry about all of it. I’m sorry that I was lonely and that the loneliness made me weak and that the weakness destroyed the one thing in my life that was good.”

And then Fatima cried.

Fatima, who did not cry. Fatima, who considered crying a form of manipulation. Fatima, who had maintained her composure through twenty-three years of infidelity and whispers and the daily, exhausting performance of being a woman who refused to be ashamed of what she had done. Fatima cried. The crying was ugly. The crying was the raw, uncontrolled, physically devastating weeping of a woman who had been holding something for twenty-one years and had just set it down and the setting down was not relief but collapse, the structural failure of a wall that had been bearing weight for too long and had finally, in the presence of her husband’s tears, given way.

They held each other. On the divan. In the house on the Street of Weavers. The merchant and his wife. The man who had chosen not to know and the woman who had chosen not to tell. Holding each other and weeping together for the first time in twenty-three years of marriage, the weeping not a performance and not a manipulation and not a strategy but the simple, devastating, irreducibly human act of two people sharing a grief that neither could carry alone.

The weeping lasted a long time. The weeping lasted until the light through the shutters changed from morning to midday and the sounds from the street changed from the opening vendors to the midday crowd and the house on the Street of Weavers was quiet except for the sound of two people breathing together, the breathing synchronized the way breathing synchronized when two bodies were pressed together and the pressing was not desire but need, the fundamental, animal need to be held by another person when the world was too heavy to hold alone.

“Tariq al-Nassir,” Omar said. His voice was hoarse. The voice of a man who had wept until the weeping had scraped his throat raw. “I dissolved the partnership. Eight years ago. I told him it was business. I told him the accounts didn’t balance. I told him —” He paused. “I knew. Even then. I didn’t know the details. I didn’t know about Mahmoud. But I knew about Tariq. The way he looked at you. The way he looked at me. The particular, unmistakable look of a man who has taken something from another man and is enjoying the taking more than the thing taken.”

“He’s in Basra now,” Fatima said. “He married. A young woman. Half his age. He writes to me sometimes. I don’t read the letters.”

“You don’t read them.”

“I burn them. In the kitchen fire. The paper curls and the ink runs and the words disappear and the disappearing is the only satisfaction I allow myself regarding Tariq al-Nassir.” She paused. “He was the first. He was not the last. But he was the one who — who opened the door. The door that I walked through and kept walking through because the walking was easier than the stopping and the stopping would have required looking at what I had become and the looking was the thing I could not do.”

“Until now,” Omar said.

“Until now.” Fatima pulled back. Looked at him. The dark eyes — red now, swollen, the beautiful eyes made ugly by weeping and made more beautiful by the ugliness because the ugliness was honest and honesty was the most beautiful thing in the world. “I am looking now, Omar. I am looking at what I have become. And what I see is a woman who was lonely and who made a choice and who kept making the choice because the making was easier than the stopping and the stopping would have required courage and I did not have courage. I had beauty and I had intelligence and I had the ability to attract men and I used all of these things as substitutes for the one thing I did not have, which was the courage to be alone.”

“The love,” Omar said. The word was heavy. The word was the heaviest word in any language — heavier than death, heavier than loss, heavier than permanence. Because love was all of those things and none of those things and the space between all of those things. “You love me.”

“I love you. I have always loved you. I will always love you. And I have been unfaithful to you since the second year of our marriage. And the two things — the love and the unfaithfulness — are not contradictions. They are — coexistences. They exist in the same space. The same heart. The same woman. And the coexistence is not a failure. It is — what I am.”

Omar was quiet. The house was quiet. The street outside was not quiet — the vendors and the children and the old men in the doorways, the eternal, chaotic, beautiful noise of Qasr Ramad. But inside the house, in the space between the man and the woman on the divan, the silence was absolute.

“I saw things,” Omar said. “On the journey. I saw a man with an arm made of clay who spoke to his mad sister every day because the speaking was the only thing that cost nothing. I saw a woman who had killed forty-three people activate a healing circle and heal a broken wrist. I saw a boy choose to become a bridge between worlds. I saw a woman who had been dying for three hundred years teach students in the hours before her death because the teaching was more important than the dying.”

He paused. Looked at his hands. The merchant’s hands — calloused from rope and reins, stained with the dyes of the fabrics he traded, the hands of a man who had spent his life handling goods and calculating prices and understanding, with the practical, unsentimental clarity of a businessman, the difference between what a thing cost and what a thing was worth.

“I learned something,” he said. “About the Balance. The alchemists’ law. The principle that says every transmutation has a cost, and the cost is always exactly what you can least afford to pay. I learned that the law is real. I learned that the law is absolute. And I learned that the law applies to everything — not just alchemy. Everything. Every choice. Every relationship. Every marriage.”

“The cost of our marriage,” Fatima said. Not a question.

“The cost of our marriage is the truth. The truth that you are unfaithful. The truth that I know. The truth that I chose not to know. The cost is the lie — the daily, exhausting lie of pretending that the truth is not the truth. The cost is the whispers and the laughter and the looks. The cost is the humiliation.”

“And the payment?”

“The payment is you. The payment is twenty-three years of marriage to a woman who is brilliant and beautiful and impossible and who has never, not once, been what I expected her to be. The payment is the love. The real love. The love that exists not because of fidelity but despite infidelity. The love that survives the truth because the love is larger than the truth.”

“Is the exchange equivalent?”

Omar looked at her. At his eyes. At the lines around the mouth. At the woman who had been his wife for twenty-three years and his mystery for longer than that and who was, in this moment, more honest with him than she had ever been.

“No,” he said. “The exchange is not equivalent. The exchange is not fair. The cost exceeds the payment. The humiliation exceeds the love. The truth exceeds the lie. By any rational calculation, by any merchant’s ledger, by any honest accounting of what I have given and what I have received — the exchange is unbalanced. I have paid more than I have received. The deficit is real.”

“Then why—”

“Because the Balance is not the only law. Because there is another law — a law that the alchemists don’t write in their circles, a law that the Djinn don’t trade in, a law that exists in the space between the cost and the payment, in the gap between what is owed and what is given. The law of — choosing. The law that says: I choose this.

Fatima’s eyes were bright. Not with tears — Fatima did not cry, Fatima had never cried in Omar’s presence, Fatima considered crying a form of manipulation and refused to manipulate the man she loved. Bright with something else. With recognition. With the devastating, beautiful recognition of a woman who had been seen — truly seen, completely seen, seen in the fullness of her contradictions and her failures and her love — and had not been rejected.

“You’re staying,” she said.

“I’m staying.”

“Even knowing.”

“Especially knowing. The knowing was the only thing I was afraid of. The knowing was the cost I couldn’t pay. And now I’ve paid it. And the paying is — lighter than I expected. The paying is almost — nothing. Because the knowing was never the weight. The not knowing was the weight. The pretending was the weight. The choosing not to know was the heaviest thing I have ever carried, and I have been carrying it for twenty-one years, and I am putting it down now, and the putting down is the lightest thing I have ever felt.”

He reached across the space between them. The merchant’s hand — calloused, stained, the hand of a man who understood the economics of exchange — took the hand of the woman who had been his wife for twenty-three years and his mystery for longer.

“I knew,” he said. “I always knew. I chose not to know. The choosing was the only thing that was mine.”

Fatima held his hand. The grip was warm. Real. The grip of a woman who had been holding things — secrets, lovers, contradictions, the weight of being a person who refused to be contained — for twenty-three years and was, at last, holding the one thing she had always wanted to hold.

Her husband’s hand. Offered freely. Without conditions. Without the demand for change or the expectation of fidelity or the suffocating, love-destroying insistence that she become someone other than who she was.

“The exchange is not equivalent,” Fatima said. “You’re right. The cost exceeds the payment. The deficit is real.”

“Yes.”

“But the choosing—”

“The choosing is enough.”

The house on the Street of Weavers was quiet. The shutters were open. The light came in. And two people who had been married for twenty-three years and had never, until this moment, been honest with each other — sat on a divan and held hands and said nothing, because the nothing was the most honest thing they had ever shared, and the sharing was the most balanced exchange they had ever made.

Outside, Qasr Ramad continued. The vendors sold. The children ran. The old men watched. The world turned. The overlap advanced — one league per day, the walking pace, the steady transformation of a world that was learning to be two worlds at once.

Omar had passed through a village on the road from Iram. A small settlement — mud-brick houses, a well, a handful of date palms, the kind of place that existed on no map and mattered to no one except the people who lived there. He recognized it from the reports that had circulated through Reyhan’s camp — the village where children had gone missing, taken in the night, gathered by agents of the Bloodsworn for purposes that the reports described in language so careful and so clinical that the horror bled through the restraint like ink through cloth. The children had been returned. Reyhan’s soldiers had found them during the chaos at Iram — confused, frightened, alive — and had brought them home in a supply wagon, wrapped in blankets, fed on dates and flatbread, carried the last stretch by soldiers who had daughters of their own and held these children with the fierce, wordless tenderness of men who understood what had almost happened. Omar had watched the mothers come running. He had watched the fathers stand in doorways with their hands shaking. He had noticed, without understanding, that the sigil scars on the children’s arms were fading — the wound-like marks that the Bloodsworn’s Voidcraft had inscribed into young skin losing their angry color, the edges softening, the tissue healing with a speed that no physician would have called natural. The Bloodsworn were destroyed. Rashid al-Fahim was dead. The Reckoner had nothing left to collect, and the instruments of collection — the sigils, the compulsions, the organic geometries that had been carved into flesh — were losing their hold, the way a vine lost its grip on a wall when the roots were cut. The village was healing. Omar had seen this and had not understood it, but he had felt it — the quiet, unremarkable miracle of a world that was slightly less broken than it was yesterday.

The overlap would reach Qasr Ramad eventually. The shimmer in the air. The warmth in the earth. The voice in the wind. The world would change. Everything would change.

But in the house on the Street of Weavers, the change had already happened. The most common transmutation in the world — the transformation of a lie into a truth, of pretending into knowing, of the crushing, universal uncertainty of loving another person into the fragile, essential honesty of a life shared — had been performed. Without a circle. Without geometry. Without the precise, mathematical framework of alchemical energy transfer.

With nothing but a choice. Freely made. With full understanding. The way every choice had always been.

The merchant was home.

The exchange was not equivalent.

But the choosing was enough.
