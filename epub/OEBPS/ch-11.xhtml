Chapter 12

Auremere Nights

The second visit to Morgause's court was different from the first.

The first visit had been reconnaissance — a player entering a new board, mapping the pieces, learning the rules. The second visit was play. Real play. The kind where the pieces move and the moves have consequences and the consequences are measured not in points but in pieces of yourself you didn't know you were wagering.

Wren arrived at Auremere in the evening. Deliberate — evening light was kinder than morning light, and kindness was a currency in Morgause's court, and arriving in kind light was a signal that said I understand your aesthetic and I am willing to play by your rules and I am not afraid of the dark.

He was afraid of the dark. But fear, like everything else in his life, had been filed and processed and converted into something useful.

Silke was not with him this time. Silke's presence on the first visit had been a statement: I come prepared. Her absence on the second was a different statement: I come trusting. The trust was a lie. The lie was the play.

Morgause met him in the garden-throne-room. She was wearing something that was not quite a dress and not quite armor — silk over steel, beauty over strength, the sartorial philosophy of a woman who believed that the most dangerous thing in the world was something beautiful that could kill you.

"You came alone," she said.

"I came trusting."

"You came performing trust. There's a difference. But the performance is appreciated. Performance is the highest form of respect in my court — it means you think I'm worth the effort."

They walked. Through the garden, past the fountains, into a part of the palace Wren hadn't seen before — a private wing, smaller, quieter, the rooms scaled for intimacy rather than spectacle. The incense was lighter here. The narcotic edge was gone — or reduced, or replaced with something subtler, something that didn't slow the mind but opened it, made the edges softer, made the distance between two people feel smaller than it was.

"I want to show you something," Morgause said.

The something was a room. Small — smaller than Wren's study, smaller than his room in the House. The walls were covered not in gold or silk but in paper. Handwritten paper. Letters, notes, sketches, maps. Pinned to the walls the way Wren pinned maps to his study walls — with the desperate, obsessive precision of a person who is trying to hold a world together with pins and paper.

"My study," Morgause said. "Not the throne room or the court. This. The real work happens here. The same way your real work happens in your study, behind the door the House opens reluctantly."

Wren looked at the walls. The papers were — he read them quickly, the way he read everything, processing, filing — intelligence reports. Trade analyses. Military assessments. Diplomatic correspondence. The administrative machinery of a kingdom, laid bare. Morgause's kingdom. Auremere's guts, pinned to the walls.

"You're showing me your vulnerabilities," he said.

"I'm showing you my work. The vulnerabilities are implied." She sat in a chair — not a throne, a chair, worn and comfortable, the chair of a person who spent hours in it. "I've been running Auremere for six hundred years. Alone. My court is beautiful and my courtiers are brilliant and none of them — not one — understands what it costs to keep the beauty running. The logistics. The intelligence. The constant, exhausting management of a thousand moving pieces that would fly apart if I stopped paying attention for a single day."

She looked at him. The look was — not the seductive look, not the political look. A different look. The look of a person who has been carrying a weight alone for a very long time and has just met someone who carries a similar weight and the meeting is not strategic but human — the recognition of a shared condition, the way two people with the same illness recognize each other in a waiting room.

"You understand," she said. "You're the only person I've met in six hundred years who understands. Not the power or the politics. The work. The daily, unglamorous, invisible work of keeping a kingdom alive while the person who's supposed to be doing it — in your case, Merlin; in my case, my own pride, which is a different kind of sleeping sorcerer — while that person does nothing."

"Your pride is your sorcerer?"

"My pride is the thing that built Auremere. The thing that made me a witch-queen. The thing that sustains my power and my court and my beauty and my — everything. But pride is also the thing that prevents me from asking for help, from admitting weakness, from saying to anyone — to my courtiers, to my allies, to the other witches — that I am tired. That the work is too much. That I have been doing this for six hundred years and the six hundred years have not made it easier, only more familiar, and familiarity is not the same as ease."

She was being honest. Wren could tell — not through analysis but through recognition. The recognition of hearing your own condition described by someone else.

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked.

"Because you're the only person who would understand. And because understanding is the most intimate thing two people can share. More intimate than bodies. More intimate than secrets. Understanding is the thing that makes you vulnerable, because the person who understands you is the person who can destroy you, and the destruction is not through force but through knowledge — the knowledge of where you're weak, where you're tired, where the mask is thinnest."

"You're making yourself vulnerable to me."

"I'm making us vulnerable to each other. That's the difference between seduction and connection. Seduction is one-directional. Connection is mutual. I'm not seducing you, Wren. I'm connecting with you. The connection is more dangerous than the seduction, for both of us."

The room was quiet. The papers on the walls rustled — not from wind but from the building itself, Auremere's version of the House's creaking, the palace expressing something that was not quite approval and not quite warning but the space between.

Wren sat in the other chair. The chair was comfortable — worn, shaped by years of use, the chair of a person who worked. He sat and looked at Morgause and saw — not the witch-queen, not the Witch of Pride, not the golden, radiant, intoxicating being that the reports described. He saw a woman. A tired woman. A woman who had been carrying a kingdom for six hundred years and had never been able to say so.

"I understand," he said.

"I know."

The silence that followed was not strategic. It was the quiet of two people who have stopped performing, and it was the most honest thing either of them had experienced in a very long time.

She kissed him.

Not strategically, not as a move in a game. The kiss was — he would analyze it later, in the study, with the maps and the letters, and the analysis would be thorough and clinical and completely inadequate, because the kiss was not a thing that could be analyzed. The kiss was a thing that could only be experienced, and the experience was—

Warm. The kiss was warm. Not pilot-light warm — human warm. The warmth of a mouth on his mouth, the warmth of a hand on his face, the warmth of a person who was touching him not because the touching served a purpose but because the touching was the purpose, and the purpose was connection, and connection was the thing he had been starving for without knowing he was hungry.

He kissed her back. The kissing back was not strategic. It was the most dangerous thing he had done since arriving in Auremere, because it was real, and the realness would compromise the strategy, blur the lines, turn the player into a person and the person into a vulnerability.

He kissed her back anyway.

The kissing lasted — he didn't know. Time in Auremere was unreliable. The palace, like the House, had opinions about time, and the palace's opinion was that time should serve beauty, and beauty required that certain moments last longer than physics permitted.

When the kissing stopped, they were still in the chairs. Still in the study. Still surrounded by the papers and the maps and the intelligence reports and the accumulated weight of two kingdoms managed by two people who were too tired and too proud and too necessary to stop.

"This complicates things," Wren said.

"Everything worth having complicates things. Simplicity is for people who can afford it. We can't."

"I'm supposed to be managing you. Feeding you false intelligence. Turning your attention inward. Making you paranoid about your own court."

"I know. I've known since your first visit. The intelligence you've been feeding me is approximately sixty percent accurate and forty percent fabricated, and the fabrications are designed to make me suspicious of my eastern trade minister, who is, in fact, loyal, and my naval commander, who is, in fact, competent. The fabrications are good. I almost believed them."

"Almost."

"Almost. The 'almost' is the compliment. No one has come close to deceiving me in three hundred years. You came close. That's why I kissed you."

"You kissed me because I almost deceived you?"

"I kissed you because you're the most interesting person I've met in six hundred years, and interest is the only emotion my pride permits, and the permission is the closest thing to love that a witch-queen can feel."

Wren looked at her. The looking was — complicated. Everyone had warned him about this: the complication of genuine feeling in a relationship that was supposed to be purely strategic.

The feeling was real. The feeling was a problem. The problem was that the feeling did not diminish the strategy — it enhanced it. A Morgause who felt connected to Wren was a Morgause who would not attack Thornhold. A Morgause who felt understood by Wren was a Morgause who would share intelligence willingly rather than having it extracted through deception. A Morgause who felt — whatever this was, this thing between them that was not love and not strategy but the space where love and strategy overlapped — was a Morgause who was more useful as an ally than as an enemy.

The feeling was real and the feeling was useful and the usefulness of the feeling was the thing that made Wren hate himself, because the hating was the proof that the feeling was real — you don't hate yourself for using something you don't care about.

"I have to go back," he said. "Tomorrow. The kingdom—"

"The kingdom. Always the kingdom." She smiled. The smile was sad. The sadness was real — the weapon and the wound and the thing between them that would never be resolved, because resolving it would require one of them to stop being what they were.

"Come back," she said. "When you can. If you can."

The words were Elena's words. The same words, spoken by a different woman, in a different context, with a different meaning. Elena's "come back" meant come home. Morgause's "come back" meant come play. Both meant I'll be here. Both meant I'll wait. Both meant something that Wren could not afford to feel and felt anyway.

"I'll come back," he said.

He meant it. He meant it the way he meant everything — with the full weight of his attention. And the meaning was the most dangerous thing he carried out of Auremere.

He rode west. Toward Thornhold. Toward the study. Toward the maps and the letters and the weight.

Behind him, Auremere glowed golden in the morning light. The beauty was real. The beauty was a weapon. And the aim was accurate.

And the warmth in his hands burned on. And the cost was always more than he expected. And the cost was always paid.
