Chapter 22

The Farm

The farm was smaller than he'd imagined.

Not small the way the study was small — cramped, dense, every surface loaded with meaning and consequence. Small the way a cupped hand is small — contained, warm, holding exactly what it needed to hold and nothing more.

He had come on an errand. A legitimate one — inspecting a supply route that ran south through the farming villages, checking the road conditions, assessing whether the route could handle increased trade traffic if the northern passes closed. The errand was real. The detour to Elena's family farm was not on any itinerary. He had simply... turned his horse. The turning was not strategic. The turning was the most honest thing he had done in months.

The farmhouse sat at the end of a dirt track, surrounded by vegetable gardens and a chicken coop and a fence that had been mended so many times it was more repair than original, a patchwork of wood and wire and stubbornness. Smoke rose from the chimney — thin, domestic, the smoke of a cooking fire, not a furnace, not a forge, not the metaphorical smoke of a kingdom burning.

A boy was in the yard. Fifteen, gangly, with Elena's brown hair and a grin that suggested he had just told a joke and was waiting for the universe to appreciate it. The chickens around him had the hunted look of animals who had heard too many punchlines.

"You're Wren," the boy said. Not a question. "Elena wrote about you. She said you work too hard and don't eat enough and you talk to walls. I'm Tam. The chickens are Gertrude, Beatrice, Margaret, and the fat one is Lord Featherbottom. He doesn't lay eggs but he has a commanding presence."

Lord Featherbottom regarded Wren with the imperious disdain of a chicken who had been given a title and intended to live up to it.

"Elena's inside," Tam said. "She's helping Mum with the — actually, I don't know what they're doing. Women things. Flour things. The kitchen smells amazing and I've been told to stay out of it, which is the story of my life. Do you want to hear a joke?"

"I—"

"A wizard walks into a tavern. The bartender says, 'We don't serve your kind here.' The wizard says, 'That's fine, I brought my own.' Then he pulls a chicken out of his hat. The chicken orders a beer. The bartender serves the chicken. The wizard says, 'What about me?' The bartender says, 'You're not a chicken.'" Tam paused. Waited. The grin widened. "The joke is that the chicken has better social skills than the wizard. It's a commentary on the isolation inherent in the pursuit of arcane power. Also, chickens are funny."

Wren stared at him. The staring was — he searched for the word — bewildered. The bewilderment of a man who has been living in a world of codes and strategies and triple meanings and has just encountered a fifteen-year-old who tells chicken jokes and means nothing by them except the joke itself.

"That's terrible," Wren said.

"Thank you. I have more. I have so many more. The chickens have heard them all. Gertrude is my best audience. She does this thing with her head—" He demonstrated. The head-tilt was, admittedly, very chicken-like. "See? Comedy gold. Elena says I'm going to die alone. I say I'll die surrounded by chickens who appreciate my art."

The front door opened. Elena's mother appeared — a woman who looked like Elena would look in thirty years, with the same brown hair gone grey at the temples and the same warm eyes and the same flour dusted her cheek, which was apparently a hereditary condition.

"You must be the apprentice," she said. "Come in. You're thin. We'll fix that. Tam, stop terrorizing the guest."

"I'm entertaining him."

"Same thing. Come in, both of you. The bread's almost ready and your father's arguing with the scarecrow again."

"The scarecrow?"

"He thinks it's not doing its job. The crows agree with him. It's a whole situation."

* * *

The kitchen was warm.

Not House-warm. A fire in a hearth. A pot on the stove. Steam rising from bread cooling on a rack. The warmth of a room that was warm because someone had built a fire and tended it and kept it going, not because the room itself had opinions about comfort.

Elena was at the table, rolling dough. Her sleeves were pushed up. Her arms were dusted with flour to the elbows. She looked up when Wren entered and smiled — the same smile, the stove-glow smile, the smile that meant exactly what it looked like — and said, "You came."

"I was in the area."

"You were not in the area. The supply route is twenty miles east. Tam, don't eat the dough."

"I'm quality-testing."

"You're stealing. Sit down. Wren, sit. Mum, this is Wren. Wren, this is my mother. She's going to feed you until you can't move and then she's going to send you home with more food. This is not negotiable."

Elena's mother — her name was Marta, Wren would learn, though everyone called her Mum, including people who were not her children, including the postman, including, apparently, the scarecrow — set a plate in front of him. Bread. Butter. Cheese. A bowl of soup that smelled like the memory of every good meal he'd ever eaten, concentrated into a single bowl.

"Eat," Marta said. It was not a suggestion.

He ate.

The soup was — he didn't have words. Not because it was extraordinary. Because it was ordinary. Perfectly, completely, devastatingly ordinary. Carrots and potatoes and onions and herbs from the garden and stock made from bones and time and the specific, unquantifiable ingredient of a woman who had been making this soup for thirty years and had refined it not through technique but through repetition, through the daily act of feeding people she loved.

The bread was the same. Not the House's philosophical bread. Just bread. Flour and water and yeast and salt and the warmth of an oven that was just an oven, not a sentient being with opinions about cuisine.

He ate and the eating was —

He zoned out.

It happened without warning. One moment he was at the table, tasting soup, hearing Tam tell another terrible joke about a chicken and a philosopher. The next moment he was in the study. Not physically — mentally. His mind had slipped its leash and gone back to the maps, the letters, the three witch-queens. Morgause's last message — a coded note about troop movements in the eastern provinces. Marcus's latest intelligence — a supply manifest that didn't add up, suggesting Nimue was stockpiling for something larger than a border skirmish. Viviane's silence — the silence that was always the most dangerous signal, because Viviane's silence meant she was working, and Viviane's work was invisible until it was catastrophic.

"Wren?"

He blinked. Elena was looking at him. The look was — not concerned. Patient. The patience of a person who has seen someone drift away and is waiting for them to come back, without urgency, without judgment, the way you wait for a cloud to pass.

"Sorry," he said. "I was—"

"Somewhere else. I know. You do that. You're here and then you're not here and then you're here again. Like a candle in a draft."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Eat your soup."

He ate his soup. He tried to stay. Tried to be in the kitchen, at the table, in the warmth. Tried to taste the bread and hear the jokes and see the flour on Elena's arms and the way Marta moved around the kitchen with the unconscious grace of a woman who knew where everything was because everything had been in the same place for thirty years.

He lasted four minutes before the drift took him again.

This time it was Aldric's report — the garrison's readiness assessment, the numbers that didn't add up, the three soldiers who had deserted last month and the two who were thinking about it. The desertion rate was a symptom. The symptom pointed to a disease: morale. Morale was low because the soldiers knew — everyone knew, even if no one said it — that the kingdom's defense rested on a sorcerer who wouldn't defend it and an apprentice who was seventeen and getting older by the day in ways that had nothing to do with birthdays.

"Wren."

He came back. Elena again. The patience again. But this time, underneath the patience, something else. Something that looked like — he searched for the word, and the searching was itself a symptom, because a year ago he would have known the word instantly, and now the words for simple human emotions were becoming harder to find, buried under the vocabulary of strategy and leverage and cost-benefit analysis.

Sadness. Elena looked sad.

Not for herself. For him.

"Tell me about the chickens," he said. The words came from the place below the systems — the place that was still seventeen, still human, still capable of wanting to hear about something that didn't matter.

Elena looked at him. The sadness didn't leave — it settled, the way sediment settles in water, becoming part of the landscape rather than a disturbance in it.

"Lord Featherbottom," she said, "has developed a rivalry with the neighbor's rooster. The neighbor's rooster is named Captain Clucksworth. The rivalry is over territory — specifically, the patch of dirt between our fence and their fence, which contains nothing of value except the principle of the thing. Lord Featherbottom patrols the patch every morning. Captain Clucksworth patrols it every afternoon. They have never actually met. The rivalry is entirely theoretical. Tam says it's the most honest political conflict he's ever seen."

Wren laughed.

The laugh surprised him. Not the fact of it — the sound. The sound was rusty. The sound was the sound of a mechanism that hadn't been used in months and was creaking back to life, reluctantly, painfully, like a door that had been closed too long and had forgotten how to open.

Tam looked up from his stolen dough. "He laughs! The wall-talker laughs! Elena, mark the calendar. This is historic."

"Tam."

"I'm just saying. Elena writes about you like you're a tragic figure in a ballad. 'The thin boy who works too hard and never smiles.' I was expecting someone grim. You're not grim. You're just — compressed. Like a spring. Like if someone let go of you, you'd fly apart."

The observation landed. It landed because it was accurate — accurate with the specific, unfiltered accuracy of a fifteen-year-old who hadn't learned to soften truth with tact. Wren was compressed. The compression was the job — the constant pressure of holding a kingdom together with warm hands and cold strategy, the pressure that was aging him, greying him, turning him into something harder and smaller and more efficient and less human.

"Tam," Elena said. Quiet. The quiet was a warning.

"What? I'm being observational. You said I should be more observational. You said—"

"I said you should observe the chickens, not the guests."

"Chickens are boring. Guests are interesting. Especially guests who laugh like they've forgotten how."

The kitchen was quiet. The fire crackled. The bread cooled. Outside, Elena's father could be heard arguing with the scarecrow about its posture.

"I should go," Wren said.

"Stay for dinner," Marta said. Not a suggestion or a request. The imperative of a mother who has decided that a thin boy needs feeding and will not be deterred by the boy's schedule, the boy's responsibilities, or the boy's apparent belief that he does not deserve to sit at a table and eat a meal he didn't earn.

"I have—"

"Dinner," Marta said. "Then you go. The kingdom will survive one dinner."

He stayed for dinner.

* * *

Dinner was chaos.

Not the sentient, purposeful chaos of the House. This was human chaos. The chaos of five people in a kitchen that was built for three, passing dishes and reaching over each other and arguing about whether the potatoes were done and whether Tam had washed his hands and whether the scarecrow was, in fact, doing its job or merely standing there as a monument to agricultural futility.

Elena's father — a man named Hector who was built like a smaller, friendlier version of Captain Aldric, with the same weathered face but none of the iron — argued with Marta about the potatoes. The argument was not about potatoes. The argument was about nothing. The argument was the sound of two people who had been married for twenty-five years and had run out of important things to disagree about and had discovered that unimportant disagreements were more fun.

Tam told jokes. The jokes were terrible. Everyone groaned. Everyone laughed. The groaning and the laughing were the same thing — the sound of a family that had developed its own language, a language made of bad punchlines and shared references and the specific, untranslatable humor of people who have known each other so long that the knowing itself is the joke.

Elena sat next to Wren. She didn't talk much during dinner — she listened, she passed dishes, she kicked Tam under the table when his jokes crossed the line from terrible to genuinely offensive (the line was narrow and Tam crossed it frequently). She was — Wren watched her, in the moments between zoning out — she was the center. Not the loudest, not the funniest, not the most visible. The center. The person around whom the chaos organized itself, the way water organizes itself around a stone — not by the stone's effort but by its presence.

He zoned out three times during dinner.

The first time: Morgause. A memory of her laugh — the real laugh, the unguarded one, the one that had haunted him since Auremere. The laugh and the strategy behind the laugh and the strategy behind the strategy and the question he couldn't answer: was the laugh real or was the realness the strategy?

The second time: Marcus. The latest intelligence drop — Nimue was moving her eastern regiments. Not toward Thornhold. Toward Morgause. The movement was unexpected. The unexpectedness meant either that Wren's scheme to turn the witches against each other was working or that Nimue had her own reasons, and Nimue's own reasons were always worse than the reasons you'd planned for.

The third time: the sealed room. Level 4 of the basement. The thing the House was built to contain. He'd been reading the old records — the records Merlin didn't know he'd found, hidden in the Unwritten Library, in a book that was writing itself as the events unfolded. The records suggested that the thing in the basement was not a prisoner. It was a failsafe. And failsafes, by definition, are the things you use when everything else has failed.

Each time he zoned out, Elena refilled his cup. Quietly. Without comment. The way you refill a cup for someone who is present and absent at the same time — with the patience of a person who understands that the absence is not a choice but a condition, and that the condition is getting worse, and that there is nothing to be done about it except to keep the cup full and wait.

After dinner, Hector took Wren outside to see the garden. The garden was — a garden. Vegetables in rows. Herbs in pots. A compost heap that smelled of decomposition and renewal, which were the same process viewed from different ends. Hector talked about soil composition and rainfall patterns and the specific variety of carrot that grew best in this particular clay, and the talking was — Wren listened, really listened, the way he'd listened to Elena talk about chickens — the talking was about carrots and was also about love. The love of a man for his land. The love that expressed itself not in words but in attention, in the daily act of tending something that grew slowly and rewarded patience and punished neglect and was, in its quiet way, the most honest relationship a person could have.

"You're welcome here," Hector said. Not looking at Wren. Looking at his carrots. "Anytime. Elena worries about you. She doesn't say so — she's not the saying type. But she worries. A father notices."

"I'm fine."

"You're not fine. You're the opposite of fine. You're whatever the word is for a person who is holding too much and won't put any of it down because they're afraid that if they put one thing down, everything falls." He pulled a carrot from the soil. Examined it. "Carrots don't worry about the whole garden. They just grow. One carrot at a time. The garden takes care of itself if you let it."

"The kingdom doesn't take care of itself."

"No. Kingdoms don't. That's why kingdoms are worse than gardens." He handed Wren the carrot. "Take this. Eat it on the road. It's good for your eyes. You look like you haven't seen anything green in months."

Wren took the carrot. The carrot was warm from the soil. The warmth was — not magical or strategic. Just warm. The warmth of a thing that had grown in the ground because someone had planted it and watered it and waited.

He left at dusk. Elena walked him to the gate — the farm gate, wooden, simple, no rust-words, no opinions about feet. Just a gate.

"Thank you," he said. "For dinner. For — all of it."

"Come back," she said. "When you can. If you can. The farm doesn't move. We'll be here."

He looked at her. The light was fading — the golden hour, the hour when everything looks warmer and softer and more like a memory than a moment. In the golden light, Elena looked like — he searched for the word and found it immediately, without effort, without the systems and the strategies getting in the way — she looked like home.

Not the House or Thornhold. Home. The word that means the place where you can stop. The place where the warmth doesn't need to burn because someone else has built a fire.

"I'll come back," he said.

He meant it. The meaning was the most dangerous thing he'd felt in months — more dangerous than Morgause's laugh or Marcus's emptiness or the three witch-queens and their armies and their schemes. Because meaning something — really meaning it, without strategy, without calculation — was a vulnerability. And vulnerabilities, in the world Wren lived in, were the things that got you killed.

He rode north. Toward Thornhold. Toward the study. Toward the maps and the letters and the weight.

Behind him, the farm grew smaller. The smoke from the chimney thinned. The chickens settled. Tam's laughter faded. The gate closed.

And Wren rode, and the carrot in his pocket was warm, and the warmth was the simplest thing in the world, and the simplicity was the thing he was losing, one day at a time, one letter at a time, one strategy at a time, and the losing was so gradual that he didn't feel it happening, the way you don't feel yourself aging until you look in a mirror and see a stranger.

The mirror was coming. But not yet. Not today.

Today, he had eaten soup. Today, he had laughed. Today, he had held a carrot that was warm from the soil and had meant something when he said he'd come back.

He didn't know it was the last good day for a long time. The not-knowing was the mercy. The mercy was the farm. The farm was still there when he finally came back to it.
