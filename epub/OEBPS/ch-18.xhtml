Chapter 19

Marcus's War

Marcus remembered what urgency felt like.

Not felt it — remembered it. The way a man who has lost his legs remembers running. The memory was precise: the quickening of the heartbeat, the narrowing of focus, the chemical surge that turned a body from a resting machine into a moving one. He could not experience it.

This was, he had discovered, an advantage.

Urgency made people sloppy. Urgency made generals rush their deployments and quartermasters skip their inventories and intelligence officers accept reports without verification. Urgency was the enemy of precision, and precision was the only currency Marcus had left.

He was in the supply depot at Greymarch — Nimue's capital, the iron city, the place where the Witch of Despair had built her war machine from the raw materials of human hopelessness. The depot was the largest in the northern kingdom: three warehouses, each the size of a cathedral, filled with grain and weapons and medical supplies and the thousand other necessities of an army that was currently three hundred miles south, pressing against Thornhold's eastern border.

The depot was also, as of tonight, on fire.

Not literally. Marcus was not crude. Fire was dramatic, visible, the tool of amateurs and revolutionaries. Marcus preferred the invisible fire — the fire that burned through ledgers and manifests and the delicate, interconnected web of logistics that kept an army fed and armed and functional. The fire that no one saw until the army starved.

He had been working for six hours. The work was meticulous — a man who could not feel boredom or fatigue, who could process information with the clinical efficiency of a machine because the parts of his brain that would have objected had been surgically removed along with his capacity for joy.

The manifests were the key. Every supply shipment to Nimue's eastern army passed through this depot, and every shipment was documented in triplicate — one copy for the depot, one for the army's quartermaster, one for Nimue's war council. Marcus had access to all three copies, because Marcus was the chief administrator, because Marcus was the man Nimue trusted to run the machine while she commanded the army, because trust was the thing that people gave to men who showed no emotion, mistaking the absence of feeling for the presence of loyalty.

He altered the manifests. Not dramatically — dramatically would be noticed. He altered them the way water alters stone: slowly, incrementally, with the patient, relentless precision of a force that has no deadline because it has no feelings about deadlines.

A grain shipment of ten thousand bushels became nine thousand. The missing thousand was redirected — on paper — to a secondary depot that did not exist. The secondary depot had been created by Marcus three months ago, a phantom facility with phantom staff and phantom inventory, existing only in the administrative records that Marcus controlled. The phantom depot absorbed the missing supplies the way a sponge absorbs water — invisibly, completely, without trace.

A weapons shipment of five hundred swords became four hundred and eighty. The missing twenty were listed as "damaged in transit" — a common enough occurrence that no one questioned it. The damaged swords were supposedly returned to the forge for repair. The forge received no swords. The forge's records, which Marcus also controlled, showed the swords as "received and repaired and redistributed." The redistribution went to a unit that did not exist, commanded by an officer who did not exist, stationed at a garrison that did not exist.

The phantom army. Marcus's masterpiece. A ghost force that existed only in paperwork — consuming supplies, requiring equipment, drawing pay — and that served no purpose except to drain Nimue's logistics from within. The phantom army had been growing for four months. It now numbered, on paper, three hundred soldiers. Three hundred soldiers who ate food that real soldiers needed, who wore armor that real soldiers lacked, who occupied barracks that real soldiers could have used.

The real army — the army pressing against Thornhold's border — was feeling the drain. Not yet critically. But the margins were thinning. The supply trains were arriving lighter. The weapons were arriving fewer. The replacements were arriving slower. The thinning was Marcus's work, and the work was precise, and the precision was the thing that made it invisible.

But Marcus was not satisfied with invisible.

* * *

Wren's last instruction had been clear: Hold. Do not escalate. Patience.

Marcus remembered what patience felt like. He could describe it. He could not practice it.

Nothing, it turned out, was not the same as patience. Nothing was the absence of the emotional friction that slowed action, that introduced doubt, that made a person hesitate before crossing a line.

Marcus had no friction. Marcus had no doubt. Marcus had no hesitation.

Marcus had a list.

The list was in his head — not written, never written, because written things could be found and found things could be used as evidence and evidence was the thing that killed spies. The list contained seven names. Seven generals in Nimue's army. Seven men who, if removed, would accelerate the collapse of the eastern campaign from months to weeks.

Wren had not authorized the removals. Wren had said hold. Wren had said patience. Wren had said do not escalate.

Marcus had considered Wren's instructions with the clinical detachment of a man evaluating a mathematical proof. The instructions were sound — from Wren's perspective. From the perspective of a man who was managing three fronts simultaneously, who was balancing the eastern war against the western schemes against the southern seduction, who needed the collapse of Nimue's army to happen slowly, controllably, in a way that could be managed and directed.

But Marcus was not managing three fronts. Marcus was managing one. And on that one front, the optimal strategy was not patience. The optimal strategy was acceleration. Remove the generals. Collapse the command structure. Let the army fragment. Let the fragments turn on each other. Let the turning become chaos, and let the chaos become the end of Nimue's kingdom.

The end of Nimue's kingdom was not Wren's goal. Wren wanted Nimue weakened, not destroyed. Wren wanted the northern threat neutralized, not eliminated. Wren wanted balance — the careful, strategic balance of a man who understood that a destroyed enemy was a vacuum, and vacuums were filled by things worse than the enemy you'd destroyed.

Marcus did not want balance. Marcus wanted revenge.

The revenge was the coldest thing in the world — the revenge of a man who remembered what passion felt like and could not feel it, and who had replaced all those feelings with the single, clinical, absolute determination to destroy the system that had made him what he was.

Nimue had made him. Nimue's kingdom had taken a man named Marcus — a man who had loved music and feared spiders and laughed at bad jokes and cried at beautiful sunsets — and had turned him into this. This thing. This machine that remembered feelings the way a museum remembers history — with perfect preservation and absolute distance.

The revenge was not for the feelings he had lost. The revenge was for the remembering. The remembering was the cruelty — not the absence of feeling but the presence of memory, the constant, clinical awareness of what had been taken, the ability to describe love without feeling it, to describe joy without experiencing it, to describe the warmth of a summer afternoon without the warmth reaching him.

The remembering was the wound. The wound was the engine. The engine was the list.

* * *

General Harwick died on a Tuesday.

The death was unremarkable — a fall from a horse during a routine inspection. The horse had been spooked by a snake. The snake had been placed by a stable hand who owed Marcus a favor — not of money or power but the more binding favor of silence.

General Harwick hit the ground at an angle that broke his neck. The breaking was instant. Marcus had ensured this — not from mercy but from efficiency. A painful death attracted attention. A painless death attracted paperwork.

The army mourned General Harwick for one day. The mourning was perfunctory — eunuch soldiers did not mourn, and the officers who could feel had learned, in Nimue's service, that mourning was a luxury the war machine did not provide. Harwick was replaced by his second-in-command, a man named Voss, who was competent but cautious, and whose caution would slow the eastern campaign by approximately two weeks.

Two weeks. The mathematics of assassination: one life equals two weeks of delay. The equation was clean. The cleanness was the thing that should have disturbed Marcus, if Marcus could be disturbed.

General Thorne died on a Thursday.

Food poisoning. The camp kitchen had served spoiled meat — an oversight, a failure of the supply chain, the kind of mundane disaster that happened in every army and was always blamed on the quartermaster. The quartermaster was blamed. The quartermaster was demoted. The quartermaster had, in fact, done nothing wrong — the meat had been spoiled deliberately, by a cook who owed Marcus a different kind of favor, the kind that involved a sister in a debtor's prison and a debt that Marcus had quietly paid.

Thorne's death was slower than Harwick's. Two days of fever and vomiting and the gradual, humiliating failure of a body that had survived a hundred battles and was being killed by a piece of pork. Marcus observed the dying from a distance — not from cruelty, not from satisfaction, but from the clinical interest of a man who was collecting data on the efficiency of different methods.

The data suggested that food poisoning was less efficient than a riding accident. Marcus filed the data. Adjusted his methods.

General Crane died on a Saturday.

A knife in the dark. Not Marcus's knife — Marcus did not do his own killing, because doing your own killing was inefficient and because Marcus's position as chief administrator required him to be visible, accountable, above suspicion. The knife belonged to a soldier — a eunuch soldier, one of the emotionless ones, one of Marcus's own kind — who had been given a simple instruction and had followed it with the simple, unquestioning obedience of a man who could not feel doubt.

Three generals in one week. The army noticed. The army could not help but notice — three senior commanders dead in seven days was not bad luck. It was a pattern. And patterns, in a military organization, triggered investigations.

Nimue ordered an investigation. The investigation was thorough — Nimue's counter-intelligence service was competent, professional, the product of a military culture that valued efficiency above all else. The investigators examined the deaths. They found the spooked horse (natural causes). They found the spoiled meat (supply chain failure). They found the knife (a disgruntled soldier, quickly identified and executed — the wrong soldier, a scapegoat Marcus had prepared in advance).

The investigation concluded: bad luck. Coincidence. The fog of war.

Marcus read the investigation's report with clinical interest. The report was wrong. The wrongness was his work. The work was precise.

But the work was also — he searched for the word, the word that described the thing he was doing that exceeded Wren's instructions, the word that described the gap between what he had been asked to do and what he was doing.

"Unauthorized." That was the word.

He remembered what guilt felt like. He could describe it with clinical accuracy. He could not feel it.

The absence of guilt was the thing that made the list possible. The absence of guilt was the thing that made Marcus dangerous — not to Nimue, not to the generals, but to Wren. Because Wren had given Marcus a leash, and the leash was the instruction hold, and Marcus had slipped the leash, and the slipping was not rebellion but optimization, and the optimization was the thing that Wren could not control.

Marcus looked at the list. Four names remained. Four generals whose removal would collapse Nimue's command structure entirely, turning the eastern army from a coordinated force into a collection of leaderless fragments.

He would wait. Not from patience — from calculation. Three deaths in one week was a pattern. Four deaths in two weeks would be a confirmation. The investigation needed time to close, the suspicion needed time to fade, the army needed time to settle into the new normal of a command structure that was three generals lighter.

He would wait. And then he would continue.

The list was the mission. The mission was the revenge. The revenge was the only thing that functioned in the space where feelings used to be.

Marcus sat in his office in Greymarch, surrounded by the manifests and ledgers and administrative records of a war machine he was dismantling from within, and remembered what satisfaction felt like.

The memory was precise. The memory was empty.

The emptiness was the thing he carried.

* * *

Three hundred miles south, in a study that smelled of ink and cold tea, Wren read Marcus's latest report and felt the specific, cold unease of a man who has built a weapon and is beginning to understand that the weapon has its own ideas about what to aim at.

He filed the unease. Picked up the next letter.

The filing was getting easier. That was the problem.
