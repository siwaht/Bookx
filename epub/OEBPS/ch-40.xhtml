Chapter 41

The Kingdom Mourns

Captain Aldric found the study at 6 AM.

He found it because the study door was closed and the study door was never closed at 6 AM — at 6 AM, the study door was open, because Wren was already inside, because Wren had been inside since 2 AM, because 2 AM was when Wren started and 6 AM was when Aldric arrived for the morning briefing and the door was always open because Wren opened it for him, every morning, without fail, for six years.

The door was closed. The closed was wrong.

Aldric opened the door. The door opened easily — no resistance, no creak, no House-opinion. Just a door. The ease was wrong too. The House always had an opinion about the study door. The House's silence was the first sign.

The blood was the second sign.

Aldric saw the blood and his body did what a soldier's body does — it went still. Still. The stillness of a man who has been trained to assess before reacting, to see before feeling, to understand the situation before the situation understands him.

He saw the blood on the desk. On the floor. On the window frame. He saw the broken window. He saw the bread knife — the bread knife, Wren's bread knife, the knife that had been a joke and a comfort and a weapon, embedded in the desk like a headstone.

He saw the manual. Four hundred pages. Centered. Untouched.

He saw the absence. The absence of a body. The absence of a person. The absence of the man who had been in this room every morning for six years, who had opened this door every morning for six years, who had looked up from the desk every morning for six years and said "Aldric" in the tone that meant "sit down, there's work to do."

The absence was — Aldric stood in the doorway and felt the absence the way you feel a missing limb. The phantom sensation of a presence that should be there and is not.

He walked to the desk. Slowly. The soldier's walk — measured, controlled, each step deliberate. He looked at the blood. He looked at the knife. He looked at the broken window.

He looked at the blood again.

The blood was pig's blood.

Aldric knew pig's blood. Aldric had been a soldier for twenty years. He had seen human blood and animal blood and the specific, terrible blood of men who had died on his watch, and he knew the difference the way a painter knows the difference between red and crimson — not through analysis but through experience, through the accumulated, bodily knowledge of a man who had lived in the presence of blood and had learned its varieties.

The blood was pig's blood. The death was staged. The apprentice was alive.

The knowledge landed and Aldric — Aldric, the iron-jawed, the steady, the man who had followed orders he disagreed with and carried weight he shouldn't have had to carry and had never, in twenty years of soldiering, broken — Aldric closed his eyes. The closing was not grief. The closing was relief. The relief was so intense, so physical, so completely overwhelming that his knees buckled and he had to put a hand on the desk to stay upright, and the hand landed in the pig's blood, and the pig's blood was warm, and the warmth was — the warmth was Wren's last joke, the last piece of theater, the warm blood of a man who was not dead but was gone.

Gone. Aldric opened his eyes. Looked at the manual. Looked at the desk. Looked at the study that was now his study, the kingdom that was now his kingdom, the weight that was now his weight.

He found the drawer. The second drawer — lift and pull, not just pull. The trick was in the manual. The letters were inside. Five envelopes. He found his — the one marked Aldric in Wren's precise hand.

He read it standing. He read it once. He did not read it again. He folded it and put it in his breast pocket, over his heart, where soldiers keep the things that matter.

"You bastard," he said. To the empty room. To the ghost. To the boy who had become a man who had become a shadow ruler who had become a friend, and the friend was the thing Aldric had not expected and could not replace and would carry, in his breast pocket, for the rest of his life.

"You magnificent bastard. You got out."

He distributed the other letters. Threadneedle's, delivered to the Broken Broom by a trusted runner. Bess's, hand-delivered, because Bess deserved the courtesy. Finch's, sent by official courier, sealed with the kingdom's seal, because Finch deserved the formality. Merlin's — the blank one, the one that weighed nothing and said everything — left on the sitting room table, beside the mead, where Merlin would find it.

Then Aldric went back to the study. Sat in the chair — not Wren's chair, his chair now, the chair that would mold to his body the way it had molded to Wren's. Opened the manual. Page one.

The wards. The ward anchors. The maintenance schedules.

He began to read. The reading was the work. The work was the weight. The weight was his now.

He carried it. The way he carried everything — with the steady, uncomplaining, iron-jawed determination of a man who understood that carrying was not a burden but a duty, and duty was not a prison but a choice, and the choice was the thing that made the carrying bearable.

He carried it. And he did not say: the study was empty. He said: the study was ready.

The distinction was Wren's last gift.

* * *

Threadneedle

Mrs. Threadneedle received the letter at the Broken Broom, delivered by a runner she recognized — one of Aldric's men, not one of hers, which told her everything she needed to know before she opened the envelope.

She opened the envelope. Read the letter. The reading took eleven seconds — Threadneedle read the way she did everything, with the efficient, economical precision of a woman who understood that time was currency and currency was power and power was the thing she traded in.

She folded the letter. Placed it in her pocket. Adjusted her hat.

"Bess," she said.

Bess was behind the bar. Bess was always behind the bar. The bar was Bess's kingdom the way the study had been Wren's — the place where the work happened, the place where the world was managed, the place where a woman with missing fingers and a survivor's eyes kept the village's secrets and the village's drinks in equal measure.

"I know," Bess said.

"You know."

"I know the way I know everything. Through the wood." Bess touched the bar — the wooden bar, the bar that had been polished by a thousand hands and had absorbed a thousand conversations and was, in its way, as sentient as the House, though its sentience was the sentience of a thing that had been loved into awareness rather than built into it. "The wood knows. The village knows. The knowing is in the air. The boy is gone."

"The boy is gone," Threadneedle confirmed. "The boy staged his death. The boy is alive. The boy is — south, I expect. The boy was always going south. The south was the only direction that made sense, given the variables."

"The girl's farm."

"The girl's farm."

They looked at each other. Two women. Two professionals. Two survivors of the specific, consuming, morally ambiguous work of managing information in a world that ran on secrets. They looked at each other and they did not say the thing that both of them were thinking, because the thing was not professional and the not-professional was the thing they did not do, not in public, not at the bar, not in the daylight where the saying could be heard and the hearing could be used.

Threadneedle adjusted her hat again. The adjustment was — Bess recognized it — the adjustment was the tell. Threadneedle's tell. The thing she did when she was feeling something and did not want the feeling to show. The hat-adjustment was the dam, and behind the dam was — Bess did not speculate. Bess respected dams. Bess had her own.

"The network will hold," Threadneedle said. "Aldric will manage. The kingdom will survive. The survival is the point."

"The survival was always the point. The boy made sure of it."

"The boy made sure of everything. The making-sure was his gift. The gift was also his disease. The disease is — the disease is gone now. The disease went south with the boy."

Threadneedle stood. Straightened her coat. Adjusted her hat a third time — the third adjustment, the one that meant the dam was holding but only just, the one that meant the professional was winning the war against the person but the war was close.

"Goodbye, Bess."

"Goodbye, Mrs. Threadneedle."

Threadneedle left. The door closed. The Broken Broom was quiet.

Bess stood behind the bar. She poured a drink — not for a customer, not for Threadneedle, not for the ghost of a boy who was not dead. For herself. A drink she did not serve. A drink she held in her hand — the hand with the missing fingers, the hand that was the price, the hand that was the proof that magic takes and the taking is the first lesson and the last.

She held the drink. She did not drink it. She held it the way you hold a candle at a vigil — not for warmth but for witness. The witness of a woman who had survived magic and had watched a boy walk into the same fire and had not stopped him because stopping was not something she could do, and the not-doing was the thing she carried, and the carrying was the price, and the price was two fingers and a drink she would not serve.

"Good," she said. To the empty pub. To the wood. To the village that knew and would not say. "Get out. Stay out. Don't come back."

The words were hard. The hardness was the love. The love of a woman who understood that the best thing you can do for someone who is drowning is not to jump in after them but to stand on the shore and shout: swim. Swim, you fool. The shore is right here. The shore has always been right here.

She set the drink on the bar. Untouched. The drink would sit there until closing, and then she would pour it out, and the pouring would be the goodbye, and the goodbye would be enough.

The Broken Broom was quiet. The wood was warm. The village turned, slowly, toward a morning that did not include the apprentice.

The morning was — the morning was just a morning. The ordinariness was the gift.

* * *

The Village

The butcher told the baker at 7 AM. The baker told the farrier at 7:15. The farrier told Giles the woodcutter at 7:30, and Giles told his wife, and his wife told the postman, and by 8 AM the entire village knew that the apprentice was dead.

The details traveled with the news, accumulating embellishment the way a snowball accumulates snow: assassins from the west, the shadow queen's remnants, the study covered in blood, the broken window, the body taken. By noon, the story had acquired a sword fight, a dying curse, and a heroic last stand that would have impressed Aldric and amused Wren and was entirely fictional.

The village mourned the way villages mourn — not with ceremony but with disruption. The market was quieter than usual. The Broken Broom closed early, which had not happened since the siege. Thomas the blacksmith, who had repaired the garrison's weapons for six years and had watched the apprentice transform the kingdom from the outside, set his hammer down at midday and did not pick it up again until the following morning. His wife found him sitting in the smithy, staring at the forge, and when she asked what was wrong, he said: "The boy who paid the soldiers is dead. The soldiers who protect us were paid by the boy. The boy is dead. Who pays the soldiers now?"

The question spread through the village the way the news had spread — quickly, unstoppably, with the specific, contagious anxiety of people who have been safe for so long that they have forgotten what unsafe feels like and are now, suddenly, remembering.

The soldiers were paid because of the apprentice. The trade routes were active because of the apprentice. The debts were managed, the disputes were settled, the kingdom functioned — all because of the apprentice. Everyone knew this. No one had said it. The not-saying had been the village's contribution to the great collective fiction that Merlin was running things, and the fiction had been comfortable, and comfort is the thing people protect most fiercely, more fiercely than truth, more fiercely than dignity, more fiercely than the nagging awareness that the thin boy who bought saffron at the market and drank tea at the Broken Broom was the only thing standing between them and the dark.

The answer came two days later, carried down from the House by a garrison runner: the sorcerer was in the study. The sorcerer was reading letters. The sorcerer was writing responses.

The village did not believe it. The village sent Thomas — Thomas, because Thomas was practical and observant and had the blacksmith's habit of assessing things by their structural integrity. Thomas went up Briar Lane. Thomas came back.

"He's there," Thomas said, standing in the Broken Broom, speaking to a pub full of people who had stopped pretending to drink and were openly listening. "He's at the desk. He's reading. He's writing. His handwriting is — I could see it from the door — his handwriting is terrible. He answered a letter about the Harrow Bridge toll dispute and his answer was wrong. But he answered it. He's trying."

The village absorbed this. The village weighed it — the way villages weigh everything, collectively, through the silent, communal calculus of people who have lived together long enough to think together.

The trying was not enough. The trying was also not nothing.

The village accepted the trying. The village had no choice. The village was practical, and practical people take what is available, and what was available was a thousand-year-old sorcerer who was trying, badly, to do the work that a twenty-three-year-old boy had done brilliantly for six years.

The fence between the two farmers — the fence that had been the subject of the first letter Merlin answered — was mended. Badly. The mending favored the wrong farmer. Aldric quietly corrected it.

It was a start.
