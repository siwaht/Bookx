Chapter 1

The Door That Wasn't There Yesterday

The thing about warm hands is that no one tells you what they're worth.

Wren's hands had been warm since the age of twelve — warm from the inside, warm as a fact of existence rather than a consequence of circumstance. The warmth had started on a Tuesday in November, which Wren would later learn was significant because Tuesdays were always significant, though by the time anyone explained this, Tuesdays had become the least of his problems.

At twelve, he had been kneading bread. His mother took in washing and washing paid for flour and flour became bread and bread was breakfast. The dough had been cold — November dough is always cold — and Wren pushed and folded and pushed and folded, the specific, physical, deeply unglamorous labor of turning a lump of pale resistance into something that could feed two people who deserved to be fed by more.

The dough warmed. Not from the pushing. From the hands.

His mother saw. She was elbow-deep in someone else's sheets, soap in her hair and red in her knuckles. She saw the dough rise and said nothing for three days. On the fourth day, she went to see the wise woman, and the wise woman held Wren's hands and turned them over and pressed her thumbs into the palms and said, "The child has a pilot light."

"Is it dangerous?"

"Everything is dangerous. There is a wizard. He takes apprentices."

The letter was sent. The letter was answered. Five years passed. Five years of bread and washing and the slow, grinding poverty of a village called Thornfield, which was exactly as pleasant as it sounds. Five years of warm hands and no one to explain them and the growing, quiet certainty that the warmth was either a gift or a curse and the difference depended entirely on who got to decide.

* * *

Wren stood at the end of Briar Lane and stared at the absence of a house.

Seventeen. A canvas bag over one shoulder containing everything he owned: one change of clothes, a book of folk tales that had belonged to his father before his father walked to the market and chose a different direction, a bread knife, and a lock of his mother's hair tied with the blue ribbon she wore on Sundays.

The lane ended at a hedge. Behind the hedge, a field. In the field, a cow.

"Right," Wren said. "So either the letter is wrong, or the house is invisible, or I've walked fourteen miles to talk to livestock."

The cow chewed. It was not helpful.

He read the letter again. The ink changed color — black to green to something that might have been purple or might have been wine.

To the child with the warm hands,

You have been identified as possessing latent magical ability of a precise and controlled nature. This is not a compliment. It is a diagnosis.

You are hereby offered an apprenticeship with the wizard Merlin (that's me) at his residence (that's the house at the end of Briar Lane). Room and board provided. Education provided. Sanity not guaranteed.

Come Tuesday.

— M.

P.S. The house is temperamental. Try being polite.

"Excuse me," Wren said to the hedge. "I'm looking for the house of the wizard Merlin."

The hedge moved. Not dramatically — it shifted the way a person shifts in a chair to make room. A gate appeared. Iron, black, covered in rust that spelled words: WIPE YOUR FEET.

Wren wiped both feet. The rust-words changed: ABOUT TIME.

Beyond the gate — a house. Wrong in the way a dream is wrong: everything present and accounted for, everything in approximately the right place, and yet the whole thing fundamentally off. Three stories, or possibly four. The top floor flickered between existing and not existing. The windows were all different sizes, placed at irregular intervals, and they didn't all face the same direction. Some looked out. Some looked in. One appeared to look sideways, into a direction Wren's eyes couldn't follow.

The front door was red. This was the only normal thing about the house, and somehow that made it worse.

Wren walked through the gate. The path curved, doubled back, split into two routes — one passing through a small, self-contained rainstorm hovering three feet off the ground. He took the dry path. The rainstorm struck the path not taken with a petulant bolt of lightning.

He reached the front door. It opened before he could knock.

The hallway beyond was dark and warm and smelled of pipe smoke, old books, and something like honey but sadder.

"Hello?" Wren called.

Something creaked. Not threatening. Settling. The first word of a conversation that would last for years — though not as many years as Wren expected, and not the kind of conversation he imagined.

He stepped inside. The door closed behind him — gently, the way a trap closes on something it intends to keep.

* * *

The sitting room was a disaster wearing the skin of a room.

Books stacked in towers that defied gravity. Papers on every surface. Bottles lined the windowsill — empty, half-empty, and one that was somehow more than full. A sleeping cat occupied a pile of documents it had claimed in 1847. The window showed a sunset happening in the wrong direction.

The man in the chair was not what Wren had expected.

He had expected a wizard. Robes. A staff. Dignity.

The man in the chair was wearing a dressing gown that had once been silk and was now mostly stains. His beard was patchy. His hair was grey and wild, standing in directions that suggested actual explosion. He was thin — the hollow thinness of someone who forgets to eat because eating requires caring about whether you continue to exist.

He was drinking. The bottle beside him suggested the glass had been filled many times. The bottle had not been refilled once.

His eyes, though.

One was blue — sharp, clear, winter-morning blue. The blue eye was merciless. The blue eye was kind.

The other was grey, half-closed, tracking something that wasn't in the room, something that might not be in this century.

Both eyes turned to Wren. The singing — he'd been singing badly about a woman named Margaret and a goat — stopped.

For one fraction of one moment, the mask was down. The brilliant, untouchable, thousand-year-old wizard was just a man in a stained dressing gown, looking at a boy in a doorway. And the looking was not hope. It was assessment — whether this new arrival would be useful enough to justify the inconvenience of acknowledging his existence.

The moment passed.

"You're small," said Merlin.

"I'm seventeen."

"That's not an answer to the observation." He drank. The glass refilled itself. "You're the one with the warm hands."

"The letter said—"

"I know what the letter said. The House wrote it. The House has been intercepting my mail for decades. It has opinions about my correspondence the way the kitchen has opinions about my diet." He lost the thread, visibly. Both eyes drifted. "What was the point?"

"The apprenticeship."

"Ah. Yes. That." He looked at Wren the way a man looks at a bill he'd forgotten about. "Show me your hands."

Wren held out both hands, palms up. Small, calloused, scarred.

Merlin leaned forward. The grey eye opened fully. The air between them became briefly solid — crackling, ozone-scented, the energy of immense power being used not to create or destroy but to perceive.

"Hm," said Merlin.

"Is that good or bad?"

"It's 'hm.' The sound a man makes when he sees something adequate and is too bored to pretend otherwise." He stopped. Looked at Wren — not at the hands but at the face. "You have a pilot light. Small. Steady. Reliable." He said "reliable" with faint contempt. "Do you know what I need?"

"A bath?" Wren said. The sarcasm was instinct — the armor of a boy who had learned that humor deflects blows better than flinching.

Merlin almost smiled. Almost.

"I need someone to manage things. The House. The wards. The — administrative elements." He waved vaguely at the chaos around him. "I am a thousand years old. I am the most powerful sorcerer in existence. I am also, by any reasonable metric, uninterested in the daily operations of maintaining a kingdom that exists primarily because people are afraid of me."

"A kingdom?"

"Thornhold. The territory protected by my presence. Several thousand people who sleep safely at night because the things that would eat them are afraid of the thing that lives in this house." He drank. "The thing that lives in this house would rather be drinking. Hence: you."

"You want me to run your kingdom."

"I want you to handle the tedious parts. Which is all of it." He set down his glass. "There are also three witch-queens on the borders who would very much like me dead, or at least diminished. They send armies. They send spies. They send temptations. I find the whole business exhausting."

Wren stared at him. "You're telling me that three hostile kingdoms are trying to destroy you, and your response is to hire a seventeen-year-old to deal with it because you find it exhausting."

"When you say it like that, it sounds irresponsible."

"It IS irresponsible."

"Yes. That's rather the point of having an apprentice. Someone responsible." He closed both eyes. The conversation, as far as he was concerned, was over. "The House will show you to your room. Breakfast is whenever the kitchen decides. Don't touch the mead in the basement. Don't open the sealed room on Level 4. Don't bore me."

He was asleep in thirty seconds. Not pretending — genuinely asleep, the way only the profoundly powerful or the profoundly indifferent can sleep in the middle of a conversation about the survival of thousands of people.

Wren stood in the sitting room of the most powerful sorcerer in the world and listened to him snore.

The House creaked. Not the settling creak. The creak of a building that has been waiting for someone competent and has finally, after centuries of disappointment, found a candidate.

"Where do I sleep?" Wren asked the walls.

The temperature shifted — warm to the left, cool everywhere else. The House was herding him.

He followed the warmth up the stairs, down a corridor, to a small room on the first floor. A bed, a desk, a chair, a window that showed Thornfield. His mother, hanging laundry. The sheets were white. The hands were red. The face was turned away.

Wren closed the curtain.

He unpacked in three minutes. Clothes in the wardrobe. Book on the desk. Bread knife in the drawer. Lock of hair under the pillow.

He sat on the bed. The walls were a pale, uncertain blue that shifted and warmed as he sat there.

"Thank you," he said to the room. "It's nice."

The blue brightened. Just slightly.

Wren did not sleep. He sat on the bed in the small room and thought about what Merlin had said. Three witch-queens. A kingdom sustained by reputation. A sorcerer who couldn't be bothered.

He thought: I came here to learn magic.

He thought: I am going to learn something else entirely.

He thought: I should leave. Walk the fourteen miles back. Tell my mother it was strange and I came home.

But the warmth in his hands was steady, and the House was breathing around him, and somewhere below, the most powerful man in the world was sleeping through a crisis, and someone had to be awake.

Someone always had to be awake.

Wren got up. Went downstairs. Found the study — the real study, not the sitting room, a room buried behind a door the House opened reluctantly, as if showing him a wound. Inside: a desk covered in unopened letters, unsigned orders, unanswered pleas. Months of correspondence. Years, maybe. The administrative detritus of a kingdom whose ruler had stopped ruling.

He sat down. Picked up the first letter. Read it.

A border dispute. Two farmers arguing over a fence line. The letter was three months old.

He picked up the next. A trade agreement requiring Merlin's seal. Six months old.

The next. A warning from a spy network — someone called "Threadneedle" — about troop movements on the northern border. Eight months old.

The next. A debt notice from someone called Mr. Finch. The amount was staggering. The tone was patient in the way that patience becomes threat.

Wren read them all. Every letter. Every plea. Every warning. Every debt. He read until the candle burned low and the House grew a new candle without being asked and he read by that light too.

By dawn, he understood.

The kingdom was not in danger. The kingdom was already falling. It had been falling for years, slowly, the way a building falls when the foundation cracks and no one notices because the walls are still standing and the paint is still fresh and the door still opens and closes and everyone assumes that a thing that looks intact must be intact.

The foundation was cracked. The walls were hollow. The paint was peeling in places no one looked. And the man who built it was asleep in a chair with a bottle in his hand and a snore that rattled the windows.

Wren put down the last letter. Looked at his hands. Warm. Steady. Calloused from bread and washing and the specific, unglamorous labor of keeping things alive.

"All right," he said to the empty study. To the House. To the kingdom. To no one. "All right."

He picked up a pen. Found ink. Found paper.

He began to write replies.

The first letter — the border dispute — he resolved with three sentences and a compromise that gave both farmers slightly less than they wanted and exactly what they needed. The trade agreement he amended, corrected two errors that would have cost the kingdom revenue, and signed with a stamp he found in Merlin's desk drawer, unused, still wrapped in the cloth it had been delivered in decades ago.

The spy report he read three times. Then he wrote a response to Threadneedle — careful, precise, asking the right questions. Not the questions of a seventeen-year-old. The questions of someone who understood that information was currency and currency was survival.

The debt notice he set aside. Mr. Finch would require a different approach. Mr. Finch would require a conversation.

By the time Merlin woke — at noon, stumbling into the kitchen demanding tea — Wren had answered forty-seven letters, identified three immediate crises, and begun to understand the shape of the thing he had inherited.

Not an apprenticeship. A kingdom.

Not a teacher. A burden.

Not magic. Politics.

Merlin looked at the stack of answered correspondence on the study desk. Looked at Wren. Both eyes open. The grey one seeing something — not the future, but the present, clearly, for the first time in years.

"You've been busy," Merlin said.

"Someone had to be."

A pause. Something flickered across Merlin's face — not guilt, not gratitude. Recognition. The recognition of a man who has been carrying a weight and has just watched someone else pick it up and is feeling not relief but the specific, complicated shame of having let it fall in the first place.

Half a second. Then the mask — the brilliant, chaotic, untouchable wizard who needed no one.

"Good," Merlin said. "Carry on."

He took his tea back to the sitting room. The chair groaned as he settled into it. Within minutes, the snoring resumed.

Wren stood in the study. The House creaked around him — not settling. Welcoming. The creak of a building that has found its keeper.

He picked up the next letter.

He had come to learn magic. He was learning something else entirely.
