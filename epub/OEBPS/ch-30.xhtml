Chapter 31

The Cost

Old Bess lost her memory on a Thursday.

Not all of it. The loss was selective. Surgical. The loss was the specific, targeted destruction of the memories that made Bess who she was — the memories of her apprenticeship, her magic, her two lost fingers, her survival. The memories that were the foundation of the woman who ran the Broken Broom and poured drinks and said the things no one else would say and looked at Wren with the eyes of a person who had been where he was and had survived.

The memories were replaced. Not erased — replaced. Filled with other people's memories, other people's pain, other people's forgotten moments that had been stored in the House's furnace and released when the furnace malfunctioned and the releasing was not controlled but catastrophic, a flood of forgotten feeling that poured through the House's systems and into the nearest mind, and the nearest mind was Bess, who had been in the Broken Broom, which was close to the House, which was close to the furnace, which was the thing that had broken.

The furnace had broken because of the siege. The siege had strained the wards, and the wards were connected to the furnace, and the furnace was connected to the House, and the House was connected to everything, and the everything was the system, and the system had been pushed too hard for too long by a war that was not supposed to happen and a keeper who was not supposed to be carrying this much and a sorcerer who was not supposed to be sleeping through it.

The malfunction was brief — thirty seconds, maybe less. Thirty seconds of the furnace venting its stored memories into the surrounding area, the way a pressure cooker vents steam when the pressure exceeds the capacity. The venting was invisible. The venting was silent. The venting was the most violent thing that had happened in Thornfield since the siege, and no one saw it and no one heard it and no one knew it had happened until Bess walked into the Broken Broom the next morning and didn't recognize the bar.

* * *

Wren found her sitting at a table. Not her usual table by the fire. A table in the corner, facing the wall.

She was crying.

The crying was — Wren stopped in the doorway. The stopping was involuntary, the way stopping is involuntary when you see something that your mind cannot immediately process, something that contradicts the model you have built of the world. Bess did not cry. Bess was the woman who had survived magic and lost two fingers and earned the right to say the things no one else would say. Bess was the rock. Bess was the foundation. Bess was the thing that did not break.

Bess was broken.

"Bess."

She looked up. The looking was — wrong. The eyes were wrong. But the thing behind the eyes was wrong. The thing behind the eyes was not Bess. The thing behind the eyes was a crowd — a crowd of memories, a crowd of feelings, a crowd of other people's pain that had been poured into Bess's mind and was now occupying the space where Bess used to be.

"I don't know you," she said. The words were — Wren heard them and the hearing was the thing that broke something in him, something that the systems and the strategies and the architecture had not protected, because the systems were designed to protect against external threats and this was not external. This was internal. This was the loss of the one person who understood him, the one person who had been where he was, the one person who could look at him and see the cost and say the words that no one else would say.

"I don't know you," she said again. "But I know — I know things. I know a woman who lost a child in a fire. I know a man who watched his brother drown. I know a girl who was left at a crossroads and waited for three days and no one came. I know — I know so many things. So many people's things. They're all in here." She touched her head. The touching was gentle — the gentleness of a person who was handling something fragile, something that might break further if handled roughly. "They're all in here and they're not mine and I can't — I can't find me. I can't find the me that was here before. The me is — somewhere. Under all the others. Under all the pain."

Wren sat across from her. The sitting was — he didn't know what to do. The not-knowing was unfamiliar. Wren always knew what to do. Wren was the man who had a plan for everything, a strategy for every contingency, a response for every crisis. But this was not a crisis that could be managed with letters and stamps and the cold precision of a mind that saw systems. This was a person. A person who was drowning in other people's memories. A person who needed — not a strategy. Not a plan. A person.

"Bess," he said. "Your name is Bess. You run the Broken Broom. You lost two fingers to magic — you won't say how. You survived an apprenticeship that killed three others. You pour drinks and you say the things no one else will say and you look at people and you see them. Really see them. You see the cost. You see the weight. You see the thing that the carrying does to a person, because you carried it yourself, and the carrying is the thing that made you wise, and the wisdom is the thing that makes you — you."

She listened. The listening was — he could see it — the listening was a lifeline. The words were a rope thrown into the flood, and Bess was grabbing for it, reaching through the crowd of other people's memories, reaching for the thing that was hers, the thing that was Bess, the thing that was underneath.

"Two fingers," she said. She looked at her hand. The hand with the missing fingers. The looking was — recognition. Not full recognition. A flicker. The flicker of a woman who was seeing something familiar in the flood of unfamiliar, something that belonged to her, something that was an anchor in the storm.

"Two fingers," she said again. "I lost them. I lost them because — because—" The because didn't come. The because was buried. The because was under the flood.

"You lost them because magic takes," Wren said. "That's the first lesson and the last. You told me that. You told me that on my first visit to the Broken Broom, when I was seventeen and scared and pretending not to be, and you looked at me and you saw through the pretending and you said: 'Magic takes. That's the first lesson and the last.' And you held up your hand and you showed me the missing fingers and the showing was the proof and the proof was the warning and the warning was the kindest thing anyone had ever done for me."

The tears continued. But the crying was different now — not the crying of a person who was lost, but the crying of a person who was being found. The crying of a person who was hearing their own story told back to them and was recognizing, in the telling, the shape of themselves.

"The Broken Broom," she said. "I run the Broken Broom. I pour drinks. I — I say things. Things that no one else says. I say them because — because someone has to. Because the not-saying is the thing that kills people. The not-saying is the silence, and the silence is the thing that — that—"

She stopped. The stopping was the flood — the other memories pushing back, the crowd reasserting itself, the pain of a hundred strangers pressing against the walls of Bess's mind and demanding space.

"I can't hold it," she said. "I can't hold all of it. There's too much. Too many people. Too much pain. It's — it's like being everyone at once. Like feeling everything at once. Like — like being the furnace. Like being the thing that holds all the forgotten things, and the holding is — the holding is too much."

Wren understood. The understanding was immediate — not the strategic understanding of a man who processed information, but the human understanding of a man who knew what it was like to hold too much. Who knew what it was like to carry the weight of a kingdom and feel the carrying pressing him into a shape he didn't recognize. Who knew what it was like to be consumed by the thing you were holding.

"You don't have to hold it," he said. "The memories — the other people's memories — they're not yours. They were stored in the furnace and the furnace broke and the breaking put them in you, but they're not yours. They're — they're like letters. Letters that were delivered to the wrong address. You don't have to read them. You don't have to carry them. You can put them down."

"How?"

The question was — he didn't know. He didn't know how to remove memories that had been magically implanted. He didn't know the spell, the technique, the procedure. He was not a healer. He was not a memory specialist. He was a man who answered letters and managed kingdoms and ordered assassinations and did not, in any of his extensive and morally questionable skill set, have the ability to fix a broken mind.

But he knew someone who did.

"Merlin," he said. The word tasted like ash. It was the name of the man he hated, the man who slept while people died, the man who could solve everything and chose to solve nothing. It was the name of the most powerful sorcerer in the world, and the power was the thing that could fix Bess, and the fixing was the thing that required Wren to ask, and the asking was the thing that the hatred made almost impossible.

Almost. Not completely. Because Bess was drowning. And drowning people were more important than hatred. And the importance was the thing that made Wren stand up and walk out of the Broken Broom and up the hill to the House and through the front door and down the corridor to Merlin's chambers and through the door that the House opened without being asked, because the House understood urgency even when its keeper was too proud to express it.

Merlin was in his chair. Merlin was always in his chair. The chair and Merlin had achieved a symbiosis that transcended furniture and occupant and had become something closer to a philosophical union — the chair supporting Merlin, Merlin justifying the chair, both of them committed to the principle that the horizontal position was the natural state of being and the vertical was an aberration to be tolerated only when absolutely necessary.

"Bess is hurt," Wren said. "The furnace malfunctioned. Stored memories flooded into her mind. She's drowning in other people's pain. She can't find herself. She needs help. Your help. The kind of help that requires you to get out of that chair and walk to the village and use the power that you have spent six years refusing to use for anything except sleeping and drinking and gambling with demons."

Merlin opened one eye. The grey eye. The eye that saw through things.

"The furnace," Merlin said. "Yes. I felt it. The venting. Thirty seconds. Unfortunate."

"Unfortunate. Your favorite word."

"It's an accurate word. The situation is unfortunate. The malfunction is unfortunate. The damage to Bess is unfortunate. The word describes the situation precisely. The precision is the point."

"Can you fix her?"

Both eyes opened. Both eyes looked at Wren. The look was — not indifferent. The look of a man who was calculating — the warm calculation of a being who had lived for a thousand years and understood, with the deep, structural wisdom of extreme age, that every action had consequences and every consequence had consequences and the chain of consequences was the thing that had to be considered before the action was taken.

"I can," Merlin said. "The memories can be extracted. Returned to the furnace. Bess can be restored. The restoration is — not simple, but possible."

"Then do it."

"I will." Merlin stood. The standing was — Wren watched, and the watching was the thing that surprised him — the standing was immediate. The immediate standing of a man who had heard something that mattered and was responding to the mattering with the speed and purpose of a being who was, underneath the laziness and the mead and the sleeping, capable of caring.

Merlin walked to the door. Stopped. Turned.

"You're paying too much," he said. "The furnace malfunctioned because the wards are strained. The wards are strained because the war is straining them. The war is straining them because you are fighting it alone. You are fighting it alone because I am not helping. I am not helping because — the reasons are complicated and old and I have explained them and you have not accepted the explanation and the not-accepting is — fair. The not-accepting is fair."

He paused. The pausing was — Wren watched — the pausing was the thing that happened when a thousand-year-old being was trying to say something that mattered and was discovering that the saying was harder than the knowing, because knowing was passive and saying was active and active was the thing Merlin avoided.

"The price is your face," Merlin said. "Look in a mirror. Please. Before the price becomes permanent."

He left. He walked to the village. He fixed Bess.

The fixing took three hours. Wren did not watch. Wren went back to the study and sat at the desk and picked up the pen and answered letters, because the letters were the routine and the routine was the prison and the prison was the thing he knew how to do.

But Merlin's words were in his mind. Look in a mirror. Please. Before the price becomes permanent.

He did not look. Not yet. The not-looking was the avoidance. The avoidance was the price.

But the price was accumulating. And the accumulation was the thing that could not continue. And the not-continuing was the thing that was coming.

The reckoning was waiting.

Bess came back. Not all at once — gradually, over days, the flood receding, the crowd thinning, the Bess underneath emerging like land after a flood, changed but recognizable, damaged but present. She came back to the Broken Broom. She poured drinks. She said the things no one else would say.

She looked at Wren and said: "You're paying too much. The price is your face. Look in a mirror."

The same words. Merlin's words. Bess's words. The words that everyone was saying and Wren was not hearing, because hearing required the systems to process the input, and the systems had classified the input as non-actionable, and non-actionable inputs were filed and forgotten.

But the words were not forgotten. The words were in the crack. The crack was growing.

And the mirror was waiting.
