Chapter 45

The Fence

The fence was, objectively, a disaster.

It ran along the eastern boundary of the farm — a hundred yards of wooden posts and crossbeams that separated Hector's vegetable garden from the Widow Marsh's goat pasture. The fence had been built by Hector's father, repaired by Hector, re-repaired by Hector, and re-re-repaired by Hector until the fence was less a structure and more a philosophical argument about persistence — ninety percent repair and ten percent original, held together by nails and stubbornness and the specific, agricultural optimism of a man who believed that anything could be fixed if you hit it with a hammer enough times.

"Here," Hector said, pointing at a post that was leaning at an angle that suggested it had given up on verticality as a lifestyle choice. "This one first."

He handed Wren a hammer. The hammer was heavy — physically heavy, the weight of iron and wood, a tool designed for one purpose. The hammer did not have a second purpose. The hammer was not also a weapon. The hammer was not also a metaphor. The hammer was a hammer.

Wren held it. The holding was different from holding a pen. The pen was light, precise, an instrument of abstraction — the tool that turned thought into ink and ink into orders and orders into the movement of armies and the fate of kingdoms. The hammer was the opposite. The hammer was blunt, direct, an instrument of contact — the tool that turned force into a nail in a post.

"Not like that," Hector said. "You're choking it. Hold it lower. Let the weight do the work. You're guiding, not forcing. The hammer knows what it's doing. You just point it."

Wren adjusted his grip. Swung. The hammer hit the nail. The nail went sideways.

"Again. Straighter. Watch the nail, not the hammer. The nail is where you're going. The hammer is how you get there. Don't watch how you get there. Watch where you're going."

He swung again. The nail went in — not straight, not clean, but in. The post shifted. A fraction of a degree toward vertical.

"Again."

He swung. The rhythm began to establish itself — not immediately, not smoothly, but in the halting, uncertain way that rhythms establish themselves when the body is learning something new. Swing. Hit. Swing. Hit. The sound of iron on iron, transmitted through wood, transmitted through the post into the ground. The sound was — satisfying. The satisfaction was immediate, physical, the satisfaction of a body doing a thing and the thing being done and the done-ness being visible.

Visible. The visibility was the revelation. After six years of invisible work — the letters no one saw him write, the crises no one saw him avert, the kingdom no one saw him carry — the visibility of a nail in a post was staggering. He could point at the nail. He could say: I did that. That nail. That post. That fraction of a degree toward vertical. Mine.

"Good," Hector said. "Now the next one."

They worked along the fence. Post by post. Nail by nail. Hector talked while they worked — not about fences, or not only about fences. About soil. About weather. About the Widow Marsh's goats, which were, in Hector's professional opinion, the most anarchic livestock in the county.

"The brown one — Mabel, she's called, though she doesn't answer to it — Mabel can open a gate. Not push through it. OPEN it. With her mouth. She works the latch. I've watched her. She studies the mechanism. She experiments. She's smarter than half the people in the village, and I include myself in that assessment."

"You're saying the goat is an engineer."

"I'm saying the goat is a problem-solver. The problem is: there is a fence between me and the carrots. The solution is: remove the fence. The method is: learn how latches work. The goat doesn't care about the fence's feelings. The goat doesn't negotiate with the fence. The goat identifies the weakness and exploits it. The goat is — what's the word — pragmatic."

"The goat sounds like someone I used to work with."

"Everyone works with a goat. The trick is knowing which side of the fence you're on."

Wren drove a nail. The nail went in straight this time — the first straight nail of the morning. The straightness was disproportionately satisfying. The satisfaction was the satisfaction of a skill being learned, a competence being built, the slow accumulation of ability that comes from repetition and attention and the willingness to hit a nail badly seventeen times before hitting it well once.

The calluses were forming. He could feel them — the new calluses, building over the old ones, the fence-calluses layering on top of the pen-calluses. The layering was the record. The body's record of what the hands were doing. The record was changing.

* * *

At noon, Elena brought lunch to the fence.

A basket. Bread, cheese, apples, water. She set it on the wall and sat beside it and looked at the fence — at the section they had mended, the posts straightened, the beams replaced, the nails driven in (some straight, most not, all functional).

"You've been busy," she said.

"Hector's been busy. I've been hitting things with a hammer and hoping for the best."

"That's what mending is. Hitting things with a hammer and hoping for the best. The hoping is the important part."

He sat on the wall. Ate bread. The bread was Elena's — he could tell by the texture, the taste, the specific quality that her bread had and other bread didn't, the quality that was not an ingredient but a presence. He ate the bread and he tasted it and the tasting was complete — no drift, no zone-out, no slide back to the study. His mind stayed with the bread. His mind stayed with the wall and the sun and the woman beside him and the fence that was less broken than it had been that morning.

"I mended a fence," he said.

"You mended a fence."

"I can see it. The mending. It's there. In the wood. In the nails. I can point at it."

She looked at him. The looking was — she saw something in his face. He didn't know what his face was doing. His face had been doing strategic things for so long that he had lost track of what it did when the strategy stopped. But whatever his face was doing, Elena saw it, and what she saw made her eyes bright in a way that was not tears and was not laughter but the thing between them.

"Yes," she said. "You can point at it."

They ate lunch on the wall. Hector joined them, bringing a carrot from the garden — a carrot that had survived the crows, which Hector presented with the pride of a man who had won a battle against a superior force. Tam appeared from somewhere — Tam always appeared from somewhere, materializing at the mention of food with the unerring instinct of a fifteen-year-old whose stomach operated on a separate and more efficient intelligence network than his brain.

"How's the fence?" Tam asked.

"The fence is improving," Hector said.

"Is it improving enough to stop Mabel?"

"Nothing stops Mabel. Mabel is a force of nature. The fence is not for Mabel. The fence is for the other goats. The other goats respect a fence. Mabel respects nothing."

"Mabel is my hero," Tam said. "Mabel is living her truth. Mabel has looked at the social contract between goats and farmers and has decided that the social contract does not apply to her. Mabel is an anarchist. Mabel is—"

"Tam," Elena said. "Eat your apple."

Tam ate his apple. The eating was — Wren watched the family eat lunch on a wall in the sun, and the watching was the thing, and the thing was ordinary, and the ordinary was the gift that kept giving because the ordinary was inexhaustible, the ordinary renewed itself every day, the ordinary was breakfast and lunch and dinner and the arguments about scarecrows and the jokes about goats and the bread and the tea and the sun on a wall at noon.

He picked up the hammer. Went back to the fence. The fence waited — patient, broken, ready to be mended.

He swung. The nail went in. Straight.

* * *

By the end of the first week, he could drive a nail in three swings.

By the end of the second week, two.

By the end of the first month, one. One swing. One nail. The economy of motion that comes from repetition, from the body learning what the mind cannot teach, from the specific, physical wisdom of hands that have done a thing a thousand times and have stopped thinking about the doing and have started simply doing.

The fence improved. Section by section. Post by post. The improvement was visible — the posts vertical, the beams level, the nails straight. The improvement was his. He had done it. With a hammer and nails and the patient instruction of a man who argued with scarecrows and the steady, daily, unglamorous labor of a body that was learning a new language.

The calluses hardened. The pen-calluses faded. The hands were becoming different hands — not the hands of a man who managed but the hands of a man who mended. The difference was in the calluses and in the grip and in the specific, physical confidence of hands that knew what they were for.

The fence was not finished. The fence would never be finished. The fence would break and be mended and break again, because fences broke, because weather and goats and time were forces that no fence could permanently resist, and the resistance was not the point, and the permanence was not the goal.

The mending was the point. The mending was the practice. The practice was the life.

He mended. Day after day. Nail after nail. And the mending was enough.
