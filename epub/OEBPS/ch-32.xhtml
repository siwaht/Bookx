Chapter 33

The Grey Men

They arrived in groups of three and four, walking south with the mechanical precision of men who had been trained to march and had forgotten how to stop.

The village called them the grey men. The village called them grey because of the thing behind their eyes — the space where feelings used to be and where now there was only the memory of feelings, which was worse than nothing because nothing was clean and memory was not.

Wren watched them arrive from the study window. The watching was — he recognized them. He recognized the condition. The condition was Marcus's condition — the clinical emptiness, the precise awareness, the ability to observe the world without participating in it. The condition was the thing that Nimue had created and that Wren had exploited and that was now, in the form of forty-seven former soldiers walking south toward Thornhold, his responsibility.

Forty-seven. The number was — he filed it. Forty-seven men who had been emptied by a process they had volunteered for, who had served in an army that had been destroyed by a sabotage campaign Wren had orchestrated, who had been discarded by a kingdom that no longer existed, and who were now walking toward the only place that would take them, because Wren had written the words bring them in and the words had become policy and the policy had become forty-seven men standing in the village square, looking at the Broken Broom and the forge and the market stalls with the specific, unsettling blankness of people who were seeing everything and feeling nothing.

Aldric was not happy. Aldric expressed his unhappiness in the way that soldiers express unhappiness — with precision, with specificity, with the detailed enumeration of risks and costs and the professional assessment that the decision was, from a security standpoint, inadvisable.

"They're former enemy soldiers," Aldric said. He was standing in the study, in the visitor's chair, with the posture of a man who was delivering a report he knew would be ignored and was delivering it anyway because the delivering was the duty and the duty was the thing that Aldric understood. "Eunuch soldiers. Trained killers. Men who cannot feel fear or loyalty or gratitude. Men who will follow orders — any orders, from anyone — because following orders is the only thing they know how to do. Bringing them into the kingdom is—"

"Necessary."

"Dangerous."

"Both. The two are not mutually exclusive. Most of the things I do are both necessary and dangerous. The necessity is the justification. The danger is the cost. The cost is — manageable."

"Manageable." Aldric's voice was — Wren heard it — Aldric's voice was the voice of a man who was tired of the word "manageable," tired of the calculation, tired of the cold precision that turned people into variables and variables into decisions and decisions into the thing that kept the kingdom running and the man running it from sleeping.

"They need housing," Wren said. "The empty barracks on the south side — the ones we built for the garrison expansion that hasn't happened yet. They need food — add them to the kitchen's provisioning. They need work — not military work. Physical work. Building. Repairing. The kind of work that uses hands and produces results and doesn't require the ability to feel."

"And if they can't — if they can't adjust? If the emptiness is permanent? If they're—"

"Then we manage that too. The managing is the job. The job is the thing we do."

Aldric left. The leaving was — Wren watched him go — the leaving was the leaving of a man who disagreed and obeyed and hated the disagreeing and the obeying in equal measure, because the disagreeing was the honesty and the obeying was the loyalty and the honesty and the loyalty were pulling in different directions and the pulling was the thing that was wearing Aldric down, the way it was wearing everyone down, the way the war wore everything down.

* * *

The rehabilitation — Wren refused to call it a "program," because "program" implied structure and certainty and the confident belief that the thing being attempted would work, and Wren had none of those things — the rehabilitation began with housing.

The barracks were adequate. Not comfortable — comfort required the ability to feel comfort, and the grey men could not feel comfort. But adequate. Clean beds. Warm rooms. Regular meals. The basics of human existence, provided to men who were human in body and absent in everything else.

The work began the next day. Wren assigned them to the village — not to the garrison, not to the House, but to the village, because the village was the place where ordinary life happened, and ordinary life was the thing that the grey men needed, if they needed anything, if the needing was possible for men who could not feel need.

They repaired fences. They built walls. They dug ditches. They did the physical, unglamorous, essential work of maintaining a village, and the doing was — Wren watched, from a distance, through Threadneedle's reports and Aldric's assessments and the occasional, direct observation from the study window — the doing was mechanical. The grey men worked the way machines work — efficiently, precisely, without complaint, without satisfaction, without the human friction that made work both difficult and meaningful.

Some of them responded. Slowly. Incrementally. The way a frozen thing thaws — not all at once but in stages, the outer layers softening first, the core remaining cold, the softening spreading inward at a pace that was measured not in hours but in weeks.

A man named Garrett was the first. Garrett had been a eunuch soldier for eight years — eight years of emptiness, eight years of the clinical precision that was the eunuch condition. Garrett was assigned to the village bakery — not as a baker, but as a laborer, carrying flour and stoking ovens and doing the heavy work that the baker, a woman named Maren, could not do alone.

Garrett carried flour. Garrett stoked ovens. Garrett did the work with the mechanical efficiency of a man who could not feel the work but could perform it. And then, on the fourteenth day, Garrett did something that was not mechanical.

Garrett smelled the bread.

Not smelled it the way a machine detects an input — the clinical registration of a chemical stimulus. Smelled it the way a person smells bread — with the involuntary, full-body response of a being whose senses are connected to memories and whose memories are connected to feelings and whose feelings are the thing that makes the smelling meaningful.

Garrett smelled the bread and stopped. The stopping was — Maren reported it to Threadneedle, who reported it to Wren — the stopping was the first non-mechanical thing Garrett had done in eight years. The stopping was the pause of a man who had encountered something that his emptiness could not process, something that reached through the clinical precision and touched the thing underneath, the thing that the eunuch process had buried but not destroyed.

The thing was memory. The memory was bread. The bread was — Garrett could not say. Garrett could not feel the memory. But Garrett could smell the bread, and the smelling was the bridge, and the bridge was the first connection between the emptiness and the thing that had been there before the emptiness, and the connection was —

Not feeling. Not yet. But the possibility of feeling. The ghost of feeling. The scent of feeling, carried on the smell of bread, reaching through eight years of emptiness and touching something that was still alive.

Some of them responded. Some did not.

The ones who did not respond were the ones who haunted Wren. The ones who worked and ate and slept and did everything that living required without any of the things that made living worth doing. The ones who looked at warmth the way a blind man looks at color — with perfect understanding and absolute absence. The ones who were Marcus. The ones who would always be Marcus. The ones for whom the emptiness was not a condition but a permanent state, and the permanence was the thing that could not be fixed, not by bread or work or housing or the well-intentioned efforts of a kingdom that was trying to undo the damage that another kingdom had done.

Wren watched them. The watching was — the watching was the recognition. The recognition that he had feared since the Broken Broom, since the conversation with Bess and Aldric, since the words I am becoming Marcus. The recognition that the grey men were mirrors — the kind that showed you what you were becoming, and the becoming was the thing that could not be unseen.

The grey men were what happened when the emptiness won. When the systems consumed the person. When the efficiency replaced the humanity. When the pilot light went out and the going-out was permanent and the permanence was the condition and the condition was the life.

Wren was not there yet. The warmth still burned. The crack still grew. The human still existed, underneath the systems, in the space between the strategies.

But the grey men were the warning. The grey men were the future that awaited him if he did not find the door. The grey men were the mirror.

And the mirror was waiting.

He went back to the study. Answered the next letter. Filed the reports. Managed the rehabilitation that was not a program.

And in the village, forty-seven grey men worked and ate and slept and did not feel, and the not-feeling was the thing that Wren carried, along with everything else, along with the kingdom and the war and the weight.

And the weight was heavier. And the carrying was harder. And the door was closer.

And the warmth burned. Low. Steady. Enough.

For now.
