Chapter 24

The Siege

They came at night.

Not the strategic night of a planned assault — the desperate night of an army running out of time and supplies and generals. The option that Wren, reading the intelligence reports from Marcus’s compromised network, had been expecting for two weeks and dreading for longer.

Nimue's army — what was left of it — hit Thornhold's outer walls at midnight.

The outer walls were not the House. The outer walls were the kingdom's perimeter — a ring of fortifications that Aldric had spent two years building and Wren had spent two years funding, stone walls and watchtowers and the mundane, unglamorous infrastructure of a kingdom that had learned, through painful experience, that magical wards were not a substitute for physical defenses.

The eunuch soldiers came in ragged waves — an army that had lost half its generals and most of its supply chain and was fighting not because it believed it could win but because it had forgotten how to do anything else. The eunuch soldiers did not feel fear. The eunuch soldiers did not feel hope. The eunuch soldiers felt nothing, and the nothing was the thing that made them terrifying, because an army that feels nothing fights without hesitation and retreats without shame and dies without complaint, and the dying-without-complaint was the thing that haunted the defenders, because killing a man who doesn't scream is worse than killing a man who does.

Aldric commanded the defense. Aldric was — Wren watched from the study window, the window that overlooked the eastern wall — Aldric was magnificent. Not the magnificence of a hero in a ballad. The magnificence of a professional. The magnificence of a man who had trained for this, who had prepared for this, who had spent two years building walls and drilling soldiers and planning contingencies for exactly this scenario, and who was now executing those plans with the calm, steady, unglamorous competence of a man who understood that war was not a story but a job, and the job was keeping people alive.

The garrison held. The walls held. The wards held — Wren's wards, the magical defenses he had maintained and strengthened over six years, the invisible barrier that supplemented the physical walls with a force that eunuch soldiers could not feel but could not pass. The wards hummed — Wren could feel them humming, the vibration in his bones, the resonance of magic that was connected to him the way a limb is connected to a body, and the connection was the cost, because maintaining the wards during an assault was not passive. It was active. It was the magical equivalent of holding a door shut while someone pushed from the other side, and the pushing was fifteen hundred emotionless soldiers, and the holding was one man with warm hands and four hours of sleep.

He held. The wards held. The walls held. Aldric held.

The first wave broke against the walls like water against stone — with the slow, grinding, inevitable erosion of a force that could not feel defeat pressing against a defense that could not afford to fail. The eunuch soldiers fell. They fell without screaming. They fell without crying. They fell with the clinical, mechanical precision of machines that had been turned off, and the turning-off was death, and death was the thing that the defenders dealt and the eunuch soldiers received and neither side felt anything about it except the defenders, who felt everything, and the everything was the thing that would haunt them.

The second wave came an hour later. Larger. More desperate. The eunuch soldiers had scaling ladders now — crude, improvised, the ladders of an army that had lost its siege engineers and was making do with whatever wood it could find. The ladders hit the walls. The soldiers climbed. Aldric's garrison pushed them back — arrows, stones, the brutal, close-quarters violence of men defending their home against men who had no home and no feeling and no reason to stop except the stopping of their hearts.

Wren coordinated from the study. The coordination was — he was good at this. He hated that he was good at this. The coordination was the thing the crack had not touched, the thing that worked even when everything else was breaking. He sent messages to Aldric — short, precise, the red-pen messages that meant orders. Reinforce the eastern gate. Pull the reserve company to the north wall. The third wave will come from the north — the terrain is weaker there, and Nimue's remaining generals are not stupid enough to keep hitting the same wall.

The third wave came from the north. Wren had been right. The third wave was the largest — the last of Nimue's reserves, the final push of an army that was spending its last strength on a gamble that the defenders would break before the attackers did.

The defenders did not break. The defenders held, because Aldric held, because the walls held, because the wards held, because Wren held, because the pilot light burned and the fuel was everything and the everything was enough, barely, just barely, the way "enough" always was in Thornhold — not comfortable, not safe, just enough.

The third wave broke. The eunuch soldiers retreated — not in disorder, not in panic, because disorder and panic were emotions and emotions were the things they didn't have. They retreated with the mechanical precision of a machine that had been given a new instruction: stop. The stopping was not defeat. The stopping was calculation — the calculation of an army that had spent its strength and needed to regroup and would come again, because coming again was the only thing it knew how to do.

The siege was not over. The siege was pausing. The pause was not peace. The pause was the space between waves, the space where the defenders counted their dead and the attackers counted their remaining strength and both sides calculated whether the next wave would be the last.

Wren counted the dead. Seven defenders. Seven names he would learn and file and carry. Seven names added to the fourteen from Ashenmere, added to the names from the border skirmishes, added to the names from the assassinations he had ordered, added to the weight that was pressing him into the shape of someone he no longer recognized.

Twenty-one names. The mathematics of war: each name a weight, each weight a piece of the thing that was consuming him.

* * *

Merlin woke up.

Not fully — Merlin never woke up fully. But enough of him woke up to walk to the window of his chambers and look out at the eastern wall, where the fires were still burning and the soldiers were still standing and the eunuch army was still visible in the distance, a dark mass on the plain, regrouping for the next wave.

Wren found him there. Standing at the window. Both eyes open — the grey and the blue, the seeing and the feeling. Both eyes looking at the war.

"Hm," Merlin said.

The sound was — Wren waited. The waiting was the thing he did with Merlin — the patient, furious, exhausting waiting for the most powerful being in the world to decide whether the thing happening outside his window was worth a complete sentence.

"Hm," Merlin said again. Then: "They're persistent."

"They're dying."

"Yes. That too. Dying and persistent. The two are not mutually exclusive. In my experience, the dying are often the most persistent. Something about the finality — it focuses the mind. Or in their case, the absence of mind. Eunuch soldiers. Nimue's specialty. Terrible idea. Brilliant execution. The worst ideas are always the ones that work."

"Seven of our soldiers are dead."

"Seven." Merlin's voice was — not indifferent. Not this time. This time, the voice was something else. Something that sounded like — Wren searched for the word and found it in the vocabulary he was losing — something that sounded like tired. Not the tiredness of a man who needed sleep. The tiredness of a man who had been alive for a thousand years and had heard the number of the dead so many times that the number had become a weight, and the weight was the thing that kept him in his chair, in his mead, in his sleep.

"I could stop it," Merlin said. Quietly. To the window. To the war. To the dead. "You know I could. You've told me. Many times. With increasing frustration and decreasing respect. I could walk out there and raise my hand and the army would stop. Not die — stop. Because I am Merlin. Because the name is the thing. Because the fear is the wall."

"Then—"

"And then what?" Both eyes turned to Wren. Both eyes seeing. Both eyes — for the first time in months, for the first time since the mead cellar, for the first time since Wren had understood the drowning — both eyes present. "Then the next army comes prepared for me. Then the next witch builds weapons designed to counter me. Then the escalation begins — the escalation that I have seen a hundred times, the escalation that starts with a hand raised in defense and ends with a world unmade in offense. I have been the escalation, boy. I have been the hand that unmade things. The unmaking is not the thing you think it is. The unmaking is not power. The unmaking is the end of power, because once you unmake something, the only thing left is to unmake more, and the more is infinite, and the infinite is the thing that consumed me, and the consumption is the reason I sleep, and the sleeping is not laziness. The sleeping is the only responsible thing I do."

The speech was — Wren filed it. Filed the words, the tone, the look in both eyes. Filed the logic, which was — again, he hated to admit it — not wrong. The logic of escalation. The logic of a being who had been the weapon and had learned that being the weapon was the thing that created the need for weapons.

The logic was sound. The logic was also the excuse of a man who was watching seven of his people die and choosing philosophy over action.

"Go back to sleep," Wren said. The words were cold. The coldness was the hatred — the load-bearing wall, the thing that kept the architecture standing. The hatred that had cracked in the mead cellar but had not fallen, because load-bearing walls don't fall from a single crack. They fall from accumulation. And the accumulation was not yet enough.

Merlin looked at him. Both eyes. The pity was there again — the pity that Wren hated more than the indifference, because the pity meant Merlin saw him, really saw him, saw the cost and the weight and the stranger in the mirror, and the seeing was not accompanied by action, and the inaction was the thing that made the pity obscene.

"You're paying too much," Merlin said. "The price is your face. I can see it. The grey. The lines. The hardness. You're twenty-three and you look—"

"I know what I look like."

"Do you? Have you looked? Recently? In a mirror?"

"Go back to sleep, Merlin."

Merlin went back to sleep. The going was slow — the shuffle of a man who was a thousand years old and carried the weight of every one of those years in his bones and his eyes and his mead-soaked memories. The shuffle was the sound of a being who had chosen sleep over action and was paying for the choice with the specific, intimate guilt of a man who knew he was wrong and was wrong anyway, because being wrong was easier than being the escalation.

Wren went back to the study. The wards needed reinforcing. The garrison needed coordinating. The dead needed counting.

The siege continued. The waves came. The walls held. The wards held. The warmth burned.

And Merlin slept. And the sleeping was the thing that Wren would never forgive. Not the indifference or the laziness. The sleeping. The deliberate, chosen, philosophical sleeping of a man who could have saved seven lives with a gesture and had chosen, instead, to sleep.

The hatred reached a new depth. The depth was the place where hatred stops burning and starts freezing. The place where the cold becomes permanent. The place where the ice never thaws.

Wren picked up the pen. Answered the next letter. Filed the seven names.

The siege would end. The war would continue. The weight would grow.

And the ice would hold.
