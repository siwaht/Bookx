Chapter 9

The Village Below

Bess had buried three apprentices.

Not literally — she hadn't dug the graves. But she'd watched them go in. Watched the bright-eyed young things walk up Briar Lane with their bags and their hopes and their latent magic crackling like static in their fingertips. Watched them come down to the village weeks or months later, changed. Dimmer. The brightness worn off like gilt on a cheap ring, revealing the base metal underneath.

The first one — Agnes, her name was, red-haired, fierce, laughed like a thunderclap — lasted two years. Came down to the Broken Broom every Sunday for a pint and a complaint. The complaints started specific: Merlin forgot to eat, Merlin gambled with something he shouldn't have, Merlin set the study on fire again. The complaints became general: the House is too much, the work is too much, I am too much and not enough at the same time. Agnes left on a Tuesday. Didn't say goodbye. Bess found a note under her usual pint glass: Gone. Don't look for me. The House will find someone else. The House always finds someone else.

The second one — a boy, Thomas, not Merlin's son, a different Thomas, quiet, careful, the kind of person who alphabetized his emotions — lasted eight months. Didn't come to the pub. Didn't come to the village at all. Bess saw him once, through the window, walking the ward line at dawn, his face the color of old paper. He left without a note. The House creaked for a week after — the grieving creak, the creak of a building that has lost someone and is trying to hold the shape of their absence.

The third one — Petra, sharp as a blade, funny as a funeral — lasted four months. She came to the Broken Broom on her last night. Sat at the bar. Ordered whiskey, not ale. Whiskey was the drink of people who had decided something. She drank three glasses and said: "He broke something I can't fix. Not magic. Not the House. Something in the — in the way I think about whether things are worth doing. He broke the worth. I can't find it anymore."

Bess had poured her a fourth glass and said nothing, because nothing was the right thing to say to a person whose worth had been broken, and saying nothing was the only skill Bess had that was worth a damn, and the worth of saying nothing was the one thing Merlin's house hadn't taken from her.

Petra left the next morning. The House didn't creak. The House had learned, by then, not to grieve too loudly. Loud grief frightened the new ones.

Now there was Wren.

Bess watched him the way she watched weather — not with the intention of changing it but with the knowledge that it was coming and the only useful response was preparation. She watched him from behind the bar of the Broken Broom, polishing glasses that were already clean, because polishing was what her hands did when her mind was working, and her mind was always working, and the working was the thing that had kept her alive through three dead apprentices and two missing fingers and a lifetime of living in the shadow of a wizard who treated the world like a toy he'd gotten bored of.

Wren came to the village on market days. Not for pleasure — for business. He walked through the market with the focused efficiency of a man who was buying supplies and gathering intelligence simultaneously, because in Wren's world, every interaction was both a transaction and an assessment, and the assessment was the part that mattered.

He bought saffron from the spice merchant — not because the kitchen needed saffron but because the spice merchant traded with Auremere, and the spice merchant's gossip about Morgause's court was more valuable than the saffron. He bought iron nails from the blacksmith — not because the garrison needed nails but because the blacksmith's brother served in Nimue's army, and the blacksmith's letters from his brother contained details about troop movements that the brother didn't know were significant and the blacksmith didn't know were being collected.

He bought nothing from Bess. He came to the Broken Broom, sat at the bar, ordered tea — always tea, never ale, never whiskey, the sobriety of a person who could not afford the luxury of impaired judgment — and talked.

The talking was not casual. Wren did not do casual. But the talking was — Bess searched for the word — honest. Honest in the way that a person is honest with someone who has seen the same fire and carries the same burns. Wren talked to Bess the way soldiers talk to other soldiers: in shorthand, in references, in the shared vocabulary of people who have been through something that civilians cannot understand and do not want to.

"Nimue moved her eastern regiments," he said one Tuesday, stirring tea he wasn't drinking. "Toward Morgause. Not toward us. The movement is either a response to the forged letters I planted or an independent strategic decision. If it's a response, my scheme is working. If it's independent, it means Nimue has her own reasons for threatening Morgause, and Nimue's own reasons are always worse than the reasons I plan for."

"Which do you think?"

"Both. The forged letters accelerated something that was already happening. Nimue and Morgause have been circling each other for decades. I just gave them a reason to stop circling and start biting."

Bess polished a glass. "And Viviane?"

"Viviane is doing nothing. Viviane is always doing nothing. The nothing is the thing I can't plan for, because you can't counter nothing. You can counter an army. You can counter a scheme. You can't counter a woman who has decided that the universe is not worth engaging with and has made the not-engaging into a weapon."

"You sound tired."

"I am tired."

"You sound more tired than last week."

"I am more tired than last week. Last week I was managing two hostile kingdoms and a sleeping sorcerer. This week I'm managing two hostile kingdoms that are about to become hostile to each other, a sleeping sorcerer who tried to gamble away the eastern wards, a debt collector who visits monthly, an intelligence network that requires constant feeding, a garrison that requires constant paying, and a furnace that requires constant stoking. The tiredness compounds. Like interest."

Bess set down the glass. Looked at him. The looking was the thing she did — the thing that made people uncomfortable, the thing that made them feel seen in a way they hadn't consented to. The looking of a woman who had survived magic and knew what it looked like from the inside: not the sparks and the spectacle but the slow, invisible erosion of the person underneath.

"You're not sleeping," she said.

"I sleep."

"You're not sleeping enough. I can see it. The skin under your eyes. The way your hands shake when you think no one's looking. You're running on the pilot light and the pilot light is not designed for this. A pilot light keeps a furnace ready. It doesn't heat the whole house."

"There's no one else to heat the house."

"There's Merlin."

The laugh that came out of Wren was not a laugh. It was the sound a laugh makes when it has been compressed into something harder and smaller and more bitter — the sound of a person who has heard a joke so many times that the humor has been crushed out of it and only the shape remains.

"Merlin," he said, "tried to bet the eastern wards on a card game last night. The eastern wards that I maintain. Every morning. At dawn. In the cold. While he sleeps."

Bess was quiet for a moment. The quiet was not empty — it was full, the way a held breath is full, containing everything that could be said and choosing not to say it.

"Agnes lasted two years," Bess said. "Petra lasted four months. Thomas lasted eight months. You've lasted — what? Six months?"

"Seven."

"Seven months. And you've done more in seven months than the last three combined. You've rebuilt the treasury, established an intelligence network, neutralized a military advance, and you're currently running a covert operation against three hostile kingdoms simultaneously. At eighteen."

"Seventeen when I started."

"Eighteen now. Happy birthday, by the way. No one remembered. I'm guessing you didn't either."

He hadn't. The realization crossed his face — brief, quickly suppressed, filed away in the architecture of a mind that had no room for birthdays. But Bess saw it. The flash of something that was not quite hurt and not quite surprise but the space between them, the space where a normal eighteen-year-old would have had a cake and a song and someone who remembered.

"The point," Bess said, "is that you're better at this than anyone who's come before. And the better you are, the more you'll do. And the more you do, the more you'll need to do. And the needing never stops. It never stops because the kingdom never stops needing, and Merlin never starts caring, and the gap between the needing and the caring is the space you live in, and the space is getting smaller."

"What are you telling me?"

"I'm telling you to look in a mirror. Not the House's mirrors — those show alternate selves. A real mirror. Look at your face. Count the lines. Check the color of your hair. Then ask yourself: is this what eighteen looks like?"

He didn't answer. He stirred his tea. The tea was cold. He stirred it anyway, because the stirring was something to do with his hands, and his hands needed something to do when they weren't writing letters or maintaining wards or carrying the weight of a kingdom that didn't know his name.

"I can't stop," he said. Quietly. The quiet was not performance. The quiet was the sound of a truth that had been living in his chest for months and had finally found its way to his mouth. "If I stop, the kingdom falls. If the kingdom falls, people die. Not abstractions — people. The soldiers in the garrison. The merchants on the trade routes. The farmers in the villages. Elena's family, on their farm, two days south. If I stop, they're unprotected. If I stop, the witches move. If I stop—"

"If you stop, someone else starts. That's how it works. That's how it's supposed to work. The kingdom is not your body. The kingdom is a system. Systems survive the loss of individual components. That's what makes them systems."

"Not this system. This system has one component. Me."

Bess looked at him. The look was — he would think about this later, in the study, in the dark — the look was grief. Not for him. For herself. For the version of herself that had sat in a different chair, in a different building, carrying a different weight, and had said the same words to a different person who had not listened, because the not-listening was the condition, and the condition was terminal.

"I lost two fingers," Bess said. She held up her left hand. The hand was missing the ring finger and the little finger — clean stumps, healed smooth, the scars old and white. "Not to magic. Not to a monster. To a door. The door of the wizard's house — my wizard's house, not Merlin's, a different wizard, a different house, a different version of the same story. The door closed on my hand because I was trying to leave and the house didn't want me to leave and the house expressed its opinion through architecture. I lost two fingers. I gained my life."

She set the hand on the bar. The stumps were matter-of-fact — not displayed for sympathy, not hidden from shame. Just there. The evidence of a cost paid and a lesson learned.

"The lesson," she said, "is that the house always takes something. The question is whether you choose what it takes or whether it chooses for you. I chose my fingers. Agnes chose her laughter — she doesn't laugh anymore, wherever she is. Petra chose her sense of worth. Thomas chose — I don't know what Thomas chose. Thomas doesn't write."

"What will I choose?"

"You won't choose. That's the problem. You won't choose because choosing requires stopping, and you won't stop, and the not-stopping means the house chooses for you. And the house — any house, Merlin's house, my wizard's house, any building that is alive and hungry and needs a keeper — the house always takes the thing you can least afford to lose."

"What's that?"

Bess picked up the glass. Resumed polishing. The polishing was the punctuation — the period at the end of a sentence that had been building for three dead apprentices and two missing fingers and a lifetime of watching bright-eyed young things walk up a lane and come back dimmer.

"Yourself," she said. "The house takes yourself. The version of you that existed before you walked through the door. The version that could taste food and sleep through the night and laugh at a joke without calculating whether the laughter served a strategic purpose. The house takes that version and replaces it with something harder and more useful and less human, and the replacement is so gradual that you don't notice until you look in a mirror and see a stranger."

She set down the glass. Clean. Gleaming. Empty.

"Look in a mirror, Wren. While you still recognize the face."

He left the Broken Broom. Walked back up Briar Lane. The House opened the gate — the rust-words said WELCOME BACK, which was what they always said, which was the House's way of saying I missed you, which was the House's way of saying I need you, which was the House's way of saying don't leave.

He went to the study. Sat at the desk. Picked up the next letter.

He did not look in a mirror.

Not yet.
