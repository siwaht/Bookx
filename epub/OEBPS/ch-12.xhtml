Chapter 13

The Eunuch's Report

The intelligence drop arrived in a hollowed-out candle.

This was Marcus's method — practical, invisible, the kind of tradecraft that a man without feelings would devise — espionage as an engineering problem. The candle was purchased from a chandler in the border town of Harren's Ford — the same chandler who supplied both Thornhold and Greymarch, the same chandler who did not know and did not care that his candles were carrying military secrets across a border that was technically at peace and practically at war.

Wren extracted the report in the study. The extraction was delicate — a thin knife, a steady hand, a rolled paper hidden in the wax. The paper was small. The handwriting was precise — Marcus's handwriting, which was not handwriting at all but a kind of architectural drawing, each letter constructed rather than written.

The report was three pages. Wren read it twice.

The first page was what he'd asked for: supply manifests. Nimue's northern army — the army that had attacked Thornhold's eastern border, the army that had been repelled by Aldric's garrison — was being resupplied. New weapons. New provisions. New eunuch soldiers, freshly converted, their emotions stripped and their bodies trained and their humanity filed away in whatever archive the Witch of Despair maintained for the things she took from people.

The supply lines converged at three points. Wren marked them on his map. The marking was routine — another data point, another piece of the puzzle, another input in the system that was his mind.

The second page was what he hadn't asked for.

Marcus had included a list. Not a supply list — a personnel list. The names of Nimue's generals, their positions, their strengths, their weaknesses. Beside each name, a notation in Marcus's architectural script:

General Harsk — commands the northern regiment. Competent. Loyal to Nimue personally, not ideologically. Weakness: his son serves in the eastern regiment. Leverage: the son.

General Voss — commands the eastern regiment. Brutal. Effective. Hated by his troops. Weakness: gambling debts. Leverage: the debts.

General Maren — commands the reserve. Cautious. Intelligent. The most dangerous of the three. Weakness: none identified. Recommendation: removal.

The word "removal" was underlined. Twice.

Wren stared at the word. The word was Marcus's word — clinical, precise, the word a man without feelings uses when he means "assassination" but cannot feel the weight of the meaning and therefore uses the lighter word, the administrative word, the word that files death under "personnel management."

Marcus was recommending the assassination of a general. Not because Wren had asked — because Marcus had calculated, independently, that the removal of General Maren would weaken Nimue's military capacity by approximately thirty percent, and the calculation was correct, and the correctness was the justification, and the justification was sufficient for a man who could not feel the difference between correct and right.

The third page was worse.

The third page was a report on Marcus's own activities — activities Wren had not authorized. Three supply depots sabotaged. Not the subtle, invisible sabotage Wren had requested — the kind that slowed campaigns without triggering investigations. This was overt. Obvious. The kind of sabotage that left evidence, that pointed to an inside job, that would trigger exactly the kind of investigation Wren had been trying to avoid.

And at the bottom of the third page, a single line:

The quartermaster of the northern depot has been eliminated. The elimination was necessary to prevent discovery. The method was efficient. No witnesses.

Marcus had killed someone. Not on Wren's orders. On his own initiative. The killing was — Marcus's word — "efficient." The efficiency was the horror — a man who could eliminate a human being with the same emotional investment he brought to eliminating a line item from a budget.

Wren set the report down. Looked at his hands. The hands were warm. The hands were steady. The hands had written three words — Silke. Corwin. Accident. — and the three words had resulted in a man's death, and the death had been filed and processed and stored. The hands were capable of ordering death. The hands were warm.

The warmth and the capability existed in the same hands. The coexistence was the condition. The condition was spreading.

He picked up a pen. Wrote a response to Marcus. The response was careful — precise, controlled, the language of a handler managing an asset that was becoming unstable.

Marcus,

Your intelligence on the supply lines is valuable. Continue monitoring.

The personnel assessments are noted. No action on General Maren at this time. Repeat: no action. The removal of a general-level target would trigger a response disproportionate to the tactical gain. We need Nimue's army weakened, not alerted.

The depot sabotage was excessive. The overt nature of the operations risks exposure. Scale back to the parameters we agreed on: subtle, invisible, deniable. If the sabotage can be traced to an inside agent, the investigation will find you.

The elimination of the quartermaster was unauthorized. Do not eliminate personnel without explicit authorization. The authorization process exists for a reason: every death creates a ripple, and ripples create patterns, and patterns create investigations. We cannot afford an investigation.

Continue your work. Stay within parameters. Report on schedule.

— W.

He sealed the letter. Placed it in the outgoing intelligence pouch — a leather bag that Threadneedle's courier would collect at dawn and deliver to the border, where it would be placed in a hollowed-out candle and purchased by a chandler who did not know and did not care.

The letter was a reprimand. The reprimand was necessary. Marcus was becoming a problem — not because he was incompetent but because he was too competent, too thorough, too willing to extend the parameters of his mission beyond what Wren had authorized. Rationality without feeling was a machine, and machines don't stop when the job is done — they stop when they're turned off. Wren was not sure he could turn Marcus off.

The promise. The promise of restoration — the possibility that Merlin could reverse the eunuch process, could give Marcus back his feelings, could make him human again. The promise was the leash. The promise was the thing that kept Marcus working for Wren rather than for himself. But the leash was fraying. Marcus's unauthorized actions were the fraying — the slow, incremental testing of boundaries by a man who was beginning to suspect that the promise was a lie, or at least an uncertainty, and the uncertainty was not enough to sustain loyalty in a man who could not feel loyalty but could calculate its value.

Wren needed to reinforce the leash. He needed to give Marcus something — not the restoration itself, which he couldn't provide, but evidence that the restoration was possible. Evidence that Merlin was working on it. Evidence that the promise was not a lie but a process, and the process was underway, and the process required patience, and patience was the one virtue that a man without feelings could still practice because patience was not a feeling but a calculation.

He needed to lie to Marcus. Again. More convincingly. With evidence.

He went to the basement. Level 3 — the Unwritten Library. The library of books that hadn't been written yet, blank pages that filled themselves as events unfolded in the world above. He found what he was looking for: a book on magical theory, half-written, the pages filling themselves with equations and diagrams that described — in the abstract, theoretical language of magical academia — the principles of emotional restoration.

The book was not about the eunuch process. The book was about something else entirely — a theoretical framework for restoring lost memories, not lost emotions. But the language was close enough that a man without feelings, reading it with the clinical precision of an engineer evaluating a blueprint, would see what Wren wanted him to see: evidence that restoration was possible. Evidence that the science existed. Evidence that the promise was real.

Wren copied three pages. Carefully. In his own hand, annotated with notes that suggested Merlin's involvement — references to "the sorcerer's preliminary findings," mentions of "ongoing research," the specific, bureaucratic language of a project in progress.

The copies were forgeries. The forgeries were convincing. The convincing was the skill — the skill of a person who had learned to create reality from paper, to build truth from lies, to construct evidence of things that did not exist with the same precision he used to manage things that did.

He included the copies in the next intelligence drop. With a note:

Marcus — enclosed are preliminary findings from the sorcerer's research into emotional restoration. The work is ongoing. The theory is sound. The practice requires time. Continue your work. The restoration depends on the success of our operation.

Every sentence was a lie. Merlin was not researching emotional restoration. Merlin was not researching anything. Merlin was asleep. The theory was not sound — it was about memory restoration, not emotional restoration, and the difference was the difference between finding a lost key and rebuilding a demolished house. The practice did not require time — the practice did not exist.

But the lies were convincing. And the convincing was the leash. The thing that kept Marcus from becoming what he was becoming — a man without feelings who was discovering that the absence of feelings was not the absence of will, and will without feeling was the most dangerous thing in the world.

Wren sealed the intelligence pouch. Set it on the desk. Picked up the next letter.

The next letter was from Elena's brother Tam. A short note, delivered through the village post, containing a joke about a chicken and a philosopher and a postscript that said: Elena says you're not eating. Eat. The chickens are worried about you. Lord Featherbottom has organized a committee.

Wren read the note. The note was — he searched for the word. Not in the vocabulary of strategy and leverage. In the other vocabulary, the one he was losing — the one that contained words like "funny" and "kind" and "warm."

Sweet. The note was sweet. The sweetness was the thing that the systems couldn't process, the thing that sat on the desk next to the forged research documents and the intelligence reports and was, by any rational measure, the least important thing on the desk and was, by any human measure, the most.

He pinned the note to the wall. Not on the map — beside the map. In the small space between the strategic and the personal, the space that was shrinking daily, the space that contained the things that mattered and the things that were useful and the increasingly desperate hope that the two categories still overlapped.

The note hung on the wall. The chickens were worried. Lord Featherbottom had organized a committee.

Wren smiled. Small. Real. The kind that didn't require calculation.

The almost was getting harder. But it was still there.
