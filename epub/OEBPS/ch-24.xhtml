Chapter 25

The Blacksmith's Hands

Thomas had been repairing swords for three weeks, and his hands were telling him things his mouth wouldn't say.

The things his hands said were: too many. Too many swords. Too many nicks and dents and the specific, ugly damage that happened when steel met steel and the meeting was not practice but purpose. Practice damage was clean — the controlled impact of two blades meeting at angles that instructors had approved. Purpose damage was different. Purpose damage was ragged, desperate, the damage of a blade that had been swung not with technique but with the animal urgency of a man trying not to die.

Thomas's hands knew the difference. Thomas's hands had been knowing the difference for twenty years, since he'd taken over the forge from his father, who had taken it from his father, who had taken it from a man whose name was lost but whose anvil remained — the same anvil Thomas used now, the iron surface worn smooth by three generations of hammering, the smoothness a record of every blade that had been made and mended and made again.

The forge was in the village — the village below Thornhold, the village that existed in the shadow of the House the way a barnacle exists on the hull of a ship: attached, dependent, aware that the ship could scrape it off at any time and choosing not to think about it. The village had a name — Thornfield — but no one used it. The village was "the village." The House was "the House." The sorcerer was "the sorcerer." The naming was the not-naming, and the not-naming was the village's way of maintaining a distance from the thing it depended on, the way you maintain a distance from a fire: close enough to be warm, far enough to not burn.

Thomas was close enough to be warm. Thomas repaired the garrison's weapons, shod the garrison's horses, and maintained the garrison's equipment with the steady, unglamorous competence of a man who understood that armies ran on iron and iron ran on blacksmiths and blacksmiths ran on the specific, calloused, heat-resistant hands that God or nature or whoever was responsible had given him.

He was also close enough to see.

The seeing was the village's second-biggest industry, after pretending. The village saw everything — the soldiers marching, the couriers riding, the supplies moving, the apprentice walking through the village at odd hours with the distracted, haunted look of a man who was carrying something invisible and enormous. The village saw and the village pretended not to see, and the pretending was the social contract, the unspoken agreement between the village and the House: we will not notice what you're doing, and you will not notice that we've noticed.

Thomas noticed. Thomas noticed because his hands told him things, and the things his hands told him were more honest than the things people's mouths told him, because hands didn't lie. Hands couldn't lie. Hands were the most honest part of a person — the part that showed what you did, not what you said, the part that recorded your life in calluses and scars and the specific, individual pattern of wear that was as unique as a fingerprint and more revealing.

The apprentice's hands had changed. Thomas had seen them — briefly, in passing, when the apprentice had come to the forge to discuss an order for new garrison equipment. The hands had been warm — Thomas had felt the warmth when they'd shaken hands, the specific, unusual warmth that was not fever and not exertion but something else, something that Thomas's grandmother would have called "the touch" and Thomas's father would have called "nonsense" and Thomas himself called "none of my business."

But the hands had changed. The calluses were different. When the apprentice had first arrived — six years ago, a boy, thin, dark-haired, with the calluses of a baker's son and the eyes of someone who expected the world to be kind — the calluses had been bread-calluses. Soft, even, the calluses of hands that kneaded dough and shaped loaves and did the gentle, repetitive work of feeding people.

Now the calluses were different. Pen-calluses. Seal-calluses. The calluses of a man who wrote and stamped and gripped and held and did not let go. The calluses of a man who had stopped making bread and started making decisions, and the decisions had changed his hands the way the decisions had changed his face — gradually, invisibly, until the change was complete and the original was gone.

Thomas had seen this before. Not in an apprentice — in soldiers. The soldiers who came back from campaigns with different hands. Hands that had learned to grip a sword instead of a plow, that had learned to strike instead of build, that had learned the specific, terrible skill of using force with precision. The soldiers' hands changed, and the changing was the record, and the record was the thing that Thomas read in the forge, when the soldiers brought their damaged swords and Thomas repaired them and the repairing was the conversation — the conversation between the blacksmith's hands and the soldier's blade, the conversation that said I know what you've done and I will fix what you've broken and the fixing is not forgiveness but it is the closest thing to forgiveness that iron can offer.

* * *

The village talked. The village always talked — talking was the village's primary form of entertainment, surpassing even the Broken Broom's ale and Old Bess's stories, which were considerable entertainments in their own right. The talking was not gossip — gossip implied malice, and the village's talking was not malicious. The talking was the village's way of processing the world, of making sense of the things it saw and pretended not to see, of constructing a narrative that explained the inexplicable and domesticated the dangerous.

The narrative was this: the apprentice was running things.

Everyone knew. No one said. The not-saying was the kingdom's second-biggest export, after pretending. But the knowing was universal — from the baker who noticed that the bread orders had been reorganized (more efficient, less wasteful, the reorganization of a mind that saw systems where others saw loaves) to the tailor who noticed that the garrison's uniforms had been standardized (same fabric, same cut, same buttons, the standardization of a mind that understood that an army that looked professional fought professional) to Thomas, who noticed that the garrison's weapons had been upgraded (better steel, better balance, better maintenance schedules, the upgrading of a mind that understood that a soldier's sword was the difference between living and dying and that the difference should not be left to chance).

The apprentice was running things. The sorcerer was sleeping. The kingdom was functioning — better than it had functioned in decades, better than it had functioned in living memory, better than anyone had expected a kingdom run by a seventeen-year-old (now twenty-three, but the village still thought of him as seventeen, because the village's memory was long and its updating was slow) could function.

The functioning was the miracle. The miracle was also the tragedy. Because the functioning was costing the apprentice something — something visible, something that the village could see even through the pretending. The grey hair. The hard eyes. The way he walked through the village with the gait of a man who was carrying weight that no one else could see, the gait of a man who was old in ways that had nothing to do with years.

Thomas saw it in the hands. The village saw it in the face. Old Bess saw it in the eyes. Everyone saw it. No one said anything. The not-saying was the contract. The contract was the kingdom.

* * *

Thomas finished the last sword at midnight. The sword was — he held it up to the forge-light, turning it, examining the edge — the sword was good. Not beautiful. A working sword. A sword that would cut and block and serve and not break, because Thomas had made it not to be admired but to be used, and the using was the thing that mattered, and the mattering was the thing that Thomas understood.

He set the sword on the rack. Wiped his hands on his apron. Looked at his hands.

His hands were the same. The same calluses — hammer-calluses, tong-calluses, the calluses of a man who shaped iron and shod horses and repaired the tools of war without fighting the war himself. His hands had not changed. His hands would not change. His hands would continue to do what they had always done — the steady, unglamorous, essential work of maintaining the things that other people used to do the things that Thomas could not do and did not want to do.

The not-wanting was not cowardice. The not-wanting was the wisdom of a man who understood his place in the machine — not the hand that swung the sword or the mind that planned the battle. The hand that made the sword. The hand that fixed the sword. The hand that ensured that when the sword was needed, the sword was ready.

It was enough. It was not glamorous. It was not the stuff of stories. But it was enough.

Thomas banked the forge. Closed the shop. Walked home through the village — the quiet village, the sleeping village, the village that saw everything and said nothing and carried on.

Above him, the House loomed. The House was awake — the House was always awake, the lights in the study burning, the wards humming, the ancient, sentient building doing the thing it had always done: watching, waiting, worrying.

In the study, a man with changed hands was answering letters.

In the forge, a man with unchanged hands had finished repairing swords.

The kingdom needed both. The kingdom always needed both — the hands that made the decisions and the hands that made the tools. The visible and the invisible. The player and the maker.

Thomas went home. His wife was asleep. His children were asleep. The house was warm — not House-warm, just warm. The warmth of a fire that someone had built and tended and kept going, the warmth of a life that was small and simple and complete.

He washed his hands. The washing was the ritual — the daily, physical, unglamorous act of cleaning the iron dust and the forge soot and the day's work from his skin. The washing was the boundary between the forge and the home, between the work and the life, between the hands that made swords and the hands that held his children.

The boundary was clean. The boundary was clear. The boundary was the thing that the apprentice didn't have — the line between the work and the life, the line that kept the work from consuming the life, the line that Thomas maintained every night with soap and water and the simple, deliberate act of washing his hands.

Thomas dried his hands. Went to bed. Slept.

In the morning, there would be more swords to repair. The war would continue. The village would watch and pretend and carry on.

And Thomas would be at his forge, doing the work that needed doing, with hands that knew the difference between practice damage and purpose damage and did not judge either, because judging was not the blacksmith's job.

The blacksmith's job was to fix what was broken.

And there was always something broken.

He thought, briefly, about the apprentice. About the changed hands. About the calluses that told the story of a boy who had stopped making bread and started making decisions. He thought about the look in the apprentice's eyes when he'd passed through the village that morning — the look of a man who was carrying something invisible and enormous and had forgotten that the carrying was a choice.

Thomas had not said anything. It was not his place to say anything. He was a blacksmith. He fixed swords. He did not fix people.

But he thought about it. And the thinking was the closest thing to prayer that Thomas knew.
