Chapter 21

The Assassin's Price

Silke arrived on a Wednesday, which was unusual, because Silke preferred Fridays.

"Fridays," she had explained once, over wine at the Broken Broom, "have a finality to them. The week is ending. People are tired. Tired people are careless. Careless people die more easily. It's not a rule — it's a preference. Like preferring red wine to white. A matter of taste."

Silke had taste. This was the thing about her that made Wren uncomfortable — not the killing or the professional detachment. The taste. Silke dressed well. Silke ate well. Silke had opinions about cheese and architecture and the proper way to fold a napkin. Silke was, in every respect except the one that mattered most, a civilized person. The civilization was the mask. The mask was so complete that it had become the face, and the face was the thing that made her dangerous, because you could sit across from Silke at a dinner table and enjoy her company and forget — genuinely forget, for whole minutes at a time — that the woman recommending the Auremere white was the same woman who had put a knife through a man's eye socket last Tuesday.

She came to the study. The House let her in — the House had learned to let Silke in, not because the House approved of Silke but because the House understood that some visitors were inevitable, the way weather was inevitable, and that the appropriate response to weather was not to lock the doors but to adjust the temperature.

"You look terrible," Silke said. She sat in the chair across from Wren's desk — the chair that visitors used, the chair that had held Threadneedle and Aldric and Finch and, once, Merlin himself, who had wandered into the study by accident and had sat in the visitor's chair and had looked around with the bewildered expression of a man who had discovered a room in his own house that he didn't know existed.

"Thank you."

"I mean it as an observation, not a criticism. You look like a man who has been running a kingdom and fighting a war and sleeping four hours a night for six years. Which is, I understand, exactly what you are. The observation is redundant. But I felt it should be said, because no one else will say it, because everyone else is either afraid of you or dependent on you or both, and fear and dependency are not conditions that encourage honesty."

"You're not afraid of me."

"I'm not afraid of anyone. It's a professional requirement. Fear is a distraction, and distractions are — well. You understand distractions. You've been eliminating them for years. Speaking of which." She placed a folder on the desk. The folder was thin — three pages, no more. Silke's reports were always thin. The thinness was a philosophy: say what needs to be said, nothing more, and let the silence carry the weight.

Wren opened the folder. Read the first page. Read the second page. Read the third page.

The target was a woman named Sera — a senior operative in Viviane's intelligence network, one of the few who had survived the network's slow collapse. Sera was not a spy in the traditional sense. She was a broker — a dealer in information, a woman who bought and sold secrets the way merchants bought and sold wool, with the same professional detachment and the same eye for value. She operated from a town called Millhaven, sixty miles west of Thornhold, and her operation was — Wren had to admit — impressive. A web of informants, couriers, and dead drops that covered the western border like a net.

The web was a problem. Not because of what it was doing — Viviane's network was collapsing, and Sera's web was collapsing with it, the informants going silent, the couriers disappearing, the dead drops going cold. The web was a problem because of what it could become. Sera was freelance now — unaffiliated, uncontrolled, a professional with skills and connections and no employer. A freelance intelligence broker on Thornhold's western border was a vulnerability. A vulnerability that any of the three witch-queens — or their remnants — could exploit.

"The contract is straightforward," Silke said. "Sera operates from a house on the edge of Millhaven. She has two guards — competent, not exceptional. She follows a routine — market in the morning, meetings in the afternoon, correspondence in the evening. The evening is the window. The guards change shift at sundown. There's a gap — four minutes, maybe five. Enough."

"The price?"

"Two hundred gold. The standard rate for a protected target in a semi-urban environment. Plus expenses — travel, lodging, the wine I'll drink afterward, which is not optional. The wine is part of the process. The process requires wine."

"The other price."

Silke looked at him. The look was — not warm. Silke was not warm. But the look was honest, which was rarer than warmth and more valuable. The honesty of a woman who killed people for money and did not pretend that the killing was anything other than what it was.

"The other price," she said. "Yes. The one you pay in the mirror."

"You said that before. After the first contract."

"I said it because it's true. The price goes up. Not the money — two hundred gold, whether it's the first contract or the fifth. The other thing changes. The thing you see when you look at yourself. The thing that looks back."

"I don't look in mirrors."

"I know. That's the price. The not-looking is the price. The avoidance is the cost. You stop looking because you're afraid of what you'll see, and the fear is the thing that tells you the price is being paid, and the paying is the thing that changes you, and the changing is the thing you can't undo."

She said this calmly. The calmness was not indifference — it was the calm of a woman who had paid the price herself, who had looked in the mirror and seen the thing that looked back and had made her peace with it, or at least her truce, the kind of truce that held as long as the wine lasted and the work continued and the not-thinking was maintained.

"I've killed eleven people," Silke said. "Professionally. Eleven contracts over fifteen years. That's less than one a year, which sounds manageable, which is the lie I tell myself. The lie works most of the time. The lie fails on the nights when I can't sleep, which are more frequent than they used to be. I'm telling you because you're twenty-three and you look forty and you've ordered more deaths than I've executed and you don't look in mirrors and the not-looking is going to kill you faster than any witch-queen."

The study was quiet. The candles burned. The House listened — the House always listened, the House couldn't help listening, the House was a building made of ears and opinions and the ancient, structural anxiety of a thing that loved its keeper and could not protect him from the thing that was consuming him.

"Take the contract," Wren said.

"I will. I always do. That's my price — the always doing. The never saying no. The professional obligation that is also the professional curse. I will take the contract and I will complete the contract and I will drink the wine and I will not sleep well for three nights and then I will sleep and the sleeping will be the forgetting and the forgetting will be the lie and the lie will hold until the next contract."

She stood. Took the folder. Folded it precisely — Silke folded everything precisely, the precision a small, daily act of control in a life that was otherwise defined by the uncontrollable — and placed it inside her coat.

"One more thing," she said. "The woman — Sera. She has a daughter. Seven years old. The daughter lives with Sera's sister in a village north of Millhaven. The daughter is not part of the contract. The daughter will never be part of any contract. But the daughter exists, and the existence is the thing I want you to know, because knowing is part of the price, and the price is the thing you're paying, and the paying should not be comfortable."

She left. The door closed. The study was quiet.

Wren sat at his desk. The candles burned. The maps stared from the walls. The letters waited in their stacks — the endless, infinite, never-diminishing stacks of letters that were the physical manifestation of a kingdom's need, each letter a problem, each problem a weight, each weight a piece of the thing that was pressing him into the shape of the stranger in the mirror he didn't look at.

He thought about Sera. About the web of informants and couriers and dead drops. About the vulnerability on the western border. About the strategic necessity of removing the vulnerability before it could be exploited.

He thought about the daughter. Seven years old. Living with an aunt in a village north of Millhaven. A daughter who would wake up one morning and learn that her mother was dead, and the learning would be the wound, and the wound would be the thing she carried, and the carrying would shape her the way Wren's father's leaving had shaped Wren — invisibly, permanently, the architecture of a life built on an absence.

He thought about his father. About the market. About the different direction.

He picked up the pen. Wrote the authorization. Sealed it. Set it aside for Silke's courier.

The authorization was the calculation. The calculation was correct. The correctness was the price.

He did not look in a mirror that night. He already knew what he would see.
