Chapter 35

The Confrontation

Wren found Merlin in the sitting room at midnight.

The finding was deliberate. The deliberateness was the thing that had been building for six years — building since the first morning, since the discovery of the neglected study, since the realization that the apprenticeship was not an apprenticeship but a sentence, and the sentence was life, and the life was the kingdom, and the kingdom was the weight.

Merlin was in his chair. The chair that had molded itself to his body. The chair that was less furniture and more philosophical statement — the statement being that the horizontal position was the natural state of being and that anyone who disagreed was welcome to their opinion but would not be changing Merlin's.

The fire was low. The mead was half-drunk — a bottle of the memory-mead, the fermented feelings, the borrowed emotions that Merlin used to replace the ones that had become too heavy to carry. The bottle's label, in Merlin's sprawling handwriting: A child's laughter, 1302. The child grew up. The laughter didn't.

Merlin was not asleep. Both eyes were open — the grey and the blue, the seeing and the feeling. Both eyes were looking at the fire. Both eyes were — Wren recognized the look — both eyes were present. Fully present. The rare, unsettling presence of a being who was usually somewhere else and was now, for reasons that Wren could not determine, here.

"Sit down," Merlin said. Not looking at Wren. Looking at the fire. The voice was — different. The voice was quiet. The quiet was — Wren recognized it — the quiet of a person who was about to have a conversation they had been avoiding and had run out of avoidance.

Wren did not sit. Wren stood in the doorway and looked at the most powerful sorcerer in the world and felt the ice — the ice that had formed during the siege, the ice that was the hatred in its final form, the form that did not burn but froze, the form that was permanent.

"Six years," Wren said.

The words were not planned. The words came from the place below the systems — the place that was still human, still capable of feeling, still able to produce words that were not strategic and not calculated and not part of any scheme. The words came from the crack. The crack that had been growing since the garden. The crack that was now large enough to let the truth through.

"Six years," he said again. "I have been here for six years. I arrived at seventeen. I am twenty-three. I look forty. I have grey hair and lines around my eyes and a mouth that has forgotten how to smile and hands that have forgotten how to make bread. I have managed your kingdom. I have maintained your wards. I have paid your soldiers and answered your letters and neutralized your enemies and carried your debts and done every single thing that you were supposed to do and chose not to. For six years. While you slept."

Merlin said nothing. The fire crackled. The mead bottle sat between them, half-drunk, glowing faintly with borrowed feelings.

"I am not angry," Wren said. The words were quiet. The quiet was the truth — the genuine quiet of a man who has passed through anger and come out the other side into something colder and more permanent. "I was angry. For years. The anger was useful — it kept me moving, kept me working, kept the pilot light burning when everything else was running out. But the anger is gone now. What's left is — this."

He gestured at himself. At the grey hair and the hard mouth and the cold eyes and the face that was twenty-three and looked forty.

"This is what six years of carrying your kingdom looks like. This is the cost. I am not asking for guilt or apology or action, because asking you to do things has never worked and I have stopped expecting it to work. I am telling you because you should know. Because the person who built this House and built this kingdom and built the reputation that kept the witch-queens afraid — that person should know what his indifference costs."

Merlin was quiet for a long time. The grey eye was open. The grey eye was seeing.

"I know," Merlin said. Quietly. The quiet of a man who has known for a long time and has been waiting for someone to say it out loud. "I have always known. The knowing is the thing I drink to forget."

"Does it work?"

"No. The mead gives me other people's feelings. It doesn't take away my own." He looked at the bottle. "Nothing takes away your own. That's the lesson of a thousand years. You can borrow other feelings. You can drown your own. But they're always there when you surface. Waiting."

Wren looked at him. The hatred was there — the cold, permanent ice of it. But underneath the ice, the crack. The crack that had been growing since the mead cellar, since the understanding. The crack that let the human through.

"I'm leaving," Wren said.

Merlin opened both eyes. Both eyes. The grey and the blue.

"I know that too," Merlin said. "I have known since the garden. Since the crack." He paused. "The House told me. In temperatures."

"The House tells you things?"

"The House has always told me things. I stopped listening. That was my first mistake. There were many after it." He looked at Wren — both eyes, the seeing and the feeling, both present, both honest. "You have done extraordinary work. The work you did was extraordinary. The kingdom is safer than it has been in centuries. The systems you built will hold. The manual you're writing will teach whoever comes next."

"You've read it?"

"I've been reading it for weeks. You leave it on the desk. I come in at night, when you're in the garden or the kitchen. I read." He almost smiled. "You write about me with remarkable restraint."

"I wrote what was useful."

"You wrote what was true. The useful and the true are not always the same thing. You knew the difference. That was always your gift." He set down the mead. "Go. Go to the farm. Go to the woman with flour on her cheek. Go to the life that is waiting for you. I will — I will try. I make no promises about the quality of the trying. But I will try."

Wren stood. The standing was — the standing was the end of something. Six years of standing in this room, in this House, in this kingdom. The last standing.

"The manual is on the desk," Wren said. "Read it. All of it. Not just the parts about the wards."

"I will."

"The furnace needs feeding every morning. Not just when you remember."

"I will."

"Aldric is good. Trust him."

"I will."

Wren looked at Merlin. Merlin looked at Wren. The looking was — the last looking. The looking of two people who had shared a building and a kingdom and six years of the specific, complicated, morally ambiguous relationship between a man who needed someone to carry his weight and a boy who had been foolish enough to pick it up.

"Goodbye, Merlin," Wren said.

"Goodbye, Wren." A pause. "The House will miss you. I will — I will notice your absence. The noticing is the closest thing to missing that I can manage. I am sorry it is not more."

Wren left the sitting room. The door closed behind him. The fire crackled. Merlin sat in his chair and looked at the mead and did not drink it.

For the first time in centuries, the mead was not what he needed.

He picked up the manual from the side table where he'd left it. Opened it to page one. The wards. The ward anchors. The maintenance schedules.

He began to read.
