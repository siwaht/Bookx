Chapter 14

The Routine

Dawn: the wards.

Wren walked the ward line every morning at first light. The line ran along the kingdom's border — not the political border, which was a line on a map, but the magical border, which was a line in reality, a seam where Merlin's power met the world's indifference and held it back. The seam was maintained by anchor points — stones, carved with sigils, placed at intervals along the border by Merlin centuries ago and maintained by no one since, until Wren.

The walk took two hours. Cold morning air and wet grass and the meditative labor of checking each anchor point — feeling the magic with his warm hands, sensing the cracks, sealing them. The sealing was small magic — not the reality-bending chaos of Merlin's power but the careful, invisible work of maintenance. The work of a person who shows up every day and does the thing that needs doing and does it well and does it without recognition and does it because the doing is the thing that keeps the world from falling apart.

The wards were holding. They were always holding, because Wren was always maintaining them. The holding was the absence of drama, the absence of breach. The best maintenance is the kind no one notices, because the noticing only happens when the maintenance fails.

He finished the ward walk at 7 AM. Returned to the House. The front door opened — the rust-words said GOOD MORNING, which was what they always said, which was the House's way of acknowledging the routine, the daily return, the reliable presence of a keeper who left every morning and came back every morning and the coming back was the thing the House needed most.

7:15 AM: the furnace.

The Furnace of Forgotten Things sat between the basement and the ground floor, in a space that shouldn't exist. Wren descended the stairs — the House's stairs, which adjusted their angle based on the urgency of the descent, steep when the furnace was hungry, gentle when it was fed. This morning, the stairs were moderate. The furnace was not hungry. The furnace was content — the content of a fire that has been fed regularly and reliably and has learned to trust that the feeding will continue.

The forgotten things looked like grey dust. Mostly. But sometimes, in the dust, shapes — a face, a place, a moment. Someone's first kiss, forgotten. Someone's grandmother's voice, lost. Someone's childhood bedroom, the wallpaper pattern fading from memory like a photograph left in the sun. The shapes were melancholy. The shoveling was melancholy work. But the shoveling was necessary, because the furnace was the House's heart, and the heart needed fuel, and the fuel was the accumulated detritus of human forgetting, and human forgetting was infinite and reliable and the one resource that never ran out.

Wren shoveled. The warmth from the furnace met the warmth from his hands and the two warmths merged and the merging was the closest thing to peace he experienced in any given day — the peace of a body doing physical work, the peace of a mind that could, for twenty minutes, focus on the shovel and the dust and the fire and nothing else.

7:45 AM: breakfast.

Elena brought the tray. The tray contained: bread (fresh, the kitchen's morning offering), butter (the kitchen's opinion on whether the butter had gone off, expressed through inclusion or exclusion), tea (always tea, never coffee, the House did not approve of coffee and expressed its disapproval by making coffee taste like regret), and whatever the kitchen had decided Wren needed that morning, which was usually something sweet, because the kitchen had determined — through whatever calculus a sentient stove uses to assess the nutritional needs of its occupants — that Wren's diet was deficient in joy and was attempting to compensate with sugar.

Wren ate at the desk. He ate quickly. He did not taste. The not-tasting was the routine — eating converted from experience into function, fuel intake rather than pleasure.

Elena set the tray down. Said good morning. Wren said good morning. The exchange was brief — the minimum viable interaction between two people who occupied the same building and the same concern and different worlds.

She left. He ate. The bread was good. He didn't notice.

8 AM: correspondence.

The morning post arrived through the front door — the House sorted it, separating the mundane from the magical from the dangerous, placing each category in a different pile on the study desk. The mundane pile was largest: trade disputes, tax inquiries, petitions from villagers, requests for magical services. The magical pile was smaller: ward reports from the border stations, supply requests for magical materials, the occasional letter from a hedge witch seeking licensing. The dangerous pile was smallest: intelligence reports from Threadneedle's network, coded messages from Marcus, diplomatic correspondence from the witch-queens' courts.

Wren processed the mundane first. The processing was efficient — each letter read, assessed, responded to, filed. Three sentences for a trade dispute, two for a tax inquiry, one for a petition. The efficiency was the product of months of practice — learning to extract the essential information in seconds and respond in minutes and move on without the human tendency to wonder whether the response was right or just fast.

The magical pile next. Ward reports: stable. Supply requests: approved, with budget allocations from the treasury. Hedge witch licensing: three new applications, two approved, one denied (the applicant's references were fabricated, which Wren knew because Threadneedle had investigated the applicant as a matter of routine and had discovered that the applicant was not a hedge witch but a spy from Viviane's network, and the denial was not a licensing decision but a counter-intelligence operation disguised as bureaucracy).

The dangerous pile last. Always last. Because the dangerous pile required not just processing but thinking, and thinking required time, and time was the one resource Wren could not manufacture.

10 AM: briefing with Aldric.

The briefing happened in the courtyard. Aldric preferred the courtyard — the open air, the sound of soldiers drilling, the physical reality of the garrison that was his responsibility and his pride and his constant, low-grade anxiety. The briefing was structured: garrison status, supply levels, morale assessment, threat analysis. Aldric delivered the information with military precision — clipped sentences, specific numbers, no editorializing.

"Garrison strength: one hundred and twelve. Up from forty when you started. Morale: moderate. The pay helps. The equipment helps more. The soldiers feel like soldiers now, not volunteers. Threat assessment: Nimue's eastern regiment has pulled back to Greymarch. The pullback is either a retreat or a repositioning. I'm assuming repositioning because assuming retreat gets people killed."

"Supply levels?"

"Three months of provisions. Six months if we ration. The southern trade route is producing — the farming villages are delivering on schedule. Elena's family's farm is one of the most reliable suppliers. Good people. Good carrots."

The mention of Elena's family was not strategic. Aldric mentioned them because they were part of the supply chain and the supply chain was part of the briefing and the briefing was part of the routine. But the mention landed differently than the other data points — it landed in the space between the strategic and the personal, the space where Elena existed, the space that was shrinking.

"Continue the repositioning assumption," Wren said. "Increase patrols on the eastern border. If Nimue's regiment is repositioning, I want to know where and when."

"Done."

The briefing ended. Aldric returned to the garrison. Wren returned to the study.

11 AM to 1 PM: intelligence review.

Threadneedle's reports. Marcus's drops. The network's accumulated output — a river of information flowing into the study, processed by a single mind, converted into strategy. The review was the most demanding part of the day — not physically but cognitively. The information was vast, contradictory, incomplete. Patterns had to be extracted from noise. Signals had to be separated from static. The truth had to be assembled from fragments, the way a broken pot is assembled from shards — piece by piece, with patience and precision and the constant awareness that the assembled truth might still be wrong, because the shards might not all belong to the same pot.

Today's review: Morgause's court was quiet. Too quiet. The quiet after Wren's second visit was either the quiet of a woman processing a genuine connection or the quiet of a woman planning something. Both were possible. Both required monitoring.

Nimue's army was regrouping. Marcus's latest report confirmed the repositioning — not a retreat but a consolidation. Nimue was pulling her forces together, concentrating them, preparing for something. The something was not yet clear. The not-yet-clear was the danger.

Viviane's network was fragmenting. The fragmentation was accelerating — operatives going freelance, contacts going silent, the web of obligations and blackmail that sustained Hollowmere's intelligence apparatus unraveling like a sweater pulled from a single thread. The unraveling was useful (fewer enemies) and dangerous (freelance operatives were unpredictable, and unpredictable enemies were worse than organized ones).

1 PM: lunch.

Elena brought the tray. Wren did not eat. Elena did not comment. The not-commenting was the routine — not acceptance but patience, and patience was the only tool she had.

2 PM to 5 PM: strategy.

The afternoon was for planning. Not reactive planning — the morning's correspondence and intelligence review were reactive, responding to events as they occurred. The afternoon was for proactive planning — the long game, the moves that would pay off in weeks or months, the slow, invisible construction of a strategic position that would make Thornhold not just safe but unassailable.

Today's strategy session: the alliance with Morgause. The alliance was Wren's most ambitious project — a formal agreement between Thornhold and Auremere that would neutralize the eastern threat and create a combined front against Nimue and Viviane. The alliance required careful management — Morgause was an ally and a lover and a witch-queen and a potential enemy, and the categories overlapped in ways that made the management exhausting.

He drafted terms. Revised them. Drafted again. The terms had to be precise — precise enough to bind Morgause without constraining Thornhold, generous enough to satisfy Morgause's pride without compromising Thornhold's sovereignty, flexible enough to accommodate the inevitable complications that arose when two kingdoms with different interests and different values and different definitions of "trust" tried to cooperate.

5 PM: the garden.

Not for pleasure. For maintenance. The garden was the House's heart — the one place the House didn't rearrange, the sacred ground where the emotion-flowers bloomed and the déjà vu tree fruited and the pond reflected intentions instead of faces. The garden required tending — not magical tending but physical tending, the weeding and watering and pruning that kept the magical plants healthy and the magical ecosystem balanced.

Wren tended the garden for thirty minutes. The tending was — like the furnace shoveling — the closest thing to peace. The physical work. The dirt under his fingernails. The warmth of the soil. The simple, ancient satisfaction of making something grow.

The emotion-flowers bloomed around him. The colors were — he had stopped being able to read them. The colors were emotions, and emotions were the thing he was losing, and the losing meant the colors were becoming abstract, becoming data, becoming inputs in a system rather than experiences in a life.

The flower nearest his hand was the color of exhaustion. He knew this not because he felt it but because he recognized the pattern — the specific, grey-tinged blue that the flowers produced when the person nearest them was running on empty. The flower was a mirror. The mirror showed exhaustion. The exhaustion was a fact.

He filed the fact. Continued weeding.

6 PM: dinner.

Elena brought the tray. He ate. Sometimes. Some evenings he ate. Some evenings he didn't. The eating or not-eating depended on something below the level of conscious decision, in the animal part of the brain that still remembered what hunger felt like and occasionally, unpredictably, insisted on being fed.

7 PM to midnight: the study.

The evening was for the work that couldn't be done during the day — the secret work, the shadow work, the work that required darkness not because it was evil but because it was invisible, and invisibility required the absence of witnesses, and witnesses were fewer at night.

The forged letters. The coded messages to Marcus. The intelligence assessments that Threadneedle delivered after dark, when the village was asleep and the delivery could not be observed. The strategic planning that required the full, uninterrupted focus of a mind that had spent the day processing a kingdom's worth of information and was now, in the quiet of the evening, synthesizing that information into action.

The candle burned. The House breathed. The maps stared from the walls.

Midnight to 3 AM: the weight.

This was not a scheduled activity. This was the thing that happened when the scheduled activities ended and the mind, released from the structure of the routine, turned inward. The weight was not a task — it was a condition. The condition of a person who has spent eighteen hours managing a kingdom and is now, in the dark, alone with the accumulated cost of the managing.

The cost was specific. The cost was: Corwin, dead on a Thursday. Marcus, killing without authorization. Morgause, kissed in a study full of intelligence reports. Elena, bringing meals he didn't eat. His mother, reading a letter that said everything was fine. Bess, telling him to look in a mirror. Aldric, following orders he didn't agree with. The soldiers, three of them dead in the eastern border attack, their names filed in the same architecture that filed trade disputes and assassination orders.

The cost accumulated. The accumulation was the weight. The weight was the thing he carried from midnight to 3 AM, in the dark, in the study, alone.

3 AM: sleep.

Four hours. Sometimes three. Rarely five. The sleep was dreamless — the dreamlessness that had started after Corwin's death and had not stopped, the dreamlessness that was not rest but absence, the absence of the subconscious processing that normal sleep provides, replaced by a blank, black nothing that was not peace but void.

He slept. The House held still. The warmth burned on, even in sleep — the warmth radiating from his hands into the pillow, into the sheets, into the walls of the small room that had grown larger over the months but never large enough, because no room was large enough to contain the thing he was becoming.

Dawn: the wards.

The routine began again. The routine was the prison. The kingdom was everything.

And the everything was the thing that was killing him. Slowly. Invisibly. The way a building kills its keeper — not through violence but through need, through the constant, insatiable, loving, terrible need of a thing that cannot survive without you and will consume you in the surviving.

The routine continued. The warmth burned.

And everything was running out.
