Chapter 16

The War Begins

The rider came at dawn.

Not the gentle dawn of the farming villages — the hard dawn of Thornhold, where the light hit the House's walls at an angle that made the stone look like teeth.

Wren was in the study. He had been in the study since 3 AM, since the crack in the garden, since the three minutes of crying that had changed nothing and everything. The crack was sealed. The defenses were rebuilt. The systems were online. But the crack was there, underneath, the way a fault line is there underneath a city — invisible, patient, waiting.

The rider was one of Aldric's border scouts — a woman named Kael, who rode the way soldiers ride when the news they carry is heavier than they are. She came through the front gate at a gallop, dismounted before the horse had stopped, and was in the study before the House had time to decide whether to open the door or make her knock.

The House opened the door. The House had learned, over six years, that when soldiers ran, doors should open.

"Eastern border," Kael said. She was breathing hard. Her horse was breathing harder. "Nimue. Not a skirmish or a probe. A full assault. Three regiments. Eunuch soldiers — the emotionless ones, the ones who don't stop. They hit the border garrison at Ashenmere two hours before dawn. The garrison held for forty minutes. The garrison is gone."

Wren's hand did not stop writing. The hand was answering a letter — a trade dispute about wool tariffs that was, in the grand architecture of the kingdom, approximately as important as the color of the curtains in a burning building. The hand finished the letter. Set down the pen. Picked up the red one — the one that meant the words were not suggestions.

"Casualties," he said.

"Fourteen dead. Twenty wounded. The rest retreated to Thornfield — the village, not the House. They're holding the bridge but they won't hold it long. The eunuch soldiers don't tire. They don't stop. They don't—"

"Feel. I know." He was already at the map. The map of the eastern border was pinned to the wall behind his desk — a map he had drawn himself, over months, from Aldric's reports and Threadneedle's intelligence and Marcus's stolen supply manifests. The map showed every garrison, every bridge, every chokepoint, every village that could be defended and every village that couldn't. "Ashenmere is here. Thornfield bridge is here. The gap between them is — twelve miles of open ground. If Nimue's regiments take the bridge, they have a clear road to the Greenvale pass, and the Greenvale pass is the door to the heartland."

"Captain Aldric is mobilizing the garrison. He sent me ahead to—"

"To ask for orders. Yes." Wren was writing. Not the trade letter — a different letter. Three letters, actually. The red pen moved with the specific, efficient speed of a mind that had been preparing for this moment for two years and was now, with the cold clarity of a machine engaging its primary function, executing the preparation.

The first letter was to Aldric. Hold the bridge. Do not advance. Do not engage beyond the bridge. The bridge is the line. If the bridge falls, retreat to Greenvale and hold the pass. I will send reinforcements within six hours.

The second letter was to Threadneedle. Activate the eastern network. I need eyes on Nimue's supply lines. The assault is too large for a border probe — she's committed three regiments, which means she's either desperate or confident, and I need to know which before I can respond. Also: where is Marcus? His last report was four days ago. Four days of silence from Marcus is not silence. It's a scream.

The third letter was to Morgause. This letter was more careful — written in the personal code they had developed during the Auremere visits, the code that looked like love letters and contained military intelligence. My darling — the weather has turned cold in the east. Three storms, moving fast. I find myself thinking of your garden, where the weather is always kind. Perhaps you might send word of conditions in your quarter — whether the storms have reached you, whether your borders feel the wind. I remain, as always, yours in the warmth. Translation: Nimue is attacking my eastern border with three regiments. Is she also moving against you? Are we facing a coordinated assault or a single-front war? Tell me now.

He sealed the letters. Handed them to Kael. "The first goes to Aldric — fastest rider you have. The second goes to the woman who runs the tea shop on Milliner's Lane — she'll know what to do with it. The third goes east, to Auremere, by the diplomatic courier route. Not the fast route — the diplomatic route. I want Morgause to receive it in three days, not one. The delay is intentional."

"The delay—"

"If Morgause thinks I'm panicking, she'll exploit it. If she thinks I'm calm enough to send a letter by slow courier, she'll respect it. Perception is the first battlefield. Now go."

Kael went. The door closed. The study was quiet.

Wren sat at his desk. The map stared at him from the wall — the eastern border, the garrisons, the bridges, the chokepoints. Three regiments of eunuch soldiers. Emotionless, tireless, relentless. The perfect army for a witch who ruled through despair — soldiers who could not be demoralized because they could not feel, who could not be frightened because fear was an emotion they had traded away, who could not be broken because breaking required something whole to start with.

He thought about Marcus. Four days of silence. Marcus, who reported every two days like clockwork. Four days meant something had changed. Either Marcus had been discovered, or Marcus had gone dark deliberately — which was worse.

The "plus more" problem. Marcus always did what Wren asked, plus more. The unauthorized operations were Marcus's own initiative, driven by the cold, precise revenge of a man who remembered what feelings were and wanted to punish the system that had taken them.

Wren pulled the intelligence file. Marcus's last report: a supply manifest showing Nimue's eastern regiments were being provisioned for a six-week campaign. Six weeks of supplies for three regiments — that was not a border skirmish. That was an invasion. Marcus had flagged it, annotated it with his clinical handwriting: She's committed. This is not a test. This is the real thing. I recommend accelerating the sabotage timeline. I have identified three supply depots that, if destroyed, would cripple the campaign within two weeks. Awaiting authorization.

Wren had not authorized the destruction. He had written back: Hold. Do not escalate. I need the supply lines intact for now — if we destroy them too early, Nimue will know she has a traitor and will hunt for him. Patience.

Marcus had not responded. Four days of silence.

Patience was not Marcus's currency. Marcus's patience was calculation, and calculation without the moderating influence of emotion was a machine that optimized for efficiency without regard for consequence.

Wren closed the file. Opened the military assessment. The numbers were not good.

Thornhold's garrison: two hundred soldiers, well-equipped, well-trained, loyal. Against three regiments of eunuch soldiers — roughly fifteen hundred, if Nimue was using standard regiment sizes. The numbers were the kind that made strategists reach for the word "untenable" and generals reach for the word "retreat."

But numbers were not the only variable. Terrain was a variable — the eastern border was mountainous, full of chokepoints and narrow passes that negated numerical superiority. Supply lines were a variable — Nimue's army was far from home, and far-from-home armies ate their own logistics. And the House was a variable — the wards, the defenses, the ancient magic that protected Thornhold's heartland. The wards would hold. The wards always held. The wards were Wren's work, maintained and strengthened over six years, and the maintaining was the one piece of actual magic he did, the one thing that justified the word "apprentice" in his title.

He began to plan. The planning was the thing the crack had not touched. The cold, precise, strategic machinery of a mind that saw the board and moved the pieces and did not feel the moving.

He planned for six hours. The planning consumed three pots of tea, two candles, and the last of his capacity for optimism. The plan that emerged was not elegant — not the three-letters-and-a-stamp elegance of Harren's Ford. This was a grinding plan. A plan of attrition and delay and the slow, expensive, morally exhausting business of fighting a war you couldn't win quickly against an enemy that couldn't feel.

The plan was: hold the bridge. Delay the advance. Let Nimue's supply lines stretch. Let Marcus's sabotage — if Marcus was still operational, if Marcus hadn't been discovered, if Marcus hadn't gone rogue — eat the logistics from within. Buy time. Time was the weapon. Time and terrain and the simple, brutal mathematics of an army that was far from home and getting farther.

The plan would work. The plan would cost lives. The cost was the thing he filed and did not feel, because feeling the cost was a luxury the kingdom could not afford, and the kingdom's inability to afford luxuries was the thing that was turning him into the stranger in the mirror.

* * *

Merlin slept through it.

The entire assault — the dawn attack, the fall of Ashenmere, the retreat to Thornfield, the mobilization of the garrison, the six hours of strategic planning that would determine whether the kingdom survived the week — Merlin slept through all of it.

Wren found him at noon, in the sitting room, in the chair that had molded itself to his body over centuries of indolence. Merlin was snoring. The snoring was operatic — a full-throated, multi-octave performance that suggested the sorcerer's lungs, at least, were in excellent working order, even if his sense of responsibility was not.

"Merlin."

The snoring continued. A particularly ambitious passage suggested Merlin was dreaming about something that required significant respiratory effort. Possibly Margaret. Possibly the goat.

"Merlin. We're being invaded."

One eye opened. The grey eye — the one that saw through things. It regarded Wren with the focused attention of a being that was simultaneously the most powerful sorcerer alive and the most committed to not using that power for anything useful.

"Invaded," Merlin repeated. Flat. Not alarmed or concerned. The way a man says "rain" when told it's raining — an acknowledgment of a fact that does not require action because rain is what happens and invasions are what happen and both of them will pass if you wait long enough.

"Nimue. Three regiments. Eastern border. Ashenmere has fallen. Fourteen dead."

"Fourteen." Merlin closed the eye. "That's unfortunate."

"Unfortunate."

"Mm. Unfortunate. The word means — well, you know what it means. You know what all the words mean. That's your particular talent, isn't it? Knowing what words mean and using them to make people do things. Very impressive. Very tiring to watch. Is there tea?"

Wren stood in the doorway and looked at the most powerful sorcerer in the world and felt the hatred — the cold hatred, the hatred that didn't burn but froze — settle into a new depth. Not a new kind of hatred. The same hatred, deeper. The hatred of a man who has just learned that fourteen soldiers are dead and is standing in front of the person who could have prevented it with a thought, a gesture, a moment of caring, and who chose instead to sleep.

"You could end this," Wren said. "You could stand up. Walk to the eastern border. Raise your hand. And every eunuch soldier in Nimue's army would stop. Not die — stop. Because you are Merlin. Because your name is the thing that keeps this kingdom alive. Because the fear of you is the wall that protects us. And you could make the fear real. You could make it so real that Nimue would never send another soldier south. You could do it in an afternoon. You could do it before tea."

Merlin opened both eyes. Both eyes — the grey and the blue, the seeing and the feeling, the truth and the beauty. Both eyes looked at Wren, and in both eyes there was something that was not indifference. Something older. Something that looked like — Wren searched for the word and found it and wished he hadn't — something that looked like pity.

"I could," Merlin said. "You're right. I could end it. I could end all of it — the war, the witches, the politics, the whole exhausting game. I could unmake Nimue's army. I could unmake Nimue. I could unmake the concept of invasion and replace it with something more pleasant, like butterflies or the smell of fresh bread. I have that power. You know I have that power. Everyone knows I have that power. That's the point."

"Then why—"

"Because the point is the having, not the using. The moment I use it — the moment I stand up and walk to the border and raise my hand — the having becomes the using, and the using becomes the expectation, and the expectation becomes the dependency, and the dependency becomes the thing that destroys everything more thoroughly than any army ever could." He closed his eyes again. "You're clever enough to understand that. You're clever enough to understand everything. That's your problem. You understand so much that you've forgotten how to not understand, and the not-understanding is where the living happens."

"Fourteen people are dead."

"Yes. And if I intervene, fourteen hundred will die next time, because next time the enemy will come prepared for me, and the preparation will be worse than the attack, and the response will be worse than the preparation, and the escalation will continue until the only option left is the one that destroys everything. I have seen it. I have seen it a hundred times. I have been the escalation. I will not be it again."

The speech was the longest thing Merlin had said in months. The length was — Wren filed it. Filed the pity. Filed the logic, which was — he hated to admit it — not wrong. Not right, either. But not wrong. The logic of a being who understood that power used was power expected, and power expected was power demanded, and power demanded was the end of everything.

The logic was sound. The logic was also the excuse of a man who didn't want to get out of his chair.

"Tea," Merlin said. "If it's not too much trouble. And perhaps a biscuit. The House makes excellent biscuits when it's anxious, and the House is very anxious right now. I can feel it in the floorboards."

Wren left the room. He did not bring tea. He did not bring biscuits. He went back to the study and picked up the red pen and continued planning the defense of a kingdom whose protector would not protect it.

The pilot light burned. The fuel was fourteen names he would learn and file and carry.

The war had begun. And the names were only the beginning.
