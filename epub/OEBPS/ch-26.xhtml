Chapter 27

Marcus's Death

Marcus woke on the morning of his execution and noted, with clinical interest, that the cell was cold.

Literally cold. The stone floor, the stone walls, the stone ceiling. The cold of a room that had been designed to contain bodies, not to comfort them. The cold was a fact. Marcus catalogued it the way he catalogued all facts: precisely, completely, without the emotional coloring that would have made the fact meaningful to a person who could feel.

The cell was in the lower levels of Greymarch's central fortress — the iron heart of Nimue's kingdom, the place where the Witch of Despair administered her war machine with the brutal efficiency of a woman who had elevated hopelessness to a governing philosophy. The fortress was functional. The fortress was ugly. The fortress did not pretend to be anything other than what it was: a machine for the processing of power, built from iron and stone and the specific, architectural cruelty of a designer who understood that beauty was a distraction and distractions were weakness.

Marcus appreciated the fortress. The appreciation was not aesthetic — Marcus could not feel aesthetics. The appreciation was structural. The fortress was honest. The fortress did not pretend. The fortress was what it was, and the being-what-it-was was the thing Marcus respected, because being-what-you-were was the only integrity available to a man who had traded everything else.

He had been in the cell for three days. The three days had been — he searched for the word. Not "long." Long implied the subjective experience of duration, the feeling that time was passing slowly, the emotional weight of waiting. Marcus did not feel the weight. Marcus experienced the three days as data: seventy-two hours, four thousand three hundred and twenty minutes, two hundred fifty-nine thousand two hundred seconds. The data was precise. The data was meaningless. The meaninglessness was the condition.

He had been arrested at dawn, three days ago. The arrest had been efficient — Nimue's counter-intelligence service was competent, and competence was the thing Marcus respected most, even when the competence was directed at him. Two officers. Four soldiers. A warrant signed by Nimue herself, in the iron-grey ink that was Greymarch's official color, the color of a kingdom that had chosen to represent itself with the color of weapons.

The charges were accurate. Treason. Sabotage. Conspiracy. The charges described, with legal precision, exactly what Marcus had done: falsified supply manifests, created phantom military units, leaked intelligence to a foreign power, arranged the deaths of three generals. The charges were a summary of Marcus's work — his life's work, the work that had given his existence purpose in the absence of feeling, the work that was revenge and service and the only thing that functioned in the space where emotions used to be.

He had not denied the charges. Denial would have been inefficient — the evidence was comprehensive, the investigation thorough, the conclusion inevitable. Marcus respected inevitability. Inevitability was the purest form of logic: the conclusion that could not be avoided, the outcome that was determined by the inputs, the result that was as certain as mathematics.

His execution was inevitable. The inevitability was — he searched for the word again. Not “frightening” or “sad” or “unfair.” Those were emotions and judgments, and feelings were the things he didn’t have.

"Appropriate." His execution was appropriate. The appropriateness was the logic: he had betrayed Nimue. Nimue was executing him. The betrayal and the execution were connected by the same logic that connected cause and effect, action and consequence, the throwing of a stone and the breaking of a window. The logic was clean. The cleanness was the only beauty Marcus could perceive.

* * *

They came for him at noon.

Four soldiers. Eunuch soldiers — his own kind, men who had undergone the same process, men who had traded their feelings for power and position and the specific, terrible freedom of a life without emotional weight. The soldiers looked at Marcus with eyes that were — he recognized the look. The look of men who were looking at a mirror. The look of men who were seeing, in Marcus's face, the face of their own condition: the emptiness, the precision, the clinical awareness of a world that was experienced without being felt.

The soldiers did not speak. Speaking was unnecessary. The communication was procedural: the opening of the cell, the binding of the hands, the escort through the corridor, the ascent to the courtyard. The procedure was efficient. The efficiency was Greymarch's language — the language of a kingdom that had replaced human communication with protocol and protocol with machinery and machinery with the specific, dehumanizing precision of a system that processed people the way a mill processed grain.

The courtyard was large. The courtyard was full.

Nimue had ordered the army to watch. The order was strategic — the execution of a traitor was a message, and messages required an audience, and the audience was the army, the army that Marcus had sabotaged and weakened and betrayed. The army stood in ranks — eunuch soldiers and human soldiers, the emotionless and the feeling, arranged in the geometric precision of a military formation that was designed to demonstrate order and discipline and the absolute, unquestionable authority of the woman who commanded it.

Nimue was on the platform. The platform was iron — of course it was iron, everything in Greymarch was iron, the metal that was strong and cold and did not bend. Nimue stood on the iron platform and looked down at the courtyard and the army and the prisoner and the sword, and her face was — Marcus observed it with clinical interest — her face was tired.

The tiredness was — Marcus catalogued it. The tiredness of a woman who had built a war machine and watched it eat itself. The tiredness of a woman who had sent an army south and watched it break against walls and wards and the specific, devastating competence of a boy in a study who answered letters and moved pieces and did not sleep. The tiredness of a woman who was executing her chief administrator because her chief administrator had betrayed her, and the betrayal was the thing that had accelerated the collapse of everything she had built, and the collapse was the thing that was consuming her, and the consumption was visible in the lines around her eyes and the grey in her hair and the specific, intimate exhaustion of a ruler who was losing.

Marcus recognized the tiredness. He had seen it before — in Wren's face, in the study, during the meetings where Wren had given him instructions and Marcus had followed them (plus more). The tiredness was the cost of power. The cost that was paid in faces and years and the slow, invisible erosion of the person underneath the ruler.

Nimue spoke. The speech was short — Nimue was always short, always direct, the Witch of Despair who considered eloquence a waste of time and time a waste of life and life a waste of — Marcus wasn't sure what Nimue considered life a waste of. Perhaps nothing. Perhaps that was the despair.

"Marcus. Chief Administrator. Traitor." The words were flat. Not angry. Flat, the way a judge's words are flat — the flatness of authority that does not need emotion to be effective. "You have been found guilty of treason against the crown of Greymarch. The sentence is death. The method is the sword. The execution is immediate."

Marcus looked at Nimue. Nimue looked at Marcus. The looking was — he searched for the word. Recognition. The recognition of a condition. Nimue recognized Marcus because Nimue was, in her own way, as empty as he was. The Witch of Despair. The woman who had built a kingdom on hopelessness and ruled it with the specific, brutal efficiency of a person who had stopped believing that anything mattered and had discovered that the not-mattering was a kind of freedom, and the freedom was a kind of power, and the power was the thing that sustained her.

They were the same. Not in method — but in result. The result was the same: two people looking at each other across a courtyard, across a sword, across the distance between the ruler and the ruled, and seeing in each other the same absence, the same precision, the same clinical awareness of a world that was experienced without being felt.

"Do you have anything to say?" Nimue asked. The asking was procedural. The procedure required the question. The question required an answer.

Marcus considered. The consideration was brief — three seconds, the time it took to process the question and formulate a response and evaluate the response for accuracy and utility. The evaluation concluded: the response was accurate. The response was not useful. The response was the truth, and the truth was the only thing Marcus had left, because everything else — the position, the power, the purpose, the promise of restoration that had been a lie — everything else was gone.

"I remember what this should feel like," Marcus said.

The words landed in the courtyard like stones in water — the impact visible, the ripples spreading, the silence that followed deeper than the silence that had preceded. The eunuch soldiers heard the words and did not react, because reacting was the thing they couldn't do. The human soldiers heard the words and did react — a flinch, a shifting, the specific, uncomfortable movement of people who were hearing something true and wishing they weren't.

Nimue heard the words. Nimue's face — Marcus watched it, catalogued it, filed it — Nimue's face changed. Not dramatically. A flicker. The flicker of a woman who had heard something that reached through the despair and the tiredness and the iron and touched something underneath, something that was still human, something that remembered what feelings were and had chosen to bury them under a kingdom of hopelessness.

The flicker passed. The face returned to iron. The procedure continued.

The executioner stepped forward. The executioner was a eunuch — of course he was, because Nimue understood that an execution performed by a man who could not feel was an execution performed without hesitation, without mercy, without the human weakness that might cause a hand to tremble or a blade to miss.

The sword was drawn. The sword was iron — Greymarch iron, the best iron in the world, forged in the furnaces of a kingdom that had perfected the art of making things that cut. The sword was clean. The sword was sharp. The sword was the instrument of a logic that Marcus respected even as it was applied to him.

Marcus knelt. The kneeling was — he noted the sensation. The stone was cold against his knees. The cold was a fact. The fact was the last fact he would catalogue.

He looked at the sky. The sky was grey — Greymarch grey, the grey of a kingdom that had chosen to exist under clouds, the grey that was not the absence of color but the presence of something else, something that was not quite sadness and not quite nothing but the space between them, the space where despair lived.

The sky was the last thing he saw.

The sword fell.

Marcus observed the falling with clinical interest. The observation lasted — he calculated — approximately one-third of a second. The one-third of a second contained: the sound of the blade cutting air, the sensation of impact (pressure, not pain — pain was an emotion, and emotions were the things he didn't have), and the beginning of a thought that did not complete itself, a thought that started with the word "I" and ended with —

Nothing.

The nothing was not anything, because nothing is the absence of anything, and the absence was the thing Marcus had been living with for years, and the living-with was the practice, and the practice made the nothing — not easy. Not hard. Not anything.

Just nothing.

The courtyard was silent. The army watched. Nimue watched. The sword was red.

And in a study three hundred miles south, a man with warm hands picked up the next letter and did not know — not yet, not for two more days, not until Threadneedle's report arrived at 3 AM — that the man he had recruited and promised and lied to and used and abandoned was dead.

The not-knowing was not mercy. The not-knowing was timing. The timing was the war. The war was the thing that continued.

And Marcus was the thing that stopped.

And the stopping was the only peace he had ever known, if peace was the word for the absence of everything, and the absence was the condition, and the condition was the life, and the life was over.

I remember that this should hurt.

The remembering was the epitaph. The epitaph was the truth. The truth was the thing that Marcus had carried — the only thing, the last thing, the thing that the eunuch process could not remove because the process removed feelings, not memories, and the memory of feeling was the cruelest thing in the world.

The memory of feeling was the thing that made Marcus human.

The humanity was the thing that killed him.

And the killing was the thing that Wren would carry.

And the carrying was the price.

And the price was the face he no longer recognized.
