Chapter 17

The Forged Letters

The forgery took three nights.

Not because the writing was difficult — Wren could replicate any handwriting he'd studied for more than an hour, and he had studied the handwriting of all three witch-queens with the obsessive attention of a man whose survival depended on the precise angle of a letter's crossbar. The difficulty was not the craft. The difficulty was the architecture.

A single forged letter was a lie. A network of forged letters was a reality — a constructed reality, a false world built from paper and ink and the specific, devastating knowledge of how three women thought, what they feared, what they wanted, and what they would believe.

He worked in the study, at the desk, by candlelight. The House kept the candles burning without being asked. The House understood — in the way buildings understand, through the vibration of purpose in the floorboards — that the work being done at the desk was the kind of work that required steady light and steady hands and the absolute absence of interruption.

The first letter was from Morgause to Viviane.

Not the real Morgause — the Morgause that Wren had constructed from two visits to Auremere, from hours of conversation and observation and the intimate, dangerous knowledge that came from kissing a woman who was also your enemy. He knew Morgause's voice the way a musician knows a melody — not just the notes but the spaces between them, the rhythm, the breath.

My dear Viviane — I write to you in confidence, as one queen to another, regarding a matter of mutual concern. The northern situation has become untenable. Nimue's ambitions exceed her capacity, and her recent actions suggest a desperation that threatens us all. I propose a conversation — not an alliance, nothing so formal, merely a conversation between two women who understand that the board has changed and the pieces must be repositioned accordingly. I have taken steps to secure certain... advantages in the east. Steps that I believe you will find interesting, if not immediately beneficial. Shall we meet? The gardens of Auremere are lovely this time of year, and I find that difficult conversations are easier among flowers. — M.

The letter was perfect. The tone was Morgause's — the elegant condescension, the implication wrapped in courtesy. The content was designed to do one thing: make Viviane believe that Morgause was moving against Nimue independently, without Wren's involvement.

The "advantages in the east" was the key phrase. Viviane would interpret it as a reference to Morgause's intelligence network. She would not suspect that the sources were Wren's, that the entire architecture of the scheme was Wren's.

The second letter was from Viviane to Nimue.

This was harder. Viviane's voice was — absence. The Witch of Apathy communicated in the spaces between words, in the things she didn't say, in the specific, devastating eloquence of a woman who had stopped caring about everything except the maintenance of her own indifference. Writing as Viviane required writing less, not more. Required the art of the unsaid.

Nimue — Morgause is moving. You know this. I know this. The question is not whether she moves but where, and the answer is: toward you. She has assets in your territory that you have not identified. She has plans that you have not anticipated. I tell you this not from alliance but from pragmatism — your fall benefits no one, least of all me, and I prefer a board with three players to a board with two. Do with this what you will. — V.

Shorter. Colder. The tone of a woman who was doing you a favor and wanted you to know that the favor cost her nothing.

The content was poison. It told Nimue that Morgause had spies inside Greymarch — which was true, but the spies were Wren's, not Morgause's. It told Nimue that Morgause was planning something — which was true, but the planning was Wren's, not Morgause's. It redirected Nimue's suspicion away from the real threat (Marcus, Wren's agent inside her military) and toward a false threat (Morgause's nonexistent intelligence network inside Greymarch).

The third letter was from Nimue to Morgause.

This was the most dangerous forgery, because Nimue's voice was the hardest to replicate. Nimue was direct. Nimue was honest. Nimue said what she meant and meant what she said, and the honesty was the thing that made her predictable and the predictability was the thing that made her vulnerable, but the honesty also meant that any forgery had to be brutally, uncomfortably direct, because a subtle Nimue was a false Nimue, and Morgause would spot the falseness instantly.

Morgause — I know about your agent in my court. I know about the intelligence you've been gathering. I know about the "advantages" you've been securing. You think you're clever. You think the golden court and the silk letters and the perfumed diplomacy make you untouchable. They don't. I am coming south. Not for the apprentice's kingdom — for yours. The apprentice is a distraction. You are the threat. Prepare accordingly. — N.

Direct. Brutal. Honest in the way that Nimue was honest — the honesty of a woman who considered deception a weakness and confrontation a virtue. The letter told Morgause that Nimue knew about her schemes (she didn't — the schemes were Wren's). The letter told Morgause that Nimue was redirecting her military south, toward Auremere (she wasn't — she was committed to the eastern assault on Thornhold). The letter told Morgause to prepare for war (there was no war coming — the war was Wren's invention, a phantom threat designed to make Morgause defensive, to make her pull her intelligence assets back to Auremere, to make her focus inward instead of outward).

Three letters. Three lies. Three realities constructed from paper and ink and the intimate knowledge of three women who trusted — or at least respected — the man who was betraying all of them simultaneously.

Wren sealed the letters. Each seal was different — Morgause's golden wax, Viviane's black wax, Nimue's iron-grey wax. He had sourced the wax months ago, through Threadneedle's network, from craftsmen who supplied the witch-queens' courts. The seals were perfect. The paper was perfect. The ink was perfect. The lies were perfect.

The perfection was the thing that should have bothered him. The ease with which he constructed false realities — the fluency, the craft, the almost artistic satisfaction of a forgery so complete that even the forger could admire it — this should have been a warning. The boy who had arrived at Thornhold with warm hands and honest eyes had been replaced by something that could build lies the way a carpenter builds furniture, with skill and pride and no particular concern for what the furniture would be used for.

It didn't bother him. That was the warning.

* * *

He dispatched the letters through three separate channels.

The Morgause-to-Viviane letter went through a merchant caravan that traded between Auremere and Hollowmere — a caravan that Threadneedle had identified as a regular courier route for the witch-queens' correspondence. The letter would be "intercepted" by Viviane's agents, who would believe they had captured genuine intelligence. The interception was staged — the merchant carrying the letter had been paid to be careless, to leave his saddlebag unattended at a specific inn on a specific night, where a specific agent of Viviane's would find it.

The Viviane-to-Nimue letter went through a different channel — a deserter from Viviane's network, a man named Aldren who had been turned by Wren's agents months ago and who now served as a double agent, feeding Nimue's counter-intelligence service with a carefully curated mixture of truth and lies. Aldren would deliver the letter directly to Nimue's intelligence chief, claiming he had stolen it from Viviane's courier. The claim would be believed because Aldren had established his credibility over months of accurate (Wren-approved) intelligence drops.

The Nimue-to-Morgause letter went through the most elaborate channel — a captured eunuch soldier, one of the prisoners from the Ashenmere assault, who would be "allowed to escape" from Thornhold's custody carrying the letter hidden in his boot. The eunuch would make his way east, toward Nimue's lines, but would be intercepted by Morgause's border patrols — patrols that Wren knew the location of because Morgause had told him, during their last meeting, in the intimate, unguarded conversation that followed the kiss that was both real and strategic.

The interception would look natural. The letter would look genuine. Morgause would read it and believe that Nimue was planning to attack Auremere. Morgause would respond by fortifying her borders, pulling her intelligence assets back from Thornhold, reducing her pressure on Wren's kingdom. The reduction would give Wren room to maneuver — room to focus on the real threat, which was Nimue's army on the eastern border, without worrying about Morgause's schemes in the west.

Three letters. Three channels. Three lies that would, if the architecture held, turn the three witch-queens against each other and buy Wren the time he needed to survive the war.

The architecture would hold. Wren had built it over two years. The architecture was his masterpiece. The masterpiece was also his greatest betrayal, because the architecture was built on trust — Morgause's trust in their relationship, Marcus's trust in the promise of restoration, Viviane's trust in her own network's security — and trust, once spent, could not be earned back.

He didn't think about the trust. He thought about the bridge at Thornfield, where Aldric's soldiers were holding the line against fifteen hundred eunuch soldiers who didn't tire and didn't stop and didn't feel. He thought about the fourteen names — the soldiers who had died at Ashenmere, whose names he had learned and filed and would carry. He thought about the next fourteen, and the fourteen after that, and the mathematics of a war that was measured in names.

The letters were dispatched. The lies were in motion. The architecture was holding.

Wren picked up the next letter — a real letter, a trade dispute, a wool tariff — and answered it. The answering was the routine. The routine was the prison. The prison was the job.

The warmth burned. The fuel was trust.

And trust, like youth, was a thing you spent once.
