Chapter 3

The Kitchen Girl

Elena had flour dusted her cheek and didn't know it.

This was not unusual. Elena had flour on some part of her body at all times — an occupational hazard of working in a kitchen that had opinions about bread and expressed those opinions by requiring bread to be made constantly, in varieties that ranged from the practical (wheat, rye, sourdough) to the philosophical (a loaf that tasted like the memory of your grandmother's kitchen, which the House's kitchen produced on Thursdays and which made everyone who ate it quietly homesick for places they'd never been).

She had been working in the House for two years. Before that, she had worked in the village bakery. Before that, she had worked on her family's farm, two days' ride south, where her parents grew vegetables and kept chickens and her younger brother, Tam, told jokes so terrible that the chickens had developed a visible flinch response.

She had come to the House because the farm couldn't support four people and the bakery paid better and the House paid better still. The House paid in gold and in meals and in the specific, unquantifiable currency of being part of something larger than a vegetable garden — though Elena would never have said this, because Elena did not think in terms of larger things. Elena thought in terms of bread and flour and whether the butter had gone off and whether Tam had remembered to feed the chickens and whether her mother's back was still hurting and whether the new apprentice — the thin, quiet boy who had appeared a month ago and had not stopped working since — was eating enough.

He was not eating enough.

She knew this the way she knew most things: by paying attention. The trays she brought to the study came back with food half-eaten or untouched. The meals she left outside his door at midnight — because he was always working at midnight, the light under his door burning like a small, stubborn star — were sometimes still there in the morning, cold and congealed and sad in the way that uneaten food is always sad.

She mentioned this to the kitchen. The kitchen — which was, like all parts of the House, alive and opinionated — responded by making the food more appealing. Richer soups. Fresher bread. A honey cake that the kitchen was particularly proud of and that it placed on the tray with the careful positioning of a gift.

The honey cake came back untouched.

The kitchen was offended. Elena was worried. There is a difference between a person who doesn't eat because they're not hungry and a person who doesn't eat because they've forgotten that eating is something bodies require. The first is temporary. The second is a symptom.

On a Tuesday morning — Tuesdays were always significant, though Elena didn't know why and didn't particularly care — she brought the breakfast tray to the study herself instead of leaving it outside the door.

She knocked. No answer. She knocked again. The House, helpfully, opened the door.

The study was a war room.

Maps covered every wall. Letters were pinned to a board in clusters — color-coded, she realized, by kingdom. Red for Thornhold. Black for the three witch territories. Blue for something she didn't recognize — trade routes, maybe, or spy networks, or the kind of information that existed in the space between what people said and what they meant.

The desk was buried under correspondence. Three candles burned — the House had given up on growing new ones and had simply made the existing ones burn indefinitely, which gave the room a strange, timeless quality, as if morning and midnight had agreed to coexist.

Wren was asleep at the desk. His head on his arms, his face turned to the side, his mouth slightly open. Asleep, he looked his age. Seventeen. A boy. The lines beginning to form around his eyes were smoothed by sleep into something younger, softer, more like the person he might have been if the person he was becoming hadn't gotten in the way.

His hands were warm. She could feel it from across the room — a gentle heat, like a banked fire, like the kitchen stove on a cold morning. The warmth was the thing that had first made her notice him, weeks ago, when he'd passed through the kitchen on his way to the basement and the air around him had been warm and she'd thought: that's not normal and then: nothing in this house is normal and then: he looks tired and then she'd stopped thinking about it because the bread needed turning.

She set the tray on the corner of the desk. Quietly. The way you set things down near sleeping people — with the specific care of someone who understands that sleep is precious and interrupting it is a small violence.

He woke anyway. The way he woke told her something: instantly, completely, with a sharp intake of breath and a hand that moved — fast, instinctive — toward the bread knife he kept in the desk drawer. The waking of a person who has trained themselves to surface from sleep like a soldier, ready for the thing that's coming.

Then he saw her. The hand relaxed. The breath slowed.

"Breakfast," Elena said. "You didn't eat dinner. Or lunch. Or yesterday's dinner. The kitchen is taking it personally."

He blinked. Looked at the tray. Looked at her. The look was — she would think about this later, turning it over the way you turn over a stone to see what's underneath — the look was confusion. Not who are you confusion. Why are you here confusion. The confusion of a person who has been so deep inside their own head for so long that the existence of other people has become surprising.

"Thank you," he said. His voice was rough with sleep. "I'm sorry about the kitchen. I've been—"

"Busy. I know. Everyone knows. The House talks." She paused. "Not in words. In — temperatures. Creaks. The House has been warmer since you arrived. Happier, if a house can be happy. But the kitchen says you're not eating, and the kitchen is the heart of the House, and if the heart is worried, the whole building gets anxious, and when the building gets anxious, the plumbing does strange things, and I would rather not explain to Merlin why the bathtub is filling with soup again."

He almost smiled. The almost was the crack in the mask — underneath it, a boy who was tired and hungry and had been working for a month without stopping and had forgotten, somewhere in the letters and the maps and the three-dimensional chess game he was playing against three witch-queens, that he was a person who needed to eat.

"The bathtub filled with soup?" he said.

"Last winter. Merlin hadn't eaten in three days. The House got creative. Tomato soup. In the bathtub. Merlin was not pleased. The House was not sorry." She pushed the tray closer. "Eat. Please. The honey cake is the kitchen's best work and if you send it back again, I think the stove might cry, and a crying stove is a fire hazard."

He ate. Not quickly — slowly, as if remembering how. The bread first, then the soup, then the honey cake. He ate the honey cake with his eyes closed, and something in his face changed — the lines softened, the jaw unclenched, and for a moment he was not the shadow ruler of Thornhold but a seventeen-year-old boy eating something sweet and finding, in the sweetness, a small and temporary peace.

"This is good," he said. Surprised. As if goodness were unexpected.

"The kitchen will be pleased." She collected the empty tray. Paused at the door. "I'm Elena. I work in the kitchen. I bring your meals. You've never asked my name."

It was not an accusation. It was a fact, delivered with the same matter-of-fact warmth with which she delivered bread — here is a thing that is true; do with it what you will.

He looked at her. Really looked, for the first time. Brown hair tied back. Flour dusted her cheek. Callused hands. An apron that had been washed so many times the pattern had faded to a ghost of itself. Pretty in the way that real people are pretty — not the calculated beauty of Morgause's court, which he had been studying in Threadneedle's reports, but the beauty of a person who is simply, uncomplicatedly, present.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I'm Wren."

"I know. The House told me. In temperatures." She smiled. It was a good smile — warm, uncomplicated, the smile of a person who means exactly what their face is doing. "Eat your meals, Wren. The kingdom can wait until after breakfast."

She left. The door closed behind her. The study was quiet.

Wren sat at his desk. The maps stared at him from the walls. The letters waited. The three witch-queens plotted in their black territories and the kingdom trembled in its red borders and the most powerful sorcerer in the world snored in the next room.

But for a moment — just a moment, just the space between one breath and the next — Wren thought about flour on a nose. About honey cake. About a smile that meant exactly what it looked like.

Then he picked up the next letter. And the moment passed. And the work continued.

And the warmth in his hands burned on. And the boy grew older. And the flour on her cheek was the last simple thing he would notice for a very long time.

* * *

That evening, Wren met Captain Aldric.

The captain was waiting in the courtyard — a square of packed earth between the House and the outer wall, where the garrison drilled and the horses were kept and the practical, unglamorous business of maintaining a military presence was conducted by people who did not have magic and did not want it and considered the whole enterprise of sorcery to be, at best, unreliable and, at worst, an excuse for the people in charge to avoid doing actual work.

Aldric was one of these people. Forty-three years old. Built like a wall — not tall but wide, with shoulders that suggested a lifetime of carrying things heavier than they looked. His face was weathered, scarred, the face of a man who had been hit many times and had developed the habit of hitting back harder. His eyes were the color of iron and approximately as warm.

He was sharpening a sword when Wren found him. The sharpening was meditative — long, slow strokes, the blade singing against the stone. He did not look up when Wren approached.

"You're the new apprentice," Aldric said. Not a question.

"I am."

"The last one lasted eight months. The one before that, four. The one before that—" He paused. The sharpening stopped. "The one before that lasted two years. She was good. She left because Merlin broke something she couldn't fix and she decided that fixing things for a man who keeps breaking them is not a career, it's a condition."

"I'm not here to fix Merlin."

"No?" The iron eyes looked up. Assessed. "Then what are you here for?"

"The kingdom. Someone has to run it."

Aldric studied him. The sharpening resumed — slower now, thoughtful.

"You've been answering the correspondence," he said. "I know because the garrison received its pay for the first time in seven months. My soldiers were beginning to wonder if they were volunteers."

"They're not volunteers. They're the only standing military force between three witch-queens and several thousand civilians. They should be paid."

"They should be paid more. They should also be better equipped, better trained, and more numerous. We have forty soldiers. Nimue has four thousand. Morgause has two thousand and a network of assassins. Viviane has — no one knows what Viviane has, which is the problem."

"I know. I've read the reports."

"Reports." Aldric said the word the way a carpenter says "blueprints" — acknowledging their existence while making clear that the real work happens elsewhere. "Reports tell you numbers. Numbers don't bleed. I've been defending this kingdom with forty men and a wizard who won't get out of bed. I've been doing it for fifteen years. I've buried six soldiers in that time — not from war, from the slow attrition of being undermanned and underfunded and ignored by the man whose name is supposed to protect us."

He set down the sword. Stood. He was shorter than Wren had expected — the authority he carried made him seem larger.

"You want to run the kingdom," Aldric said. "Fine. Run it. But understand what you're running. This is not a kingdom. This is a bluff. Merlin is the bluff. His power is real but his willingness to use it is not, and the only reason the witch-queens haven't called the bluff is that calling it wrong means annihilation. They're afraid of what he might do. Not what he will do. The 'might' is the only thing keeping us alive."

"Then we need to make the 'might' more convincing."

"How?"

"By being competent. By responding to threats. By showing that someone in Thornhold is paying attention, even if it's not Merlin. The witch-queens don't need to believe Merlin is active. They need to believe the kingdom is functional. A functional kingdom with a sleeping dragon is more dangerous than a dysfunctional one with a waking one — because a functional kingdom means someone is making decisions, and decisions are unpredictable, and unpredictability is what keeps enemies cautious."

Aldric was quiet for a long time. The courtyard was quiet. The horses shifted in their stalls. Somewhere in the House, Merlin was singing badly about Margaret and the goat.

Aldric studied him with the iron eyes of a man assessing a battlefield. "You're talking about managing three witch-queens through strategic competence. That's not something an apprentice should know how to do."

"And yet."

Another silence. Then Aldric did something unexpected. He extended his hand.

"Captain Aldric. Garrison commander. Fifteen years of service to a kingdom that doesn't deserve it and a wizard who doesn't notice it." He gripped Wren's hand — firm, calloused, the grip of a man who has decided something. "If you're going to run this kingdom, you'll need someone who knows where the bodies are buried. Some of them literally."

Wren shook his hand. The warmth from his palm met the iron of Aldric's grip, and something passed between them — not magic, not friendship, but the recognition of two people who have looked at the same impossible situation and decided, independently, to do something about it.

"I need to know everything," Wren said. "The garrison's capabilities. The defensive positions. The supply situation. The morale. Everything you've been managing alone for fifteen years."

"That'll take a while."

"I have time."

Aldric almost smiled. The almost was different from Elena's almost — harder, more weathered, the almost-smile of a man who has forgotten how to smile fully and is surprised to find the muscles still work.

"No," he said. "You don't. But we'll pretend you do. It's what we've been doing for fifteen years. Pretending is the kingdom's primary export."

They talked until midnight. Aldric laid out the garrison's situation with the brutal honesty of a man who has been lying to himself for years and has decided to stop. Forty soldiers. Twenty of them competent. Ten of them good. Three of them exceptional. Equipment that was fifteen years old and held together with prayer and pig iron. A defensive wall that had been designed for a garrison of two hundred and was manned by forty. Supply lines that depended on trade routes that depended on Merlin's reputation that depended on a man who was currently asleep in a chair with mead on his breath.

Wren listened. Asked questions. Took notes. Filed everything in the architecture of a mind that was growing, daily, into something larger and more complex than a seventeen-year-old's mind should contain.

By the time Aldric left, Wren had a clearer picture of the kingdom's military situation.

It was worse than he'd thought.

He went back to the study. Sat at the desk. Looked at the maps.

Three witch-queens. Forty soldiers. A sleeping sorcerer. A sentient house. A treasury that was barely solvent. An intelligence network run by a grandmother with knitting needles. And a seventeen-year-old boy with warm hands and a small, steady flame that was already burning hotter than it should, consuming fuel it couldn't afford to spend.

He picked up a pen. Began to write.

Not letters this time. Plans. Contingencies. The skeleton of a strategy that would take months to build and years to execute — if he had years, which he didn't, because Nimue was moving and Morgause was watching and Viviane was doing whatever Viviane did, which was nothing, which was the most dangerous thing of all.

The candle burned. The House breathed. The warmth in his hands consumed.

And in the kitchen, Elena washed the dishes from dinner and hummed a song her mother used to sing and did not think about maps or witch-queens or the thin boy in the study who had eaten the honey cake with his eyes closed.

She thought about flour. And bread. And whether Tam had remembered to feed the chickens.

The simplicity was not ignorance. It was choice. The choice to live in the world as it was — small, immediate, full of bread and flour and chickens — rather than the world as it might become, which was large and dark and full of things that Elena could not control and did not wish to.

She dried the last dish. Hung up her apron. Went to her small room on the ground floor — smaller than Wren's, because the House allocated space based on perceived importance, and the House did not yet understand that the person who feeds the kingdom is as important as the person who runs it.

She slept easily.

Above her, the light in the study burned on.
