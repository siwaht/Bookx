Chapter 10

The First Blood

The spy's name was Corwin.

Wren knew this because Threadneedle's file on Corwin was thorough — three pages of careful, precise intelligence covering his background (minor noble from Hollowmere, third son, no inheritance), his recruitment (Viviane's network, approached through a gambling debt, turned through a combination of blackmail and ideology), his placement (clerk in Thornhold's trade office, with access to shipping manifests, tariff records, and the movement schedules of every merchant caravan entering or leaving the kingdom), and his handler (unknown, likely operating through dead drops in the village market).

Wren also knew Corwin's breakfast habits (porridge with honey, every morning, at the same table in the same tavern), his evening routine (a walk along the eastern wall, ostensibly for exercise, actually to check a dead drop behind the third buttress), and his one personal weakness (a woman in the village, a seamstress named Dora, whom Corwin visited on Thursdays and who did not know that the man who brought her flowers was selling her kingdom's secrets to a witch-queen who embodied the absence of caring).

Threadneedle had identified Corwin six weeks ago. She had watched him for four. She had confirmed the dead drop, traced two of his reports (shipping manifests, both accurate, both damaging — Viviane's network now knew the schedule of every supply caravan on the eastern route), and identified the pattern: Corwin reported weekly, on Wednesdays, through the dead drop behind the buttress.

The file sat on Wren's desk. He had read it three times. Each reading was the same — clinical, thorough, the reading of a person who processes information the way a machine processes raw material, converting it into something useful.

The useful thing was a decision.

Corwin had to be removed. Not arrested — arrest meant a trial, exposure, and Viviane's network would know their agent was compromised. They would pull their other assets. Threadneedle would lose the threads she was following. And operating blind against Viviane — the Witch of Apathy — was suicide.

Corwin had to be removed quietly. Permanently. In a way that looked like something other than what it was.

Wren sat at the desk. The file was open. The candle burned. The House was quiet — the 3 AM quiet, the quiet of a building that was holding its breath because it could feel the weight of the decision its keeper was making and the weight was different from the usual weight, heavier, darker, the weight of a line that, once crossed, could not be uncrossed.

He picked up a pen. Wrote a note. Three words.

Silke. Corwin. Accident.

He sealed the note. Left it on the desk. Went to bed.

He did not sleep.

* * *

Silke collected the note the next morning. She read it. Nodded. Said nothing. The nothing was professional — the nothing of a person who has received an order and will execute it and does not require discussion, because discussion implies doubt, and doubt is a luxury that professionals in Silke's line of work cannot afford.

She left the House. The front door closed behind her. The House's temperature did not change. The House had no opinion about assassination — or if it did, the opinion was buried so deep in the foundation that it registered not as temperature but as a faint, subsonic vibration, the vibration of a building that has felt something shift in its structure and is not sure whether the shift is damage or settling.

Wren went to the study. Sat at the desk. Opened the next letter. The next letter was from the Merchant Guild — a dispute about tariff rates on the southern route. He read it. Drafted a response. The response was precise, fair, balanced. The response was the work of a competent administrator managing a trade dispute.

The response was also the work of a man who had, thirty minutes ago, ordered another man's death, and was now writing about tariff rates, and the writing about tariff rates was not a distraction from the ordering of death but a continuation of the same process — the process of managing a kingdom, which included trade disputes and intelligence operations and, now, assassination, and the inclusion was seamless, and the seamlessness was the thing that should have frightened him and didn't.

* * *

Corwin died on a Thursday.

The death was an accident. A fall from the eastern wall — the wall Corwin walked every evening, the wall where the dead drop was hidden. The wall was old. The stones were loose in places. A man walking at dusk, distracted, not watching his footing — a loose stone, a stumble, a fall. Twenty feet to the cobblestones below. The fall was survivable. The landing was not.

The village constable investigated. The investigation was brief — loose stones, poor lighting, a man who walked the same route every evening and had grown careless with familiarity. Accidental death. The constable filed a report. The report was filed in the village records. The village records were stored in a cabinet that no one opened and no one read and the not-reading was the system working as designed.

Dora, the seamstress, cried. She cried for a week. She cried in her shop, over her sewing, the tears falling on fabric that would become someone's dress, and the tears would be sewn into the seams, and the dress would carry the grief of a woman who had loved a man who had brought her flowers and sold her kingdom's secrets and died on a Thursday because a boy in a study had written three words on a piece of paper.

Wren read Silke's report. The report was brief — one page, no details, just the confirmation: Done. Clean. No complications.

He set the report down. Picked up the next letter. The next letter was from Elena's mother — a note, delivered through the village post, thanking Wren for the supply route improvements that had made it easier for the farming villages to get their produce to market. The note was warm. The note was grateful. The note was written by a woman who did not know that the person she was thanking had ordered a man's death that morning and was reading her thank-you note that afternoon and the reading was the same process as the ordering — the same desk, the same pen, the same hands.

The hands were warm. The hands were always warm.

He filed the report. Filed the letter. Picked up the next piece of correspondence.

The filing was the thing. The filing was the mechanism by which the ordering of a man's death became equivalent to the reading of a thank-you note — both inputs in the same system, both processed by the same mind, both filed in the same architecture. The architecture did not distinguish between trade disputes and assassinations. The architecture processed everything with the same clinical efficiency, because efficiency was the only way to manage the volume, and the volume was the kingdom, and the kingdom was everything.

* * *

That night, Wren went to the kitchen.

For the table. The old table with the knife marks and the burn rings and the centuries of meals. He sat at the table in the dark. Put his hands on the wood. Let the warmth seep into the scars.

He thought about Corwin. Not about the death — about the man. The file said Corwin was thirty-two. Third son. No inheritance. Recruited through a gambling debt. The gambling debt was the lever — the thing that Viviane's network had used to turn a minor noble into a spy. The debt was the weakness. The weakness was the entry point. The entry point was the thing that made Corwin useful to Viviane and dangerous to Thornhold and dead on a Thursday.

Thirty-two. A gambling debt. A woman who brought him flowers — no, he brought her flowers. Dora. The seamstress. Thursdays.

Wren thought about Dora. About the crying. About the tears on the fabric. About the dress that would carry the grief.

He thought: I should feel something.

The thought was clinical. The thought was the thought of a person who has noticed the absence of a feeling and is examining the absence the way a doctor examines a wound — not with emotion but with professional interest. The absence was notable. The absence was a symptom. The symptom pointed to a condition.

He should feel guilt. He had ordered a man's death. The man was a spy, yes. The man was a threat, yes. The removal was necessary, yes. But the man was also a person — a person with a breakfast routine and an evening walk and a woman who loved him and a gambling debt that had been the crack through which the cold wind of espionage had entered his life. The man was a person and Wren had ordered his death and the ordering had been three words on a piece of paper and the three words had been written with the same pen and the same hand that wrote tariff responses and trade agreements and letters to Elena's mother.

He should feel guilt. He felt nothing.

A different nothing from Marcus's surgical void. The nothing of a person who has been processing too much for too long and has, without noticing, developed the capacity to process anything — including death, including murder, including the specific, terrible efficiency of converting a human life into a filed report.

The nothing was not permanent. He knew this. The nothing was a defense — the mind's way of protecting itself from the weight of what it had done, the way a circuit breaker trips to prevent overload. The feeling would come later. The feeling always came later — at 3 AM, in the dark, in the moments between sleep and waking when the defenses were down and the filed reports opened themselves and the contents spilled out and the contents were not data but faces, and the faces were not abstractions but people, and the people were dead.

But tonight, the nothing held. Tonight, the circuit breaker was tripped. Tonight, Wren sat at the kitchen table in the dark and felt nothing and the nothing was a mercy and the mercy was temporary and the temporary was all he had.

The kitchen was dark. The stove was banked. The bread rose in its bowls.

Elena was asleep. The House was quiet. The kingdom was safe.

A man was dead. A woman was crying. A report was filed.

And the pilot light burned. And the fuel was something new — not youth, not sleep, not integrity. The fuel was the capacity to feel the weight of what he'd done. The capacity was finite. The capacity was shrinking. And when the capacity was gone — when the nothing became permanent, when the circuit breaker stopped resetting, when the filed reports stayed filed and the faces stayed abstractions and the people stayed data — when that happened, Wren would be something else. Something harder. Something more efficient. Something less human.

Something like Marcus.

The thought surfaced and submerged. Filed. Processed. Stored.

He went to bed. He slept. The sleep was dreamless.

He had expected to dream of Corwin. Of Dora. Of the tears on the fabric. He had expected the night to be the thing that broke through the defenses, the way nights always broke through defenses — the 3 AM ambush of a conscience that had been held at bay all day by the work.

There was nothing. Just dark. Just the absence of Corwin, the absence of Dora, the absence of the weight he had expected to feel.

The dreamlessness was the first sign.

He did not recognize it as such. Not yet.
