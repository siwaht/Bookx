Chapter 40

Merlin's Morning

Merlin woke at dawn.

This was, in itself, remarkable. Merlin had not woken at dawn in — he searched his memory, which was vast and unreliable and organized according to a system that only made sense to a mind that had been accumulating information for a thousand years — he had not woken at dawn in decades. Possibly centuries. Dawn was for farmers and soldiers and people who had things to do, and Merlin had not had things to do since the fall of Camelot, which was a long time ago by anyone's reckoning and an especially long time ago by the reckoning of a man who had been there and remembered it with the specific, weary clarity of a person who had watched something beautiful destroy itself and had decided, in the aftermath, that beautiful things were not worth the investment.

But he woke at dawn. Both eyes open. Both eyes seeing.

The grey eye — the eye that saw truth, that saw through illusion, that saw the bones of things beneath their skin — the grey eye saw the House. The House was — wrong. The wrong of a building that was grieving.

The blue eye — the eye that felt, that absorbed emotion, that was the reason Merlin drank memory-mead and slept until noon and cultivated indifference the way other men cultivated gardens — the blue eye felt the grief. The grief was structural. The grief was in the walls and the floors and the pipes and the temperature, which was wrong — too warm in some places, too cold in others, the thermal equivalent of a person who cannot stop shivering because the shivering is not about temperature but about loss.

Merlin sat up. The sitting was — unusual. The sitting required effort, not physical effort but the effort of engagement, the effort of a man who had spent decades in the horizontal position choosing, for reasons he could not yet articulate, to become vertical.

He stood. The standing was worse. The standing required commitment. The standing said: I am here. I am present. I am going to walk to the door and open it and enter the world and deal with whatever the world has decided to present.

He walked to the door. Opened it. The hallway was — the hallway was wrong. The wrongness was specific: the temperature was fluctuating, the lights were dim, the walls were — he touched one — the walls were cold. The House's walls were never cold. The House's walls were the House's skin, and the skin was always warm, because warmth was the House's language of love, and the House loved its inhabitants the way a body loves its organs: automatically, constantly, without the option of stopping.

The walls were cold. The House had stopped.

Not stopped functioning — the wards were holding, the furnace was burning, the structural systems were intact. Stopped caring. The stopping was temporary — the House was too old and too alive to stop caring permanently — but the temporary stopping was the grief, and the grief was the thing that had woken Merlin, because the House's grief was loud in the way that only structural grief can be loud: not with sound but with absence, the absence of warmth, the absence of the constant, background hum of a building that was paying attention.

The House was not paying attention. The House was looking inward. The House was mourning.

Merlin walked down the hallway. Past the bathroom — the mirror was dark, showing nothing, not even reflections. Past the kitchen — empty, the stove cold, the bread bowls sitting on the counter with dough that had risen and fallen and risen again, unattended. Past the sitting room — his chair, his mead, his fire, all exactly as he had left them, untouched, unchanged, the still life of a man's indifference preserved in furniture and alcohol.

He reached the study.

The study door was closed. The study door was always closed — the study was Wren's domain, the room that Merlin had never used and had signed away on documents he thought were grocery lists and had not entered in six years because entering would have required acknowledging what was happening inside, and acknowledging what was happening inside would have required caring, and caring was the thing Merlin had decided, centuries ago, was too expensive.

The door was closed. The door was also — he touched it — the door was warm. Not House-warm. A different warm. The residual warmth of a room that had been occupied by a person with warm hands for six years, the warmth soaked into the wood the way memory soaks into stone, persistent, indelible, the thermal ghost of a presence that was no longer present.

He opened the door.

* * *

The study was covered in blood.

The blood was on the desk — splashed across the surface, pooling in the grain of the wood, staining the papers that were still stacked in neat piles because even in the simulation of violence, Wren had been organized. The blood was on the floor — a trail leading from the desk to the window, the trail of a body being dragged, the narrative of a struggle written in red on stone. The blood was on the window frame — the window was broken, the glass shattered outward, the point of exit, the place where the body had been taken.

The bread knife was embedded in the desk. The bread knife — the knife that Wren had carried since he arrived, the knife his mother had given him, the knife that had been a comfort and a weapon and a reminder of a life that existed before the kingdom consumed him. The knife was in the desk, blade-deep, as if wrenched from a hand during a fight, as if the last act of a dying man had been to drive his only weapon into the wood.

The manual was on the desk. Four hundred pages, centered, untouched by the blood — because the blood had been placed around it, not on it, because the manual was the thing that mattered, and Wren had protected it even in the staging of his own death.

Merlin looked at the blood. The grey eye saw the truth immediately — the truth it always saw, the truth it could not help seeing, the truth that was the grey eye's gift and curse. The blood was pig's blood. The viscosity was wrong. The color was wrong — too bright, too uniform, lacking the specific, terrible darkness of human blood that has been exposed to air. The splatter pattern was wrong — too even, too deliberate, the work of a man who had studied forensics in the House's library and had applied the knowledge with the precision of an administrator, not the chaos of a murder.

The death was staged. The apprentice was alive.

The grey eye saw this. The grey eye had always seen this — had seen it coming, the way it saw everything coming, with the patient, inevitable clarity of an eye that had been watching the world for a thousand years and had learned to read the patterns of human behavior the way other people read books. The grey eye had seen the boy arrive. Had seen the boy become the keeper. Had seen the keeper become the shadow ruler. Had seen the shadow ruler become the stranger in the mirror. Had seen the stranger decide to leave.

The grey eye had seen all of it. The grey eye had said nothing. The grey eye was Merlin's eye, and Merlin's policy on saying things was well established.

The blue eye — the blue eye felt something.

The feeling was — Merlin examined it with the clinical curiosity of a man who had not felt anything genuine in decades and was now, unexpectedly, feeling something and was not sure what to do with it. The feeling was — he searched his vast, unreliable, thousand-year-old vocabulary for the word — the feeling was shame.

Shame. The word landed like a stone in still water. Shame — the specific, intimate, corrosive shame of a man who could have helped and didn't. Who could have carried the weight and let it fall. Who could have been what the kingdom needed and chose to be what he wanted. The shame of a thousand-year-old being who had watched a boy burn himself alive to keep a kingdom warm and had not, at any point, in any way, lifted a finger to help.

The shame was — Merlin sat down. Not in Wren's chair — he would not sit in Wren's chair, the chair that had held the boy for six years, the chair that was shaped by a body that was not his. He sat on the floor. The floor was cold. The cold was the House's grief, and the grief was — Merlin understood now — the grief was for him as much as for Wren. The House was grieving the keeper it had lost. The House was also grieving the builder who had never built, the sorcerer who had never sorcered, the man who had sat in a chair and drunk mead and slept while a boy did the work.

The House was grieving Merlin's failure. The failure was a thousand years old. The failure was also six years old. The failure was the same failure, repeated, the failure of a man who had the power to change things and the apathy to not bother.

* * *

He found the manual.

He picked it up. Four hundred pages. The weight of a kingdom in ink and paper — the weight that a boy had carried for six years and had finally, at the cost of his youth and his face in the mirror, put down.

The weight was in his hands now.

He opened the manual. Page one. The handwriting was small, precise, legible — the handwriting of a man who understood that clarity was not a courtesy but a necessity. The first section: the wards. The ward anchors. The maintenance schedules. The failure modes. The furnace. The feeding schedule. The fuel sources. The warnings.

He read. The reading was slow — not because the writing was difficult but because the reading was an education, and the education was the thing Merlin had been avoiding for six years. He read about the intelligence network. About Threadneedle and the cell system and the cipher. He read about the military — Aldric's garrison, the training, the contingency plans. He read about the finances — the treasury, the debts, Mr. Finch. He read about the court.

He read about himself.

Merlin is the most powerful sorcerer alive. Merlin will not help you. Do not expect him to. Do not ask him to. Do not waste your time hating him for it. The hatred is a luxury you cannot afford, and the luxury will consume you the way it consumed me. He is what he is. Work around him. Use his reputation. Maintain the illusion. The illusion is the kingdom's primary defense. The illusion is also the kingdom's primary prison. You will learn to live in both.

Merlin read the passage three times. The grey eye saw the clinical, devastating truth of a man describing his captor to the next prisoner. The blue eye felt the shame — the shame that was becoming familiar, the shame that was becoming motivation.

Motivation. The word was foreign. Motivation was for people who did things, and Merlin had not done things in a very long time. But the boy had done things. The boy had cared enough to carry a kingdom for six years. The boy had cared enough to write a four-hundred-page manual so that the kingdom would survive his leaving. The boy had cared enough to stage his own death so that the leaving would be clean.

The boy had cared. And the caring had cost him everything. And the cost was Merlin's fault — not entirely, but enough. Enough for the shame. Enough for the motivation.

He turned to the last page. The last page — the page Wren had left for the next keeper. The page with the single paragraph:

The job will try to become you. Don't let it. The kingdom needs a keeper, not a sacrifice. Be better than I was. Be smarter. Be kinder. And when the time comes — and it will come — leave. Leave before the mirror shows you a stranger. Leave before the pilot light eats the last of your fuel. Leave while you still remember what your hands were for before they learned to hold the weight.

— W.

Merlin closed the manual. Set it on the desk. Placed his hands — both hands, the hands that could unmake reality — flat on the surface, beside the pig's blood, beside the bread knife, beside the evidence of a boy's escape.

"All right," he said. To the study. To the House. To the kingdom. To the ghost of a boy who was not dead but was gone, which was worse than dead because the gone was a choice, and the choice was a judgment, and the judgment was correct.

"All right," he said again. A beginning. The first word of a man who had spent a thousand years ending things and was now, at the prompting of a four-hundred-page manual and a staged death and the shame of a blue eye that would not stop feeling, beginning something.

He picked up the first letter on the desk. A trade dispute. A border village. A fence — an actual fence, not a metaphorical one — that two farmers were arguing about.

He read the letter. He picked up the pen — Wren's pen, the pen that had written a kingdom into existence. He began to write a response.

The response was terrible. The handwriting was sprawling, the language was imprecise, the solution was — he read it back — the solution was wrong. The solution was the solution of a man who had not solved a problem in decades and had forgotten how problems worked.

He crossed it out. Started again. The second attempt was better. Not good — not Wren-good, not the precise, elegant, minimum-necessary-force good that had been the boy's signature. But better. Functional. The response of a man who was trying.

The trying was — the trying was the thing. The trying was the beginning. The trying was the first step of a thousand-year-old sorcerer who had finally, at the cost of a boy's youth and a building's grief and a blank letter that said everything by saying nothing, decided to try.

The House creaked. The creak was — Merlin listened, the way he had not listened in years, the way he had not listened since the House was young and he was young and the listening was the thing they did together, the builder and the building, the sorcerer and the stone. The creak was — not forgiveness. The creak was acknowledgment. The acknowledgment of a building that had watched its builder fail and was now watching its builder begin, and the beginning was not enough, and the not-enough was understood, and the understanding was the first step toward the enough that would come later, slowly, imperfectly, the way all real things come.

Merlin wrote. Badly. But he wrote.

The sun rose. The study was bright — brighter than it had been in years, because the window was broken and the light came in unfiltered, unmanaged, the raw, unstrategic light of a morning that did not care about kingdoms or sorcerers or the specific, devastating cost of indifference.

The light fell on the manual. The light fell on the blood. The light fell on the bread knife and the broken glass and the man on the floor who was reading letters and writing responses and trying, for the first time in a thousand years, to care.

The House watched. The House waited. The House began, slowly, to warm.

Just the study. Just the room where the builder was sitting. Just the space around the man who was, finally, reluctantly, imperfectly, beginning to do the work.

The warmth was not forgiveness. The warmth was a start.

Merlin wrote another letter. This one was slightly less terrible.

He would get better. He would have to. The manual said so. The boy had made sure of it — had designed the systems to require maintenance, had built the kingdom to need a keeper, had ensured that the absence of the keeper would force the builder to build again.

The boy had been, Merlin realized, managing him. Even in leaving. Even in dying. Even in the blank letter that said nothing. The boy had been managing the most powerful sorcerer in the world, the way he had managed everything else: quietly, invisibly, with warm hands and a steady flame and the absolute, devastating competence of a person who understood that the only way to make Merlin care was to stop caring for him.

The realization was — Merlin almost smiled. Almost. The almost was the closest he had come to smiling in years.

"Well played, Wren."

The House creaked. Agreement.

Merlin picked up the next letter. Read it. Began to write.

The morning continued. The sorcerer worked. The House watched. And the kingdom turned, slowly, uncertainly, toward a future that did not include the boy who had saved it.

For the first time in a thousand years, Merlin had a problem. The having was almost good.

He wrote another letter. This one was only slightly terrible.

Progress.

* * *

At dawn the next morning, Merlin walked the ward line.

He had not walked it in fifteen years. He had not needed to — the boy had walked it every morning, two hours in the cold, checking every anchor point. Merlin had known this. He had known it the way he knew most things that happened in his kingdom: distantly, abstractly, the way you know that your heart is beating without feeling each beat.

The ward line ran along the kingdom's border — the magical one, the seam where his power met the world's indifference. He had built it centuries ago. He had not maintained it in decades. The boy had maintained it. The boy was gone.

He walked. The morning was cold. The grass was wet. His feet, in the silk slippers he had not thought to change, were immediately soaked. He did not go back for boots. Going back for boots would have been the old Merlin — the Merlin who found reasons not to do things. He walked in wet silk slippers through wet grass and felt the cold and kept walking.

The first anchor point was a stone, carved with sigils, set into the earth at the base of an oak tree. He crouched beside it. Put his hands on it. The magic hummed — his magic, the magic he had poured into this stone centuries ago, still there, still holding, but thin. Stretched. The way a rope is stretched when it has been bearing weight for too long without being replaced.

The boy had been repairing this. Every morning. With his small, steady warmth and his precise, careful attention. The repairs were visible — not to the eye but to the hand, to the sense that Merlin had not used in years, the sense that felt the texture of magic the way a blind man feels the texture of a face. The boy's repairs were neat. Methodical. The repairs of a person who understood that maintenance was not glamorous and did it anyway.

Merlin sat beside the stone for a long time. The cold seeped through his dressing gown. The wet grass soaked through his slippers. He sat and felt the magic and thought about a boy who had walked here every morning for six years, in the cold, alone, doing the work that no one saw.

Then he put his hands on the stone and began to repair it properly. Not the boy's way — the boy's way was careful, precise, the minimum necessary force. Merlin's way was different. Merlin's way was the way of a being who had more power than he knew what to do with and had spent centuries not doing anything with it.

The repair took twenty minutes. When he was done, the anchor point was stronger than it had been in a century. Not just maintained — renewed. The magic was deep and solid and would hold without attention for years.

He stood. His knees ached. His slippers were ruined. He looked at the stone and felt something he had not felt in a very long time.

Useful. He felt useful.

The feeling was — he examined it with the careful attention of a man who has found something he thought was lost and is not sure whether to trust the finding. The feeling was small. The feeling was unfamiliar. The feeling was the beginning of something that he did not yet have a name for, because the name required more than one morning of wet slippers and a repaired ward anchor.

But the beginning was there. And beginnings, in Merlin's experience, were the hardest part.

He walked to the next anchor point. And the next. He walked the entire ward line — two hours in the cold, checking every stone, repairing what needed repairing, renewing what had been merely maintained.

By the time he returned to the House, the sun was fully up. The House opened the door — not reluctantly, not with the architectural flinch it had developed for visitors it didn't trust. The door opened the way it had opened for Wren: immediately, warmly, the way a door opens for someone who belongs.

Merlin stopped in the doorway. Looked at the rust-words on the gate.

WELCOME BACK.

He stood there for a moment. Then he went inside. Changed his slippers. Made tea — badly, because he had not made tea in decades and had forgotten the ratio. Drank it anyway. Sat at the desk.

Picked up the next letter.
