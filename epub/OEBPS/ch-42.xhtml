Chapter 43

The Morning After

He woke to the sound of an argument about a scarecrow.

The argument was happening outside, beneath his window, in the voice of a man who had clearly been having this argument for years and had not yet accepted that the scarecrow was winning. Hector's voice carried through the thin walls of the farmhouse with the penetrating clarity of a man who believed that volume was a valid agricultural technique.

"The crows are back. The crows are BACK. You had ONE job. One. Scare the crows. It's in your NAME. Scare. Crow. Two words. Both simple. Both within your capabilities, or so I THOUGHT—"

The scarecrow, presumably, said nothing. The scarecrow's silence was, Wren reflected, a masterclass in negotiation strategy. Nimue could have learned something.

He lay in the bed — the narrow bed, the straw mattress, the lavender pillow — and listened to the argument and did not get up. The not-getting-up was the strangest part. His body wanted to get up. His body had been getting up at 2 AM for six years, and the body's clock was still set to 2 AM, and the body was confused by the sunlight coming through the window and the fact that the sunlight meant it was at least 7 AM and the fact that 7 AM meant he had slept for nine hours and the fact that nine hours was more sleep than he had gotten in any single night for the past three years.

Nine hours. The number was absurd. The number was the number of a person who had nothing to do in the morning, and Wren had nothing to do in the morning, and the nothing was still so new that his mind kept testing it, the way a tongue keeps testing a missing tooth — probing the gap, expecting to find something, finding absence.

The ceiling was white. Just white. Not blue, not gold, not the color of his mood. White. The white of lime wash. The white of a ceiling that was a ceiling and nothing more.

He got up. Not because the body demanded it — because the scarecrow argument had escalated to include Tam, who had apparently taken the scarecrow's side.

"Dad, the scarecrow is doing its best. You can't fire a scarecrow. Where would it go? What would it put on its references? 'Stood in a field. Scared nothing. Available immediately.'"

"The crows are eating my CARROTS, Tam."

"The crows are eating everyone's carrots. It's what crows do. It's their whole thing. You might as well be angry at rain for being wet."

"Rain doesn't have a FACE. Rain doesn't stand in my garden with a SMUG EXPRESSION—"

"The scarecrow doesn't have a smug expression. You GAVE it that expression. You CHOSE those buttons for its eyes. You made a smug scarecrow and now you're angry at it for being smug. That's a you problem, Dad."

Wren stood at the window and watched the argument. The watching was — the watching was the first thing he did that morning that was not strategic, not calculated, not part of any system. The watching was the watching of a man who was seeing something funny and was letting the funny be funny without converting it into anything else.

Hector was gesturing at the scarecrow with a carrot. The carrot was, presumably, evidence. Tam was gesturing at the scarecrow with both hands, in the manner of a defense attorney making a closing argument. The scarecrow stood between them with the serene indifference of a defendant who knew the charges wouldn't stick.

Wren laughed. The laugh was quiet — a breath, really, an exhale that had more shape than usual — but it was real, and the real was the thing.

* * *

The kitchen was chaos.

Human chaos. The chaos of a kitchen that was too small for the number of people using it, where the stove was in the wrong place and the table was in the way and the bread was rising on a shelf that was also holding a jar of pickles and a ball of twine and something that might have been a dried herb or might have been a dead spider.

Marta was at the stove. Marta moved through the kitchen the way water moves through a riverbed — around obstacles, through gaps, with the fluid, practiced efficiency of a woman who had been cooking in this kitchen for thirty years and had memorized its geography the way Wren had memorized the geography of three hostile kingdoms.

"Sit," Marta said. She did not look up from the eggs. The eggs were sizzling. The sizzling was the sound of a kitchen that was working, and the working was the thing, and the thing was breakfast.

He sat. The table was wooden, scarred, stained with decades of meals. The table was honest in the way that the study desk was honest — both surfaces bore the marks of their purpose. But the desk's marks were ink stains and seal impressions and the specific, indelible evidence of a man who had managed a kingdom with paper. The table's marks were flour and soup and the rings left by cups of tea and the gouges left by knives cutting bread and the burn mark where someone — Tam, probably — had set a hot pan down without a cloth.

The table was a record. The record was of meals. The record was of a family eating together, day after day, year after year, the daily, unremarkable, infinitely precious act of people sitting at a table and sharing food.

Elena came in from the garden. She had been up since dawn — he could tell from the flour on her arms, the bread already cooling on the rack, the specific, competent tiredness of a woman who had done two hours of work before the rest of the house was awake. She looked at Wren at the table and her face did the thing — the almost-smile, the raw material of a smile — and she poured tea without being asked.

The tea was strong. The tea was hot. The tea was tea.

He drank it and the drinking was — present. He was present for the drinking. His mind was in the kitchen, not in the study. His mind was with the tea, not with the maps. The presence was fragile — he could feel the systems lurking at the edges, the old reflexes waiting to pull him back to Thornhold, to the correspondence, to the weight. But the systems were quiet. The systems were — resting. The way a muscle rests after a cramp. Not healed. Resting.

Tam burst through the door. "The scarecrow situation is escalating. Dad is threatening to replace it. The crows are watching. I think the crows are taking notes. Also — you're the wizard's apprentice, right? Can you do something about crows? Magically? Like, a ward? A crow ward? An anti-crow perimeter?"

"Tam," Elena said.

"What? It's a legitimate question. He maintained wards for a whole kingdom. Surely he can manage a few crows."

"He's eating breakfast."

"Crows don't respect breakfast. Crows respect nothing. Crows are the anarchists of the bird world. This is well documented."

Wren looked at Tam. Tam looked at Wren. Tam's face was — open. Completely, recklessly, beautifully open. The face of a fifteen-year-old who had not yet learned to guard his expressions, who said what he thought and thought what he felt and felt everything at full volume. The face was the opposite of every face Wren had encountered in six years of court politics — the opposite of Morgause's calculated beauty, Nimue's iron mask, Viviane's absence, Merlin's indifference. Tam's face hid nothing because Tam had nothing to hide.

"I can't ward against crows," Wren said. "Crows are too smart. They'd find a way around it."

"See?" Tam said to Elena. "Even the wizard's apprentice respects the crows. Dad needs to accept the situation. The crows have won. The carrots are a casualty of war. We should negotiate terms."

"Tam, sit down and eat."

Tam sat. Tam ate. Tam talked while eating, which Marta disapproved of and Hector ignored and Elena tolerated with the specific, practiced patience of a sister who had been tolerating her brother's talking-while-eating for fifteen years.

Hector came in from the garden. Dirt on his hands. Carrot in his pocket — the evidence, retained for future arguments. He looked at Wren with the practical, assessing gaze of a man who was evaluating a pair of hands.

"You're the one Elena wrote about," Hector said. "The thin one."

"I'm the thin one."

"You're thinner than I expected. Marta, give him more eggs."

"He has eggs."

"Give him MORE eggs. The boy looks like he hasn't eaten a proper meal in years."

"He hasn't," Elena said quietly. The quietly was — the quietly was the truth, delivered without emphasis, without drama, the way Elena delivered all truths: simply, directly, with the understanding that the truth did not need decoration.

Marta gave him more eggs. The eggs were — the eggs were good. The eggs were from the chickens in the yard, the chickens that Lord Featherbottom supervised with imperial authority, the chickens that Tam told jokes to, the chickens that were part of the farm the way the walls were part of the house — not added but integral, not managed but belonging.

He ate the eggs. He ate the bread. He drank the tea. He listened to Tam explain his theory about crow intelligence ("They have councils, I'm telling you. They meet in the oak tree. They have an agenda."). He listened to Hector rebut the theory ("They're birds, Tam. They eat things. That's the whole theory."). He listened to Marta mediate ("Both of you are wrong. The crows are fine. The carrots are fine. Eat your breakfast.").

He listened and he was here. In the kitchen. At the table. With the eggs and the bread and the tea and the family that was not his family but was — was offering him a seat at the table, which was the thing families did, which was the thing he had not had since he was seventeen, which was the thing he had not known he was missing until he was sitting in it.

The missing had been so constant, so total, so completely integrated into his existence that he had stopped feeling it. The way you stop feeling a sound that never stops — the hum of the House, the scratch of the pen, the weight. The missing had become background. The missing had become normal. And now, sitting at a table with eggs and bread and a boy who was explaining crow politics, the missing was — visible. The missing was the shape of the thing he had been living without. The shape was a table. The shape was a family. The shape was breakfast.

"The fence," Hector said, pushing back from the table. "After you've finished. I'll show you."

"All right."

"You know fences?"

"No."

"Good. A man who knows fences is a man who's stopped learning. I'll teach you. The fence is patient. The fence will wait."

The fence would wait. The breakfast was now. The now was the thing.

He finished his eggs. He finished his bread. He finished his tea. Elena refilled the cup without asking. The refilling was the same refilling she had done a thousand times in the House's kitchen — the same gesture, the same warmth, the same quiet act of care. But the context was different. The context was a farmhouse kitchen, not a sorcerer's study. The context was morning, not 2 AM. The context was a life, not a job.

The same gesture. Different context. The difference was everything.

He drank the tea. He tasted it. He was here.

Outside, the crows held council in the oak tree. The scarecrow maintained its position. Lord Featherbottom patrolled the perimeter. The fence waited.

The morning was ordinary. The ordinary was the gift.
