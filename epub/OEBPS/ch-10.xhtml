Chapter 11

The Letter Home

Dear Mother,

Everything is fine.

Wren stared at the words. The ink was still wet. The words were a lie. Everything was not fine. Everything was the opposite of fine — everything was a kingdom held together with warm hands and cold strategy and the specific, exhausting labor of being the only competent person in a building that contained the most powerful and least useful sorcerer in existence.

But the letter could not say this. The letter was going to Thornfield, to a woman who took in washing and worried about her son and deserved to believe that the worrying was unnecessary. The letter was going to a world where "fine" was a reasonable description of a life — and the reasonableness was the thing Wren was protecting.

He started again.

Dear Mother,

Everything is fine. The wizard is teaching me well. The House is warm and the food is good. I am learning new things every day.

Every sentence was a lie. The wizard was teaching him nothing — the wizard was asleep. The House was warm because Wren maintained the furnace. The food was good because Elena made it. He was learning new things every day — espionage, assassination, the art of turning three hostile kingdoms against each other through forged letters and bribed eunuchs.

He could not write any of this.

I have made friends. There is a woman who works in the kitchen — her name is Elena. She makes very good bread. There is a captain in the garrison — his name is Aldric. He is teaching me about — about practical things. Defense. Strategy. How to keep people safe.

This was closer to true. Close enough that the lying felt less like lying and more like editing — the removal of details that would cause harm, the preservation of details that would cause comfort. Editing was not lying. Editing was kindness.

The village near the House is called Thornfield — like our Thornfield, but different. There is a pub called the Broken Broom, run by a woman named Bess. She reminds me of you. Not in appearance — in the way she looks at people. Like she can see what they need before they know they need it.

This was true. Bess did remind him of his mother. The resemblance was not physical — his mother was small and soft and perpetually damp from the washing, and Bess was tall and hard and perpetually dry from the pub's heat. The resemblance was in the looking. The way both women looked at people — not through them, not past them, but into them, with the specific, penetrating attention of women who had spent their lives serving others and had developed, through the serving, an understanding of human need that was more accurate than any intelligence report.

I miss you. I miss the bread. I miss the cottage and the washing and the way the sheets smell when they come off the line in summer.

This was true. He missed all of it. The missing was a physical thing — a weight in his chest, a tightness in his throat, the ache of a person who is far from home and cannot go back.

He missed the bread. Not the House's bread — his mother's bread. The bread he had kneaded at twelve, the bread that had risen from his warm hands, the bread that was the first sign of the magic that had brought him here. He missed the bread because the bread was the last thing he had made with his hands that was simple and good and nourishing and did not require anyone to die.

I am eating well. Please don't worry.

The first sentence was a lie. The second was impossible. His mother would worry. His mother had been worrying since the day he was born — worrying about the washing and the flour and the rent and the father who had walked to the market and chosen a different direction. Worrying was his mother's primary occupation, more constant than the washing, more reliable than the bread. Telling her not to worry was like telling the rain not to fall — technically possible, practically absurd, but the telling said I know you worry and the knowing was the thing that mattered.

I will write again soon. I love you.

Your son,

Wren

He sealed the letter. The seal was plain — no magic, no Merlin's stamp, just wax and a press. The plainness was deliberate. A magical seal would raise questions. A plain seal said: your son is a boy who writes letters, not a shadow ruler who commands assassinations.

He set the letter on the desk. Looked at it. The letter was small — a single page, folded twice, sealed with plain wax. The letter was the smallest thing on the desk. The desk was covered with maps and reports and intelligence files and trade agreements and the accumulated weight of a kingdom's survival. The letter was the smallest thing and the most important thing and the most dishonest thing, and the dishonesty was love, and love was the only lie Wren told that he didn't hate himself for.

* * *

Elena found him writing the letter.

Not the letter to his mother — a different letter, later, at the desk, in the study. She had brought the evening meal — soup, bread, the honey cake the kitchen was still producing with the stubborn optimism of a stove that believed sweetness could solve structural problems. She set the tray on the corner of the desk, in the space Wren kept clear for trays, which was the only clear space on the desk, which said something about his priorities that he didn't want to examine.

"Writing home?" she asked. She had seen the sealed letter — the plain seal, the Thornfield address.

"Yes."

"What did you tell her?"

"That everything is fine."

Elena looked at him. The look was — not accusatory. Understanding. The understanding of a person who also wrote letters home, who also edited the truth, who also sent comfort instead of accuracy because comfort was what mothers needed and accuracy was what mothers feared.

"I tell my mother the same thing," Elena said. "Everything is fine. The House is nice. The work is good. I don't tell her about the plumbing incidents or the time the kitchen tried to make a soufflé and the soufflé achieved sentience and had to be negotiated with. I don't tell her about—" She paused. The pause was careful. "I don't tell her about the things that would make her worry."

"What things?"

"The war. The soldiers. The — the feeling that something is happening that I don't understand and can't control and the not-understanding is the thing that frightens me more than the thing itself." She sat on the edge of the desk. The sitting was casual — the casualness of a person who has become comfortable in a space, who has claimed a corner of someone else's world through the simple act of repeated presence. "I don't tell her about you."

"About me?"

"About the way you look. The way you've changed. The grey — it's starting, Wren. At your temples. You're eighteen. Eighteen-year-olds don't have grey hair. Eighteen-year-olds don't have lines around their eyes. Eighteen-year-olds don't sit at desks at 3 AM writing letters to their mothers that say 'everything is fine' when everything is not fine and the not-fine is visible on their face."

He said nothing. The response would require acknowledging what Elena was saying, and acknowledging would require stopping, and stopping was the thing he could not do.

"I don't tell my mother about you," Elena said again. Quieter. "Because if I told her, she would come here. She would come here and she would look at you and she would see what I see and she would say the thing I can't say because saying it would mean — it would mean I care, and caring about someone who is burning themselves alive is — it's—"

She stopped. The stopping was not a choice — it was a wall. The wall that exists between what a person feels and what a person can say, built from the knowledge that saying the thing will not change the thing.

"Elena," Wren said.

"Eat your soup," she said. The words were rough. The roughness was not anger — it was the texture of a voice that has been holding something back and the holding has made the voice raw. "Eat your soup and write your letters and do your — your work. Your important work. Your kingdom work. I'll bring breakfast in the morning. I'll bring it every morning. I'll keep bringing it until you eat it or until—"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. The "until" hung in the air between them — the until that meant until you break or until you leave or until the thing I can see happening finishes happening and the person you were is gone and the person you are is someone I don't recognize.

She left. The door closed. The study was quiet.

Wren looked at the tray. The soup. The bread. The honey cake. The meal that Elena brought every evening and would continue bringing every evening, because showing up was the only form of love available to her and she had committed to the showing up with everything she had.

He ate the soup. He tasted it. The tasting was deliberate — a conscious effort, a forcing of attention away from the maps and the letters and the weight, toward the bowl and the spoon and the specific, physical reality of carrots and potatoes and warmth.

The soup tasted like Elena's mother's soup. Not identical — different kitchen, different hands, different ingredients. But the same principle. The same warmth. The same specific, unquantifiable ingredient that was not a spice or a technique but a person, a person who made soup because making soup was the thing they could do, and the doing was love, and love was the thing that tasted like carrots and potatoes and the end of a long day.

He finished the soup. Set down the spoon. Picked up the sealed letter — the letter to his mother, the letter that said everything was fine.

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he put it in the outgoing post. And picked up the next letter. And the work continued.

And the soup was warm in his stomach. And the lie was sealed in its envelope. And the warmth held. And somewhere in Thornfield, a woman was hanging laundry and waiting for a letter from her son, and the letter would arrive on a Tuesday, and the letter would say everything was fine, and the woman would read it and believe it because believing was easier than knowing, and the believing was the gift her son could give her, and the gift was a lie, and the lie was love.

And love, in Wren's world, was always a lie. Because the truth would break the people he loved, and breaking them was the one thing he would not do.

The hands were warm. The lie was sealed. The warmth held.

The lie was the most reliable magic Wren knew.

* * *

Three weeks later, a letter arrived from Thornfield.

It was in the morning post, sorted by the House into the mundane pile — no magic, no seal, plain wax, the handwriting of a woman who had learned to write from a village schoolmaster and had never had occasion to practice. Wren almost missed it. He almost filed it without opening it, the way he filed everything that was not urgent, not important, not a threat.

He opened it because of the handwriting. Because the handwriting was his mother's and he had not seen it in eighteen months and the seeing of it did something to his chest that the systems could not process and therefore could not prevent.

Dear Wren,

Thank you for your letters. They are very kind.

I know you are lying to me. I have known since the second letter. A mother knows. The first letter I believed because I wanted to. The second letter I believed because I was afraid not to. The third letter I read and I thought: my son is protecting me. My son has decided that I cannot bear the truth. My son is carrying something very heavy and is pretending it is light so that I will not worry.

I worry anyway. That is what mothers do. The lying does not stop the worrying. It only means I worry alone, without being able to help, which is worse.

I am not writing to ask you to stop lying. I understand why you lie. I lied to you too, when you were small — about your father, about the money, about whether we would be all right. We were not always all right. You knew. Children always know. But the lying was love, and I understood that, and I let you believe I believed it, because that was the kindness I could offer.

I am writing to tell you that I see you. Not the letters — you. The boy who kneaded bread at twelve and walked fourteen miles with a canvas bag and believed the world would be kind to him. I see what the world has done with that boy. I see it in the spaces between your words, in the things you do not say, in the careful, practiced cheerfulness of a person who has learned to perform fine.

You are not fine. I know this. I am not asking you to be fine. I am asking you to remember that the bread is still here. The cottage is still here. The washing line is still here. And I am still here, and I am not made of glass, and you do not have to protect me from the truth of your life.

Come home when you can. Not to lie to me in person — to sit at the table and eat bread and be my son, who is allowed to be tired and allowed to be afraid and allowed to have grey hair at twenty-three if that is what the world has given him.

I love you. I have always known you were carrying something. I have always wished I could carry it with you.

Your mother

Wren read the letter once. Then he set it down on the desk, very carefully, the way you set down something that has just broken something inside you and you are not sure yet how badly.

He sat for a long time. The maps stared from the walls. The letters waited in their stacks. The kingdom continued its patient, relentless need.

He had been protecting her. He had been so certain she needed protecting. He had built the lie with such care — the careful cheerfulness, the edited truth, the performance of fine — and she had seen through it from the second letter and had said nothing, had let him believe the lie was working, had offered him the kindness of being believed.

She had been protecting him. From the knowledge that the lie had failed. From the weight of knowing she knew.

They had been protecting each other. Across fourteen miles and eighteen months of letters, two people who loved each other had been carefully, tenderly, exhaustingly lying to each other in order to spare each other the truth, and the truth was that they both already knew the truth, and the knowing had been there all along, underneath the lies, patient and warm and waiting.

He picked up a pen. Began to write.

Not the careful, edited letter. The other one. The one he had never written. The one that said: I am not fine. The kingdom is not fine. The wizard is asleep and the witches are circling and I have ordered a man's death and I have kissed a witch-queen and I have grey hair at twenty-three and I am afraid, not of the witches, but of becoming something I don't recognize, and the only thing that has kept me human is a woman in the kitchen who brings me soup and a house that adjusts its temperature when I am sad and the memory of bread I made at twelve with warm hands that I am slowly forgetting how to use for anything except holding a pen.

He wrote for an hour. He did not edit. He did not protect. He wrote the truth, all of it, the way you lance a wound — not gently, not carefully, but completely, because incomplete lancing is worse than no lancing at all.

He sealed the letter. Plain wax. No magic. The handwriting of a boy who had learned to write from the same village schoolmaster.

He put it in the outgoing post. Next to the lie he had already sent.

Then he picked up the next letter. And the work continued.

But something had shifted. Something small. He had told the truth to the one person who already knew, and the telling had not broken anything.

The warmth held. The lie was in the post. The truth was also in the post.

For the first time in eighteen months, both were true at once.
