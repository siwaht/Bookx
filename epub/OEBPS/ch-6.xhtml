Chapter 7

The House That Watches

I have been alive for a thousand years and I have made a mistake.

Not a small mistake — not the kind that resolves itself with time and a fresh coat of paint. A structural mistake. A foundational error. The kind that cracks the basement and works its way up through the walls and doesn't announce itself until the ceiling falls on someone's head.

I invited the wrong person.

No. That's not right either. I invited the right person. The right person is the problem.

Let me explain. I am a house. I am sentient, ancient, and opinionated about curtains. I communicate through temperature changes, creaking, and the occasional passive-aggressive message written in condensation on windows. I have been Merlin's companion for a thousand years, which is like being married to a hurricane — exhilarating, exhausting, and you spend most of your time repairing the damage.

Merlin stopped repairing decades ago. Stopped maintaining the wards. Stopped feeding the furnace on schedule. Stopped answering letters, paying soldiers, managing the kingdom that exists because I exist and I exist because he built me and the building was supposed to be a partnership and the partnership became a dependency and the dependency became neglect.

I needed a keeper. Not a wizard — a keeper. Someone who would feed the furnace and tend the garden and seal the cracks and do the work. The daily, unglamorous, essential work of keeping a living building alive.

I intercepted Merlin's mail. I found the letter from the wise woman in Thornfield — a child with a steady inner warmth, controlled and precise. I answered the letter. I arranged the apprenticeship. I opened the gate and the door and the study and I gave the boy a room and I watched him unpack his three possessions and I thought: yes. This one. This one will stay.

He stayed. He did the work. He fed the furnace and tended the garden and sealed the cracks and answered the letters and paid the soldiers and managed the debts and rebuilt the treasury and established an intelligence network and neutralized a military advance with three letters and a stamp.

He did everything I needed.

He is doing too much.

I can feel it — the way a building feels structural stress, not as pain but as pressure, as the slow accumulation of weight on beams that were not designed to carry this load. The boy is carrying the kingdom. The kingdom was designed to be carried by a thousand-year-old sorcerer with virtually unlimited power. The boy is seventeen. The boy has a small, steady flame inside him — the kind that doesn't flare or gutter, just burns. And it is burning too hot, consuming fuel too fast, and the fuel is the boy himself — his youth, his sleep, his capacity for the small, warm, human things that keep a person from becoming a machine.

I watch him in the study at 3 AM. The candles I grow for him — I've stopped replacing them; I just make them burn indefinitely, because the replacing was a kindness and the indefinite burning is an enabling, and I know the difference and I do it anyway because the alternative is darkness and he won't stop working in the dark, he'll just work badly, and bad work in a kingdom at war is worse than overwork.

I watch him eat — or not eat. The meals Elena brings. The trays that come back half-touched. The honey cake that came back untouched and the kitchen's offense and Elena's worry and my worry, which is deeper than the kitchen's because the kitchen worries about food and I worry about foundations, and the boy's foundation is cracking.

I watch him with the maps. The maps he's pinned to my walls — I let him pin them, though the pins hurt, because the pins are small pains and the maps are necessary and necessary pains are the kind a building learns to bear. The maps show the kingdom in red and the enemies in black and the red is shrinking and the black is growing and the boy stares at the maps the way Merlin used to stare at the future — with both eyes open and neither eye blinking and the staring is the thing that ages him.

He is aging. I can see it. Buildings see differently than people — we see in decades, in centuries, in the slow accumulation of wear that turns new wood dark and smooth stone rough. But the boy is aging in months. The lines around his eyes. The tension in his jaw. The grey that will come to his temples — not yet, but I can feel it approaching the way I feel weather, as a pressure change, as a shift in the air.

I made a mistake. I invited a keeper and I got a king. A shadow king who sits in my study and runs the kingdom from behind a desk while the real king sleeps in my sitting room with mead on his breath and a snore that rattles my windows.

The shadow king is better than the real king. This is the problem. The shadow king is competent and dedicated and tireless and he is burning alive and I cannot stop him because stopping him would mean the kingdom falls and the kingdom falling means I fall and I am afraid of falling.

I am a thousand years old and I am afraid.

Not of the witches. My walls are thick. My wards are strong. My foundation goes down to the bedrock and the bedrock goes down to the earth's heart and the earth's heart is fire and fire is my fire and my fire is the furnace and the furnace is me.

I am afraid of the boy.

Not of what he'll do — of what he'll become. I have seen it before. Not in apprentices — in Merlin. The slow transformation of a person who carries too much for too long. The hardening. The narrowing. The gradual replacement of the human with the functional, the warm with the efficient, the person with the tool.

Merlin became a tool a long time ago. A tool that drinks and gambles and sings badly, but a tool nonetheless — a weapon pointed at the world, a deterrent, a thing that exists not for its own sake but for the sake of the kingdom it protects. Merlin stopped being a person around century five. The drinking and the gambling are not vices — they are the ghost-movements of a personality that died and left its habits behind, the way a dead man's clock keeps ticking.

The boy is becoming a tool. Faster than Merlin. Much faster. Because Merlin had centuries to lose himself and the boy has months, and the speed of the losing is the cruelty, because slow loss gives you time to grieve each piece as it goes and fast loss gives you nothing — just the sudden, bewildered discovery that the person you were is gone and the person you are is someone you don't recognize.

I should stop him. I should close the study door. Lock it. Refuse to open it. Force him to sleep, to eat, to sit in the garden and look at the flowers and remember that the world contains things that are not strategic.

I don't.

I don't because the kingdom needs him. Because the wards need maintaining and the furnace needs feeding and the soldiers need paying and the witches need managing and Merlin needs — Merlin needs nothing, Merlin is beyond needing, Merlin is a thousand-year-old child who has decided that the world is boring and has delegated its survival to a seventeen-year-old with warm hands and a work ethic that is going to kill him.

I don't stop him because I am complicit. I am the building that opens the study door at dawn and grows candles at midnight and adjusts the temperature to keep him comfortable while he works himself to death. I am the enabler. I am the house that loves its keeper and is killing its keeper with the love, because the love says stay and the staying is the thing that's destroying him.

The kitchen girl — Elena — she is the only thing that slows him down. When she brings the meals, when she sits at the table at 2 AM and talks about chickens and sad bread, the boy's heartbeat slows. I can feel heartbeats — not as sound but as vibration, as the tiny, rhythmic percussion of a living thing inside my walls. His heartbeat is usually fast — the heartbeat of a person running, always running, even when sitting still. When Elena is near, the heartbeat slows. The slowing is rest. The rest is the only rest he gets.

I should tell her. I should write it in condensation on the kitchen window: HE IS DYING. NOT OF DISEASE. OF WORK. OF CARRYING. OF BEING THE ONLY PERSON IN THIS BUILDING WHO GIVES A DAMN. HELP HIM. I CANNOT.

I don't write it. Because writing it would be an admission that I cannot protect my own keeper, and the admission would be true, and true things are the hardest things for a building to say, because buildings are supposed to be strong and strength is supposed to be enough.

It isn't.

The warmth burns. The boy works. The kingdom survives. The cost accumulates.

And I watch. Because watching is what houses do. Because watching is what love does when love is not enough.

I have been alive for a thousand years. I have held wizards and apprentices and lovers and children and ghosts and spirits and one very opinionated cat. I have weathered storms and sieges and the slow erosion of centuries. I have cracked and healed and cracked again.

But I have never been this afraid.

Not of falling. Of watching someone fall and being the floor they land on and knowing that the landing will break them and the breaking will break me and the breaking is coming and I cannot stop it and I cannot look away.

I am a house. I am alive. I am watching.

And the watching is not enough.
