Chapter 6

The Man Who Felt Nothing

Marcus remembered love.

Not the feeling — the architecture. The way it had structured his days: waking and thinking of her name, eating and wondering if she'd eaten, sleeping and reaching for a body that was there or wasn't. The architecture was intact. The feeling that had lived inside it was gone, removed with surgical precision by the Witch of Despair's alchemists, leaving the framework standing like a house with no one home.

He remembered this the way a blind man remembers color — with perfect intellectual clarity and absolute experiential absence. He could describe love in terms that would make a poet weep. He could not feel it. The description and the feeling occupied different countries, and the border between them had been sealed.

He was standing in the war room of Greymarch's northern fortress, reviewing supply manifests, when the letter arrived.

The letter was unremarkable. Brown paper. No seal. No scent. The handwriting was precise — not beautiful, not ugly, just efficient. The efficiency was what caught his attention. In Nimue's court, everything was either brutal or bureaucratic. This letter was neither. It was something else. Something that operated in the space between.

To the Administrator of the Northern Garrison,

You manage Nimue's supply lines. You do this well. You do this without passion, without ambition, without the messy human complications that make other administrators unreliable. This makes you valuable. It also makes you predictable.

Predictable men are useful. But useful men who know they are predictable are more useful still, because they can choose to be unpredictable at precisely the right moment.

I can offer you something Nimue cannot: a reason to choose.

If you are interested in having reasons again, leave a candle in the north window of the fortress on the third night after receiving this letter. If the candle is there, we will talk. If it is not, this letter never existed.

— A friend in Thornhold

Marcus read the letter with the same clinical attention he gave to supply manifests. He noted the paper (common, untraceable), the ink (standard iron gall, available in any market), the handwriting (controlled, young — the writer was not old), and the content (an appeal to something he did not have: desire).

He should report the letter to Nimue. This was protocol. Unauthorized correspondence from enemy territories was to be reported immediately, the sender identified, the network traced and destroyed. Marcus had overseen the destruction of six such networks in his tenure. The process was efficient. The process was correct.

He did not report the letter.

He did not report it because the letter had done something that nothing had done in the seven years since his emotions were removed: it had made him curious. Not emotionally curious — intellectually curious. Marcus calculated that the letter represented an anomaly, and anomalies, in a system as rigid as Nimue's military machine, were worth investigating.

On the third night, he placed a candle in the north window.

* * *

The meeting happened in a border tavern — neutral ground, the kind of place where people from both sides of a conflict drank the same bad wine and pretended not to recognize each other. The tavern was called the Blind Pig, which Marcus noted was either a commentary on the quality of the food or a statement about the establishment's policy regarding the identities of its patrons. Both interpretations were accurate.

The boy was already there.

Marcus had expected — what? A spymaster. A diplomat. A grey-haired operative with decades of experience and the specific, weathered competence of someone who had been playing this game since before Marcus was born.

The boy at the corner table was seventeen. Maybe eighteen. Thin. Dark-haired. Hands wrapped around a cup of tea — not wine, tea, which was either a statement of sobriety or a statement of youth, and Marcus calculated it was both.

The boy's eyes were the thing. Not the color — the quality. The quality of eyes that were seeing everything in the room simultaneously: the exits, the other patrons, the bartender's hands, the weight of the shadows in the corners. The eyes of a person who had learned to see the way a hunted animal sees — in all directions at once, with the specific, exhausting vigilance of someone who cannot afford to be surprised.

"You're younger than I expected," Marcus said, sitting down.

"Everyone says that." The boy — Wren, he would learn — sipped his tea. "You're exactly what I expected. Nimue's administrators are consistent. Grey skin. Grey eyes. Precise movements. You sit down the way a machine sits down — no wasted motion, no adjustment, no comfort-seeking. You don't blink enough. You don't breathe at the right rhythm. Something is missing and my body knows it before my mind does."

Marcus noted the observation. It was accurate. It was also delivered without revulsion or pity — the two responses he most commonly encountered from people who noticed what he was. This boy noticed and simply... catalogued. The way Marcus himself catalogued. The recognition was — not pleasant, because nothing was pleasant — but notable.

"You said you could offer me reasons," Marcus said.

"I said I could offer you a reason to choose. There's a difference. Reasons are plural and abstract. A reason is singular and specific."

"What reason?"

"Restoration."

The word landed in the space between them. Marcus processed it the way he processed everything — from every angle, testing its weight, assessing its probability.

"The eunuch process is irreversible," he said. "Nimue's alchemists have confirmed this. The emotions are not suppressed — they are removed. You cannot restore what no longer exists."

"Nimue's alchemists have a vested interest in the process being irreversible. If it's reversible, their product is temporary. Temporary products are less valuable than permanent ones. Their confirmation is not science — it's marketing."

Marcus considered this. The logic was sound. The logic being sound did not make the claim true, but it made the claim worth investigating, and investigation was the only form of hope available to a man who could not feel hope.

"You're claiming Merlin can reverse the process."

"I'm claiming Merlin's magic operates on a different principle than Nimue's alchemy. Nimue's alchemists remove emotions through chemical extraction. Merlin's magic works with the fabric of reality itself. What chemistry removes, reality might restore — if the cost is paid."

"What cost?"

"I don't know yet. That's honest. I don't know if it's possible and I don't know what it would cost. What I know is that Merlin is the most powerful sorcerer alive, and that 'impossible' is a word that applies to everyone except him."

Marcus studied the boy. The study was thorough — posture, micro-expressions, vocal patterns, the specific tells that indicate deception. He found none. This meant either that the boy was telling the truth or that the boy was a better liar than anyone Marcus had ever encountered.

Both possibilities were interesting.

"What do you want from me?" Marcus asked.

"Information. Access. The ability to influence Nimue's military operations from the inside. Specifically: I want her supply lines disrupted. Not destroyed — disrupted. Enough to slow her campaigns, not enough to trigger an investigation. I want her battle plans leaked — selectively, strategically, the plans that matter and not the ones that don't. I want her generals undermined — not killed, not yet, just... made less effective. Promotions delayed. Resources misallocated. The slow, invisible erosion of a military machine from the inside."

"You want me to betray my queen."

"I want you to serve your own interests. Your queen took your emotions. Your queen's alchemists told you the taking was permanent. I'm offering the possibility — not the certainty, the possibility — that they were wrong. The price of that possibility is cooperation."

"And if the possibility turns out to be false? If Merlin cannot restore what was taken?"

Wren met his eyes. The meeting was steady — no flinch, no waver, no guilt. The steadiness was either courage or sociopathy, and Marcus calculated that it was both, in proportions that shifted depending on the day.

"Then you'll have betrayed your queen for nothing," Wren said. "And I'll have lied to you. And we'll both have to live with that."

"I cannot live with anything. That is the point."

"Then you have nothing to lose."

The logic was — Marcus searched for the word. The logic was correct. A man who cannot feel has nothing to lose. A man with nothing to lose is the most dangerous kind of ally and the most reliable kind of traitor.

"I'll need proof," Marcus said. "Not of Merlin's ability — of your authority. You speak for Thornhold. But you are not Merlin. You are a boy. Boys do not command kingdoms."

"This one does."

"Prove it."

Wren reached into his coat. Produced a document — Merlin's seal, Merlin's signature, authorizing the bearer to act in the sorcerer's name in all matters of state. The document was genuine. Marcus could tell because the seal radiated a faint magical signature — the ozone-and-old-libraries scent of Merlin's power, embedded in the wax.

"Merlin signed this?" Marcus asked.

"Merlin signed a stack of blank documents three months ago because I told him they were requisition forms for kitchen supplies. He didn't read them. He doesn't read anything. He signed them because signing them was faster than arguing, and arguing would have required him to care, and caring is the one thing Merlin refuses to do."

Marcus processed this. The processing produced something that, in a man with emotions, might have been amusement. In Marcus, it was simply the recognition of an elegant solution to a bureaucratic problem.

"You forged authorization from the most powerful sorcerer in the world by telling him it was a grocery list."

"I didn't forge anything. He signed them. The fact that he didn't know what he was signing is his problem, not mine."

Marcus stood. The standing was precise — no wasted motion, no adjustment.

"I will consider your proposal," he said. "Consideration is the only response I can offer, because response requires motivation and motivation requires feeling and feeling is the thing I do not have. But consideration is a process, and processes produce outcomes, and outcomes are what you're interested in."

"How long?"

"Seven days. I will send word through the same channel. If I agree, I will require specific guarantees — written, sealed, binding. If I decline, this conversation never happened."

"Agreed."

Marcus turned to leave. At the door, he stopped. Not because he felt the impulse to stop — impulses required feelings. He stopped because a thought had completed its processing and the output required delivery.

"The woman," he said. "The one I remembered loving. Her name was Sera. She had red hair and she laughed at things that weren't funny and she burned every meal she cooked and I loved her with a completeness that I can describe but not access. She is dead now. She died of fever, three years after my conversion. I was told. I processed the information. I did not grieve. I could not grieve. The inability to grieve is — I am told — worse than the grief itself. I cannot confirm this because I cannot feel 'worse.' I can only know, intellectually, that something is missing, and that the missing thing had a name, and the name was Sera."

He looked at Wren. The look was empty — not hostile, not warm, not anything. The emptiness was the point.

"If you are lying to me about restoration," Marcus said, "I will not be angry. I cannot be angry. But I will be thorough. And thoroughness, in a man without mercy, is worse than anger."

He left. The tavern door closed behind him. The Blind Pig resumed its business of serving bad wine to people who pretended not to know each other.

Wren sat at the corner table. His tea was cold. His hands were warm. The warmth felt — for the first time since he'd arrived at the House — like a lie. Because the warmth was the thing he was selling to Marcus: the promise of feeling, the possibility of restoration, the hope of a man who could not hope.

He had offered a possibility because a possibility was all he had, and a possibility was enough to turn a man — even a man who felt nothing — into an asset.

He had just recruited his first spy with a promise he might not be able to keep.

The pilot light burned. The fuel was something new — not youth, not energy, not sleep. The fuel was integrity. The small, invisible currency of a person who says what they mean and means what they say. He had spent some of it tonight. Not much. Just a piece. Just the piece that separates "I don't know if this is possible" from "this might be possible," which is the difference between honesty and salesmanship, which is the difference between a good person and a useful one.

He finished his cold tea. Left money on the table. Walked out into the night.

The stars were bright. The road was dark. Somewhere to the north, Marcus was walking back to Greymarch's fortress with a candle's worth of maybe in his chest where feelings used to live.

Somewhere to the east, Morgause was sitting in her golden court, thinking about a boy who had looked at her without flinching and wondering what it would take to make him flinch.

Somewhere to the west, Viviane was doing nothing, which was the most dangerous thing anyone was doing. She was doing nothing the way a glacier does nothing — slowly, invisibly, reshaping everything around her through the sheer weight of her indifference.

And somewhere to the south, on a farm two days' ride from the kingdom, Elena's brother Tam was telling a joke to chickens who had learned to flinch, and Elena's mother was arguing with the weather, and Elena's father was mending a fence that didn't need mending because mending things was how he showed love, and the simple world continued to exist, patient and warm and waiting for someone to come home to it.

Wren walked south. Toward Thornhold. Toward the study. Toward the maps and the letters and the weight.

He did not walk toward the farm.

Not yet.
